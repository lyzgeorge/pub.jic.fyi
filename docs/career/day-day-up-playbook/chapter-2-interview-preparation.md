# Chapter 2 — Interview Preparation

> **Source:** Condensed from *The Day Day Up Career Playbook* (concise edition), based on Jonathan Lee's career-coaching videos.

Preparation should produce a clear case for your fit and a small bank of evidence you can adapt. It should not produce a script for every possible question.



![Three questions behind an interview evaluation](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-2/01-framework-three-interview-questions.png)
## 2.1 Prepare for the real evaluation

Across the corpus, interviewers ask variations of three questions:

| Evaluation | Prepare |
|---|---|
| Can you solve our problems? | Relevant achievements and the reasoning behind them. |
| Do you want this particular role? | A specific connection between its work and your strengths or direction. |
| What risks come with hiring you? | A concise explanation of gaps, transitions, and how you work with others. |

Treat the interview as a two-way working conversation. Listen to what is being asked, clarify uncertainty, and adjust your answer. Calm curiosity is more useful than trying to sound flawless.

**Before the meeting:** Know the value you offer, the manager’s likely priority, and the most obvious concern about your fit. Prepare evidence for all three.

## 2.2 Turn the job description into a preparation brief

Create one page with:

1. **The work:** What the company does and how this role contributes.
2. **The priorities:** Three to five important competencies, with a plain-language explanation of why each matters.
3. **Your evidence:** One relevant achievement for each priority.
4. **Your gap:** The weakest match and a truthful bridge to related experience.
5. **Your questions:** What you still need to learn to judge the role.

AI can help translate jargon and suggest priorities. Treat its interpretation as a hypothesis to confirm, not inside information about the employer.

**Useful prompt:**

> From this job description, identify the main responsibilities and likely business problems. Suggest the five most important competencies and explain why each might matter. Separate explicit requirements from your inferences.

## 2.3 Research people and ask for context

Read the company’s product or service information, stated values, and relevant recent developments. Look at the interviewer’s role and background. Find one genuine point of interest; avoid forced personal familiarity.

Use the recruiter to improve your preparation:

> Thanks for arranging the next round. Is there any feedback from the previous conversation I should keep in mind? What will this round focus on, and are there skills or examples you recommend I prepare?

When appropriate, ask someone who previously did the job about expectations and the manager’s working style:

> I’m interviewing for [role] with [team]. If you’re open to sharing, what helped someone succeed in that role? No pressure if you’d rather not discuss it.

Record what you learn and change your preparation accordingly. A request for insight is useful only if the answer affects your choices.



![Story bank matrix matching examples to competencies](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-2/02-framework-story-bank-matrix.png)
## 2.4 Build a small story bank

Start with three strong achievements. Add another only when an important competency remains uncovered.

**Selection criteria:**

- **Challenge:** The problem involved ambiguity, constraints, resistance, or meaningful stakes.
- **Validation:** Someone used, recognized, or relied on the work.
- **Impact:** Your contribution changed a business or team outcome.

For each story, record the situation, your responsibility, key decisions, actions, result, and lesson. Distinguish what you did from what the team did. Keep enough detail to answer follow-ups, but prepare a shorter spoken version.

Build a matrix: stories as rows, target competencies as columns. Mark only skills you can demonstrate with specific actions. An empty column tells you where to find another example or acknowledge a gap.

**Make stories adaptable:** The same project can show analysis, collaboration, or ownership. Change which decisions you emphasize, not the facts. Use Chapter 3 to shape the answer.

## 2.5 Match preparation to the time available

| Time available | Priority |
|---|---|
| About one hour | Understand the company and role; select your strongest stories; prepare the introduction, motivation answer, and questions. Say the key answers aloud. |
| About two hours | Use the coach’s four-part sequence: 30 minutes understanding the role, 30 selecting and adapting evidence, 30 preparing core answers, 30 speaking without the script. |
| More time | Add follow-up questions, a realistic mock, and a review of weak evidence or unclear delivery. |

Do not spend the final hour collecting more question lists. Resolve the largest gap in your case for the role.

## 2.6 Improve from recordings and real interviews

Review a mock recording in three passes:

- **Audio:** Pace, filler words, clarity, and whether the answer sounds natural.
- **Video:** Eye contact, visible tension, and distracting movement.
- **Transcript:** Whether you answered the question, made your contribution clear, and explained the result.

Use a mock by default. Record or share a real interview only with the necessary consent and permission.

After each interview, note what was asked, what landed, what caused confusion, and one change for the next conversation. Keep the change specific: “State my recommendation before the background” is actionable; “Be more confident” is not.

**Preparation is complete when:** You can explain the role’s needs, deliver a relevant introduction, adapt your stories without inventing details, discuss your main gap honestly, and ask questions that help both sides evaluate fit.
