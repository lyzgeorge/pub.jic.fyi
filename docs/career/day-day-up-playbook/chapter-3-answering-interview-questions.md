# Chapter 3 — Answering Interview Questions

> **Source:** Condensed from *The Day Day Up Career Playbook* (concise edition), based on Jonathan Lee's career-coaching videos.

Choose the right answer form, make your contribution clear, and connect your evidence to the role. Use scripts as starting points, not lines to recite.

## 3.1 Match the answer to the question

| Question | Answer form |
|---|---|
| “Tell me about a time…” | A specific past example with your actions and result. |
| “How would you…” | Your approach, assumptions, and reasoning; add brief evidence if helpful. |
| “Tell me about yourself” | A concise summary of relevant strengths, proof, and fit. |
| “Why this role?” | What the work needs, what you offer, and why you want that combination. |

If the intended focus is unclear, ask:

> Before I answer, are you most interested in how I analyzed the problem or how I aligned the stakeholders?

Then answer that focus. Avoid delivering a full prepared story simply because one keyword sounds familiar.

## 3.2 Give a story stakes, decisions, and a result

STAR remains a useful checklist for completeness. The coach’s **HEALER** framework adds emphasis on why the work was difficult and why the result matters:

| Element | Include |
|---|---|
| Hook | A brief opening that identifies the achievement. |
| Emergency | The problem, stakes, and constraints. |
| Approach | The principle or reasoning guiding your response. |
| Launch | The specific actions you took. |
| Effect | The measured result and business consequence. |
| Reflection | What you learned and how it applies to this role. |

Keep background short. Spend most of the answer on the decisions and actions that prove the competency being tested. Use a genuine human consequence when it clarifies the impact; do not manufacture drama or quotes.

**Practical example, condensed from the corpus:**

> Our ride-hailing business was expanding into the consumer market, but driver acceptance was below 50%. I treated the supply problem as an experiment: surveyed 200 drivers, tested bonus messages, and helped launch an in-app bonus tracker. Completed rides increased by 14%, contributing over a million dollars in revenue and stabilizing supply for the expansion.

This works because the problem, method, and business outcome connect. In a collaboration interview, the same project should explain how people reached agreement instead of repeating the analytical version.

## 3.3 Make your contribution and judgment visible

Use **“I” for your decisions and work; “we” for shared delivery**. Neither claim the whole team’s achievement nor hide your contribution inside “we.”

Start with the outcome, then explain how you reached it:

> I led [specific contribution] to address [problem]. We achieved [result]. The hardest decision was [trade-off], and I chose [approach] because [reason].

Translate technical work into consequences the interviewer understands: time saved, fewer errors, capacity gained, a decision enabled, or a commercial outcome. Only attribute outcomes you can support.

For situational questions, state a principle and show how you apply it:

> I start by understanding what the stakeholder is trying to protect. In one campaign, legal’s concern was our wording rather than the promotion itself. We changed the wording to address the risk and obtained approval.

A principle is useful when it explains a choice. “I communicate clearly and collaborate well” is only a claim.

## 3.4 Introduce yourself with strength, proof, and fit

Use **HAS: Hook → Achievement → So what**. Aim for roughly 45–60 seconds as a starting point, then adapt to the conversation.

> I’m a [role] with [experience], and my strongest contribution is [relevant strength]. Most recently, I [specific action and outcome]. That connects to this role because [priority you understand they have].

Select one clear thread through your career rather than reciting every employer. If the role covers several areas, ask which would be most useful to focus on.

**For “something not on your resume”:** Add a relevant working style or motivation, supported by an example. Do not simply repeat another resume bullet.

## 3.5 Answer motivation questions specifically

| Question | Structure | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Why here? | What you understand about their work → why it fits your strengths → what you want to contribute. | Praise that could apply to any employer. |
| Why are you leaving? | What you valued → what you now seek → why this role offers it. | Complaints or an invented reason. |
| Where do you see yourself? | Near-term contribution → skills or scope you want to develop → connection to this opportunity. | A title-only ambition detached from the work. |

**Example, adapted:**

> I’ve learned a lot in my current role, especially about [area]. I’m now looking for more opportunity to solve [type of problem]. From our discussion, your team needs [specific contribution], which fits the work I do best.

If you were laid off or dismissed, state the facts briefly. Where relevant, acknowledge your part, explain what changed, and return to evidence of your fit. Do not turn the answer into a defense of your entire career.

## 3.6 Handle weaknesses, gaps, and missing experience

**Weakness:** Name a real limitation, its effect, the action you are taking, and evidence of progress. Avoid a disguised strength.

> Stakeholder communication is an area I’m improving. I started regular updates to make risks visible earlier, and [specific evidence of improvement].

**Missing tool or experience:** Be direct, identify the underlying capability, and support the transfer with evidence.

> I haven’t used that tool in production. I have solved a similar problem using [tool or method]. Which part of your workflow matters most? I can explain the relevant work and where I would need to learn.

**“Why shouldn’t we hire you?”:** Acknowledge a genuine limitation without arguing yourself out of the role. Explain the trade-off and what relevant value you bring.

**Unknown answer:** Ask for context, state your assumptions, and work through the problem aloud if invited. Do not bluff.

## 3.7 Use competency questions to show decisions

| Competency | Evidence to include |
|---|---|
| Conflict | The underlying difference, how you understood it, and the agreement or decision reached. |
| Prioritization | The competing demands, business criteria, trade-off, and communication to affected people. |
| Influence | What the other person cared about and how that changed your approach. |
| Communication | How you adapted the message and enabled understanding or action. |
| Leadership | Direction set, others enabled, and ownership taken beyond completing your own task. |
| Growth | Feedback received, behavior changed, and what improved. |

For AI questions, describe a real problem you used AI to solve, how you checked the output, and what you kept under human judgment. Avoid listing tools without an outcome.

**Before finishing an answer:** Check that you answered the question, made your role clear, provided credible evidence, and connected the result to the interviewer’s concern. Then stop and allow a follow-up.
