# Chapter 6 — Clear Workplace Communication

> **Source:** Condensed from *The Day Day Up Career Playbook* (concise edition), based on Jonathan Lee's career-coaching videos.

Optimize for understanding and a useful response. Simple language, a clear point, and a specific ask matter more than elaborate phrasing.

## 6.1 Decide the message before editing the words

![Scissor method: replace a tangled status history with the main point, cause, and decision request.](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-6/01-comparison-scissor-update.png)

Before an important message, answer:

1. What does this person need to know?
2. Why does it matter to them?
3. What do I want them to do?

Then write the main point first. Keep necessary context; cut background that does not change the decision.

**Before:**

> We had a vendor issue in week two, then the data migration took longer, and QA found some things. The timeline might move.

**After, adapted from the corpus:**

> We’re two weeks behind and the launch date is at risk. The vendor delay caused the slip. Should we hold the date and cut the reporting feature, or move the date?

This is the coach’s “scissor method”: start with **the main thing**, end with **the reason I’m telling you**.

## 6.2 Choose one structure for the situation

| Situation | Structure |
|---|---|
| Short update | Main point → cause → requested decision or action. |
| Recommendation | Recommendation → supporting reasons → expected outcome. |
| Complex decision | Situation → complication → question → answer (SCQA). |
| Meeting contribution | Conclusion → supporting point → implication → ask. |
| Presentation | One takeaway → three supporting milestones → evidence. |

Do not stack several frameworks into one answer. Use the simplest structure that makes the issue understandable.

**Recommendation:**

> My recommendation is to prioritize feature A. Based on X and Y, it gives us the fastest path to improving metric Z.

Be ready to explain trade-offs and uncertainty. A clear recommendation does not require hiding material risks.

For slides, put the conclusion in the title. “Margin is improving because of customer mix” tells the audience more than “Q4 analysis.” Each slide should support one idea.

## 6.3 Be confident about your recommendation and precise about evidence

![A precise recommendation links an evidence limit, a directional signal, and a next step.](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-6/02-framework-calibrated-recommendation.png)

Distinguish observation, interpretation, and proposal. Commit to a direction while making important limits visible.

> The sample is small, so this is a directional signal rather than a definitive result. It agrees with last quarter’s trend. I recommend [next step] to test whether it holds.

When discussing someone else’s behavior, describe what happened rather than labeling the person:

| Avoid | Use |
|---|---|
| “You’re not detail-oriented.” | “The daily update was missing, so the client could not see the status.” |
| “This is wrong.” | “Here is the part I see differently, and why.” |
| “Why did you do that?” | “Can you walk me through your thinking?” |

Ask for clarification rather than pretending to understand:

> Could you rephrase that? I want to make sure I understand what you need.

## 6.4 Make commitments explicit

Remove unnecessary apology and hedging, but do not remove accountability or genuine uncertainty.

| Habit | Better wording |
|---|---|
| “Sorry to bother you, can I get an update?” | “Do you have an update on [item]?” |
| “Just checking in…” | “Checking in on [item].” |
| “I’ll try to finish tomorrow.” | “I expect to finish tomorrow. I’ll update you by end of day if that changes.” |
| Apologizing before understanding a problem | “Let’s clarify what happened and what needs to change.” |

When you did make a mistake, acknowledge it and explain the correction. The point is to avoid reflexively accepting blame for everything, not to avoid saying sorry.

## 6.5 Use your voice to make the message easier to follow

Adjust three things deliberately:

- **Pace:** Slow down for important reasoning and unfamiliar terms. Pause before the key point.
- **Volume:** Be audible for the room or microphone without forcing your voice.
- **Tone:** Let your interest or concern show. Finish a statement as a statement rather than making every recommendation sound like a question.

For routine conversations, prioritize participation over perfect grammar. For high-stakes communication, prepare the structure and rehearse aloud. The coach suggests brief daily speaking opportunities tied to existing routines; use them to build comfort, not to monitor every grammatical error.

## 6.6 Speak up by making the meeting easier

You do not need a novel idea every time. There are three useful entry points:

1. **Clarify:** “Which customer group are we discussing?”
2. **Connect:** “Does this relate to the retention issue we saw last week?”
3. **Move toward action:** “What would we need to decide before we can proceed?”

If the discussion is moving too quickly, summarize what you understood and ask for confirmation. If you lead the meeting, clarify its purpose, bring the discussion back to the decision, and close with owners and next steps.

For small talk, give the other person a small conversational opening and ask a genuine follow-up. Be interested rather than trying to perform an impressive personality.

## 6.7 Use AI as an editor without losing the message

The coach’s preferred approach is to decide the content yourself, write it in your own English, and use AI for editing.

> Correct the grammar and improve clarity. Keep my meaning, level of certainty, and natural tone. Do not add claims, generic praise, or new arguments.

Read the result aloud. Restore wording you would actually use and verify that the edit did not change your commitment or certainty. Use company-approved tools for work material.

**Before sending or speaking:** The main point is easy to find, the evidence supports it, the audience knows what response is needed, and the language sounds like something you would say.
