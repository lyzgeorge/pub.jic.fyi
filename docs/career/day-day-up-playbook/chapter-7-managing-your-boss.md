# Chapter 7 — Managing Your Boss

> **Source:** Condensed from *The Day Day Up Career Playbook* (concise edition), based on Jonathan Lee's career-coaching videos.

Use the relationship to establish shared expectations, make progress predictable, and raise difficult issues before they become surprises.

## 7.1 Make the 1:1 an alignment meeting

Send routine status separately when useful. Spend conversation time on decisions, priorities, support, and development.

| Topic | Ask or bring |
|---|---|
| Expectations | “What does success look like, and are we still solving the right problem?” |
| Priorities | “What is most important right now? What should move down the list?” |
| Support | A problem, possible options, and the help or decision you need. |
| Development | “What strength should I build on, and what improvement would most increase my impact?” |

Surface disagreement while there is still time to act. Private disagreement combined with apparent agreement creates avoidable surprises.

**Resource trade-off:**

> If we need A by next week, I’ll need support from Y. Without that support, I can deliver B by next week. Which fits our priorities better?

End with a shared understanding of the next action, owner, and expected outcome.

## 7.2 Give predictable updates and escalate early

Your manager should not have to discover a missed deadline after it has passed. Agree on the level of detail and update cadence they need.

Use **status → risk → options → recommendation → next update**:

> The report is likely to be three days late because of a data issue. We can deliver the verified portion Friday or the full report Monday. I recommend [option] because [reason]. I’ll confirm the remaining work tomorrow.

Bring options when possible, but do not delay escalating a serious risk merely because you have not solved it. Be explicit about what you know, what you are checking, and what help you need.

## 7.3 Build trust through useful follow-through

Understand how your manager communicates, decides, and handles uncertainty. Adapt your updates to observable preferences rather than diagnosing their personality.

- If surprises create problems, give earlier heads-ups.
- If they want involvement, share a draft before the final version.
- If they avoid decisions, make the decision smaller and clearer.
- When advice helps, report what you tried and what changed.

> I tried your suggestion about [action]. It helped us [result]. Thanks for the guidance.

In a new role, ask about tools, team routines, priorities, and informal decision-making before proposing major changes. Curiosity can reveal more than an early attempt to prove yourself.

## 7.4 Push back with a trade-off and a path forward

A useful “no” protects an agreed priority:

1. Acknowledge the need or pressure.
2. Explain the constraint and its consequence.
3. Offer delegation, reduced scope, reprioritization, or a later date.

> I understand the reporting request is urgent. The launch is also our top priority this week. Taking on both would put it at risk. Could someone else draft the report, or should we move [launch task]?

For a larger request, first understand the manager’s goal. Use specific examples to explain the cost of the current plan, then propose an alternative that supports the same goal.

You can make a sound case and still receive a no. Decide your next step from the response rather than assuming better wording can resolve every disagreement.

## 7.5 Give feedback without making a personal accusation

Choose a private conversation and ask whether they are open to feedback. Describe observable behavior, its effect on work, and the improvement you want to explore.

> I’ve noticed that ideas from the last few meetings are being raised afterward in private rather than in the room. I’m concerned we’re missing input. What are you seeing from your side? What could we change together?

After public criticism, cool down before responding. Seek clarification privately:

> When [specific behavior] happened in the meeting, I felt [effect]. I’d like to understand your concern and discuss how we can handle this feedback more productively.

This approach depends on some mutual respect. Repeated disrespect is not necessarily a problem you can solve through phrasing alone.

## 7.6 Keep a record for reviews and promotion discussions

Maintain a short evidence log as work happens:

- The problem and why it mattered.
- Your contribution and the resulting impact.
- Supporting feedback, documents, or metrics.
- The capability demonstrated and any development need.

Use that record to discuss growth without relying on memory or general claims of effort. Pair each development area with action already underway.

If a promotion request is declined, ask what specific evidence is missing, who decides, and when progress can be reviewed. Chapter 8 covers promotion planning in more detail.

## 7.7 Handle a PIP with clarity and documentation

The coach distinguishes a genuine improvement process from one intended to lead to an exit. His suggested commitment question can open discussion, but a manager’s answer cannot reliably prove which situation you face.

**Practical steps:**

1. Ask what successful completion means and how it affects your position.
2. Convert vague goals into measurable expectations, deadlines, and evidence.
3. Clarify what support will be provided and who evaluates progress.
4. Keep written records of the agreement, updates, and feedback.
5. Understand the document and your options before signing or negotiating an exit.

> To make this actionable, can we agree on specific outcomes for “improved communication,” the support available, and how progress will be assessed?

**Editorial caution:** PIP, resignation, severance, and reference arrangements depend on the employer and jurisdiction. Do not resign on the assumption that severance follows; get appropriate advice and any agreement in writing before acting.

**Working standard:** Expectations are explicit, risks are visible early, and important decisions do not depend on competing memories of a conversation.
