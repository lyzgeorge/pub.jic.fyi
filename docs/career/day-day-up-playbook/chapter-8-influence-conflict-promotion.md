# Chapter 8 — Influence, Conflict & Promotion

> **Source:** Condensed from *The Day Day Up Career Playbook* (concise edition), based on Jonathan Lee's career-coaching videos.

Connect your work to business priorities, help others act, and make evidence of your impact visible to the people who need it.

## 8.1 Make useful work visible

Visibility should explain outcomes, not advertise busyness. Send a short summary at an appropriate cadence:

> **Delivered:** [result and why it matters].
>
> **Unblocked:** [decision or obstacle resolved].
>
> **Next:** [priority, risk, or support needed].

Share credit accurately. A team win is also a chance to show your contribution and make a useful approach reusable:

> We solved [problem] by [approach]. My part was [contribution], with [colleague] leading [contribution]. The result was [impact]. This may also help [other team].

Keep the supporting evidence in the record described in Chapter 7; do not maintain several duplicate “brag books.”

## 8.2 Understand what the other person is protecting

Before trying to persuade, identify who can influence the decision and what matters to them.

1. Ask how they define success and what risk concerns them.
2. Explain how your proposal addresses that concern.
3. Offer a small next step that reduces the cost of engaging.

> Why is cost the main concern right now? I want to understand what we need to protect before recommending a change.

The coach’s **ACT** framework makes a proposal easier to consider:

- **Assert credibility with nuance:** State what the evidence supports and where it is limited.
- **Contextualize with social proof:** Mention relevant adoption or results, if real.
- **Trigger a small action:** Suggest a review, pilot, or short discussion rather than demanding full commitment.

> The sample is small, but the signal agrees with last quarter’s trend. Product and operations have tried the draft and found it useful. Could I add you to the review so you can flag concerns before we expand it?

## 8.3 Disagree on the issue while preserving respect

Separate the shared goal from the proposed method. Ask why the current approach matters, then compare alternatives against that goal.

> We both want the VP to trust the recommendation. You’re emphasizing completeness; I’m concerned about making the decision clear. Can we lead with the conclusion and keep the detailed numbers available underneath?

If your intent has been misunderstood, clarify it directly:

> I’m not dismissing the idea. I want to make sure we present it clearly enough for the team to act on it.

Use observations rather than judgments about personality. State trade-offs and decide how the group will proceed. Once a decision is made, distinguish a commitment to execute from agreement with every part of the reasoning.

When a conflict is heated, pause before choosing a response. Aim for a result and behavior you can stand behind afterward, not the sharpest reply.

## 8.4 Help without silently taking over accountability

Make ownership explicit before stepping into another workstream.

> My part is complete. I noticed part B is behind. Would you like me to help with [specific piece]?

Then align with the relevant manager:

> [Colleague] agreed I’ll support [piece]. They remain responsible for [scope], and I’ll deliver [scope] by [date]. Please flag any concern with that plan.

If a problem occurs, explain the timeline, responsibilities, and next action factually. Do not accept blame automatically or use documentation to avoid responsibility for your actual part.

## 8.5 Build relationships around useful exchanges

| Relationship | Purpose | Practical action |
|---|---|---|
| Sponsor | Advocates for opportunities based on confidence in your work. | Share relevant impact and make it easy to see where you could contribute more. |
| Informed peer or mentor | Provides context you lack. | Ask a specific question and offer information or help in return. |
| Mentee | Gives you practice developing others. | Help with onboarding, review work, or act as a sounding board. |

A useful opening is concrete:

> My team recently solved [problem]. I think your team may face something similar. Would a short walkthrough of what worked be useful?

Build relationships beyond your immediate manager without treating every interaction as a promotion request. Follow through when people help you.

## 8.6 Work at the level you want to grow into

The coach’s career-level model is a useful lens, not a universal company rubric:

| Level | Main contribution | Visible behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Junior individual contributor | Reliability | Delivers agreed work and flags risks early. |
| Senior individual contributor | Ownership | Defines problems, makes trade-offs, and reduces the need for supervision. |
| People manager | Team performance | Delegates outcomes, removes recurring obstacles, and develops others. |
| Director | Organizational performance | Aligns teams, resources, and longer-term priorities. |

Do not respond to every plateau by doing more of the previous level’s work. Identify what your organization actually expects at the next level and seek a bounded opportunity to demonstrate it.

For delegation, specify the outcome and constraints rather than prescribing every task:

> You own [outcome]. Success means [criteria], by [date]. Let’s agree on constraints and checkpoints, then you can choose the approach.

## 8.7 Make promotion a concrete discussion

Ask three questions:

1. **Who decides?** Your manager may advocate without having final authority.
2. **What evidence matters?** Obtain the rubric or examples of next-level work.
3. **How will that evidence be seen?** Agree on projects, presentations, and a review date.

> What would you need to see to support my promotion? Which decision makers need that evidence, and when can we review progress?

Keep doing work linked to business priorities. Avoid inventing side projects that no decision maker values. If the path remains vague despite clear evidence and repeated discussion, compare external opportunities rather than waiting indefinitely.

**Weekly focus:** Decide your most important outcome, run a small learn-and-adjust loop, and communicate what changed. More activity is not automatically more impact.
