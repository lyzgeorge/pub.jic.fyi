# Chapter 9 — Career Strategy & Mindset

> **Source:** Condensed from *The Day Day Up Career Playbook* (concise edition), based on Jonathan Lee's career-coaching videos.

Choose a direction you can sustain, gather evidence through action, and distinguish problems you can improve from environments you may need to leave.

## 9.1 Choose the costs as well as the reward

A desired title or salary comes with a working life. Before pursuing it, name the demands you are accepting: hours, responsibility, uncertainty, visibility, politics, or a different kind of work.

Ask:

- Do I want the actual work, or mainly the approval attached to it?
- Which demands am I willing to sustain?
- Which trade-offs would make the outcome unattractive?

Then choose deliberately. A path is not wrong merely because it differs from someone else’s expectations.

When frustration turns into “they should,” convert it into a request or decision:

> “My manager should support my growth” becomes “What support do I need, how can I request it, and where else could I obtain it?”

This does not excuse unfair treatment. It identifies where you can act.

## 9.2 Build confidence from evidence

Do not wait for confidence before every useful action. Choose a small behavior you can repeat: ask a clarifying question, state a recommendation, or apply for a credible stretch role.

Afterward, record what happened and adjust. Measure progress through behavior and feedback rather than how fearless you felt.

When reviewing a setback, separate:

1. **Facts:** What happened?
2. **Interpretation:** What story am I telling about it?
3. **Learning:** What should change next time?

Retell the event fairly, including your contribution and constraints, without deleting mistakes. A setback can reveal a skill gap or poor fit without defining your entire ability.

## 9.3 Take risks you can afford to learn from

![Bounded career experiment versus unbounded risk: limit downside, learn, and preserve options](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-9/02-comparison-bounded-career-risks.png)

The coach describes growth as **Stretch × Stay**: attempt work beyond your current comfort and remain long enough to develop capability. Treat this as a metaphor, not a financial formula.

Before a move, identify the possible downside, what limits it, the learning value, and the conditions for stopping. Favor experiments that preserve options: a bounded project, a small business test, or a targeted skill investment.

Do not confuse persistence with refusing to update your decision. New evidence about health, finances, fit, or opportunity can justify changing course.

At work, move beyond reporting by offering a reasoned recommendation:

> I’m seeing [pattern]. If it were my call, I’d [action] because [reason]. The main uncertainty is [uncertainty], which we can check by [step].

## 9.4 Diagnose skills, environment, and market separately

The coach’s “garden” model distinguishes three sources of growth:

![Career garden model showing seed (skills and interests), soil (manager, company, team), and climate (industry, geography, timing)](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-9/01-infographic-career-garden-model.png)

| Layer | Question | Possible action |
|---|---|---|
| Seed: capabilities and interests | What work do I do well and want to keep improving? | Invest in a relevant strength or address a specific gap. |
| Soil: manager, company, and team | Does this environment let that capability grow? | Seek better support, scope, or a different team. |
| Climate: industry and timing | Is demand for this work expanding or weakening? | Explore adjacent markets and transferable skills. |

Avoid blaming your ability for every environmental problem, or blaming the environment for every skill gap. Use real projects, feedback, job openings, and conversations with experienced people to distinguish them.

Change one variable at a time where possible. It makes the move easier to explain and the result easier to interpret.

## 9.5 Set your pace and know when to leave

The coach recommends periodically interviewing externally—roughly every two years in his own practice—to test your market value and avoid letting one company set your entire pace. Use a cadence appropriate to your situation, not an automatic resignation schedule.

When deciding whether to stay, consider:

- Skills and scope you are gaining.
- Compensation and future options.
- Manager support and whether the role is needed.
- Effects on confidence, health, and relationships.
- What a realistic alternative would improve or cost.

The useful question is **“What is staying doing to me?”** Pair it with **“What would leaving require?”** A poor month and a persistently damaging role call for different responses.

If the role’s value is unclear, ask how it contributes and what success should look like. The coach’s more direct backfill question—“If I left, would you replace this role?”—can reveal concerns, but use it with awareness of the conversation it may start.

## 9.6 Find direction through work, not labels alone

Start with evidence from real projects. Identify the skills that produced results and the conditions in which you worked well.

The corpus recommends a Good Time Journal: over several weeks, note activities that create engagement and energy. Look for patterns in the activity, environment, people, and tools. Personality frameworks may prompt reflection, but should not choose your career for you.

Compare plausible roles against:

- What you can already demonstrate.
- What you would enjoy developing further.
- The daily work, not just the title.
- Market demand and practical constraints.

Use conversations and applications to test the fit. Another course is worthwhile when it closes a specific, evidenced gap; it is less useful as a way to postpone testing the market.

## 9.7 Use AI to expand your contribution

Move the discussion from tools to outcomes:

| Level | Focus |
|---|---|
| Task | Completing an individual piece of work more efficiently. |
| Process | Making a recurring workflow more reliable or scalable. |
| Business | Improving decisions or enabling a valuable new capability. |

Choose the appropriate level for your role. Explain the problem, your use of AI, the checks you applied, and the result.

The coach’s stakes test is useful: **If this goes wrong, how bad is it?** Routine drafts may need light review; consequential decisions need stronger verification and human judgment. Confidentiality still matters even when the task appears low stakes.

Build on capabilities others already value in you—judgment, domain context, relationships, communication—and use AI to free time or improve those capabilities. For writing and interview preparation, retain ownership of the message and verify the output.

**Career decision standard:** You can explain what you are pursuing, why it fits, what it costs, what evidence supports it, and what would cause you to reconsider.
