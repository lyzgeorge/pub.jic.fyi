# Chapter 1 — Resume & Application Materials

> This chapter covers everything that happens before an interviewer ever speaks to you: the resume, the LinkedIn profile and filters, the decision to apply, and the outreach that gets a human to look at you. The coach's central claim is that most Chinese and non-native candidates in North America lose at this stage for reasons that have nothing to do with ability. They write resumes that describe responsibilities ("I'm the same as everyone else who had this job — nothing to see here"), they leave in details that trigger a recruiter's quiet doubts about fit, they reject themselves before HR does, and they spend 100% of their hours in the single most crowded channel — online applications. The fix is to treat the resume as a sales document and the job search as a system with four channels, not one.

**What you'll be able to do**

- Rewrite any responsibility bullet as an impact story using the skill → action → result (problem → skills → achievement) formula, and pass the "boss test" on every line.
- Lay out a one-page, one-column, ATS-safe resume in the coach's template order, with a one-line candidate statement instead of a summary.
- Strip the red flags that make a Western recruiter hesitate: company locations, Chinese-only tools, old or irrelevant jobs, graduation dates, and missing keywords.
- Manufacture credible experience when you have none, using free company-branded simulation projects and freelance work.
- Read a job description the way the hiring manager wrote it — order as priority, plain-language anomalies as the real pain — and apply when you match roughly a third.
- Use LinkedIn filters, the URL "seconds hack," Open-to-Work refreshes, and "hiring" post searches to be among the first ten applicants.
- Rebalance effort across the "core four" channels with the rule of twenty, send a three-part cold pitch to a decision maker, and keep a rejection warm for the next opening.

## 1.1 What a resume is for: a sales document read in six seconds

**The concept.** The coach returns to one sentence in almost every resume video: the resume "is not a legal document. It is a sales document". It is not a history textbook, not a passport, and nobody audits it. Its only job is to make one person decide "we need to talk".

**The reader's perspective.** Two facts about the reader shape everything else in this chapter:

1. **They are looking for a reason to say no.** The screener's real question is "is there any reason I shouldn't interview this person?". Every line either helps answer that in your favour or gives them the reason they were looking for.
2. **They spend about six seconds.** Recruiters average roughly six seconds per resume, so the document has to be scannable: one page, best points in the top third, keywords bolded, white space left alone, and the interview decision made "a no-brainer".

The coach also names a bias that Chinese candidates walk into. Western recruiters are biased against factual, responsibility-based writing and tend to discount China experience. His response is not to complain about it: "Discrimination is human nature. The question is... how to turn it around to work for you". Lectures 1.2–1.4 are the toolkit for doing that.

**Two things a resume must never do.**

- *Describe responsibilities.* Everyone who held the title did the same things; a responsibility list makes you invisible "no matter how fancy your action verb is".
- *Lie.* Lying is common among new grads and people without relevant experience, and it fails because the interviewer will be impressed, then ask how you measured the result, why you chose that method, and what the hardest part was. You are "trying to fool someone who is way more experienced than you". Estimating a number you genuinely produced is fine (1.2); inventing an achievement is not.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Don't waste their time, or they might just waste your resume."

**Common mistakes**

- Treating the resume as a complete record and listing every job since university.
- Filling white space and shrinking the font to fit more in.
- Writing for yourself ("I worked hard") instead of for the screener ("here is why the business was better because I was there").

## 1.2 The bullet: three levels, the boss test, and the golden formula

**The concept.** The single highest-leverage edit on any resume is the bullet point. The coach describes three levels of bullet writing, and only the third gets interviews:

| Level | What it looks like | How the reader hears it |
|---|---|---|
| L1 Responsibility | "Performed month-end reconciliations to verify accuracy of financial records." | "Same as everyone with this title." |
| L2 Quantified | Numbers stuffed into every line. | "Made-up numbers that I can never verify." |
| L3 Impact | The business problem, what you did, what changed for the business. | "This person understands why the work matters." |

Note the trap at Level 2: the coach is *not* against numbers — he insists on them — but numbers without a business story read as unverifiable padding. "Effort is cheap, but impact is what matters to HR".

**The boss test.** To find the impact hidden in a responsibility, ask: *if I nail this responsibility, why is my manager happy?* The answer cannot be "because I finished it." It has to be about the business. The coach's worked example is an accounting candidate who said "I just do reconciliations." He asked, "What happens if your month end close is two days late?" — and she realised the impact was that the company grew without hiring more accountants.

**The golden formula.** Several videos give the same three-part structure under slightly different names — skill + action + result; problem solved + skills used + impact on the business; problem → skills → achievement; "what challenge you faced, what relevant skills you used, and how it helped your team or business". The consolidated method:

1. **Open with the business problem** (the client complaint, the close delay, the scaling volume) — this is what the boss test surfaces.
2. **Name the skill or tool you used** — and lift the wording from the target JD (1.5) so it also matches the ATS.
3. **State the result** as one of three kinds of impact: revenue up, cost down, or efficiency up.
4. **Keep it to two lines.** "Three lines is tiring to read, and one line just doesn't have enough detail".

**Worked examples from the transcripts.**

```
BEFORE  Performed month-end reconciliations to verify accuracy and
        completeness of financial records.
AFTER   Redesigned reconciliation workflows to solve month-end close delays
        as transaction volumes scaled. Reduced close time by two days,
        supporting rapid growth without additional headcount.
```


```
BEFORE  Used Python to clean data pipelines and reduce reporting time by 40%.
AFTER   Proactively resolved client reporting complaints by building an
        automated data cleaning pipeline with Python. Reduced manual
        reporting time by 40%.
```
Notice that the "before" already had a number — it was a Level 2 bullet. What moved it to Level 3 was stating the client complaint first.

```
BEFORE  I input data into the customer database spreadsheets.
AFTER   Managed customer databases with over 500 data entries per day and
        took initiative to automate 25% of common entries to increase
        personal and team efficiency.
```
The same bullet appears later as: "Managed 500+ data entries, taking initiative to automate 25% of tasks with RPA, inspired organization-wide automation". The pattern the coach names here: scale (numbers per day) → initiative taken → percentage improvement → who benefited.

**On numbers you don't have.** Estimate. "Nobody's gonna audit whether you actually increased efficiency by 20% or two percent"; nobody will call your old manager to check 21% versus 22%; "no one's auditing you". The point of the number is to show you understand and measure value. "I've never met an accomplishment that couldn't be quantified with some creativity".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "A good resume does one thing above all. It shows what happened because you were there."

**Common mistakes**

- Fancy verbs on a responsibility ("Spearheaded data entry project to improve business outcomes") — still Level 1.
- A number on every line with no problem stated — reads as fabricated.
- Answering the boss test with "because I got it done".
- Three-line bullets.

## 1.3 Structure and formatting: the template, the one-line statement, and ATS-safe layout

**The concept.** "Good formatting doesn't get you hired, but bad formatting will get you rejected". The coach shares one template he says produced his own offers from Google, Uber, P&G and Shopify and is the best-performing template among his candidates.

**Section order**
1. **Name** — if you have no preference between your English and Chinese name, use the English one: "recruiters who discriminate are rare, but they do exist."
2. **Summary** — skip it unless you truly have something meaningful; at most one line that captures your unique core competency and the reader's curiosity.
3. **Work experience** — at least half the resume; chronological; 2–5 bullets per role; two lines per bullet.
4. **Other professional experience** — older or less relevant jobs, kept short, "mainly to make timelines make sense."
5. **Education** — at the top if you recently graduated or are in school; otherwise "down, down, down."
6. **Skills / certifications** — hard skills lifted from target job descriptions, plus required certifications.
7. **Interests** — optional, for a personal touch.

**The one-line candidate statement.** Multi-line summaries stuffed with "fast learner, dynamic professional, leader" hurt you; your resume already *is* the summary. The top of the page is prime real estate, so replace the paragraph with a single line that confirms focus, experience and skills, and that you can re-tailor in 30 seconds. The one exception is a genuinely arresting line — the coach's example is an Olympic gold medalist pivoting into strategy.

```
[data-fluent] [role] with [N years'] experience leveraging [skill] to drive [impact] across [industry].

"Data-fluent business analyst with five years' experience leveraging data
 storytelling to drive impact across CPG."
```


**Projects versus work.** A data analyst with three years' experience came to the coach with a project section longer than her work section. That proportion signals lack of confidence in your real experience; projects are "toys, not a production-grade asset" — no deadlines, no business requirements, perfect data. Keep projects shorter than work experience. (The exception is when you have no work experience at all — see 1.4 on manufacturing it.)

**Formatting rules** (consolidated from):

- Traditional single-column, black-and-white text. Designed templates "kill ATS"; sections must be readable by humans and machines.
- One page, unless you have 10+ years of experience or need a CV-style document.
- Font size: the Bilibili videos say 10pt or larger; the earlier YouTube video says not to use size-10 fonts at all and not to fill white space. Read the two together as "10pt is the floor, and if you're at the floor you have too much content."
- Best points in the top third; bold keywords.
- The file name is part of the first impression.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Oh, wait, that's what your resume is supposed to be." — on the "summary of qualifications" section.

**Common mistakes**

- Two-column or graphic templates that the ATS can't parse.
- A project section that outweighs work experience.
- Education at the top for someone ten years out of school.
- Sending "resume_final_v3.pdf".

## 1.4 Removing red flags: de-risking the immigrant resume and filling gaps

**The concept.** Even inclusive recruiters have a quiet voice that says "they're from another country, they're ESL, will they fit in?" Your job is to mute that voice by removing everything that triggers it, so the reader focuses on experience rather than origin. "Don't just translate your experience, translate the story it tells".

### The four removals

Consolidated from:

1. **Company locations.** List Chinese companies by name only, using the official English name if one exists. "Your resume is not a passport."
2. **Non-global tools and processes.** Swap for the Western equivalent: Xiaohongshu / Douyin → Instagram / TikTok; DingTalk → Slack or MS Teams. If that feels like lying, take a course in the equivalent tool. "Hiring managers care about recognition, not translation."
3. **Old or unrelated jobs.** Listing everything makes you look unfocused or overqualified — and overqualification is often "corporate speak for good old fashioned ageism", "ageism in a suit". The coach's example: a Canadian accountant had five years of journalism in China and five of China finance removed entirely so the resume focused on recent Canadian experience, and was "presented as a completely different person".
4. **Graduation dates.** Western resumes don't require them; a ten-year-old ML degree invites a comparison with this year's graduates that you don't need.

### Missing skills: "just in time"

If the JD names a skill you lack and you leave it off, the ATS keyword match fails and a human may never see the resume. The coach's rule: put the skill on the resume, then go learn it — a weekend Python course is the standard example — never claiming mastery, and preparing properly if there's a technical interview. "You don't need permission to claim basic proficiency". "Speed is survival".

### No relevant experience: manufacture it

"You can't always be employed, but you can always be hustling". Three sources of experience you can create yourself:

- **Company-branded virtual programs (Forage).** A free platform with hands-on simulation projects designed by JP Morgan, BCG, Pepsi, Tata and others; each takes a few hours and yields a certificate plus a recognisable company name for the resume. Two AI examples the coach names: BCG's Gen AI financial chatbot and Tata's Gen AI data analytics. (The coach notes he is not paid to recommend it; the transcript renders the name as "theforge.com".)
- **Freelance.** "One client, one project can be its own work experience" on Upwork.
- **Side projects posted online**.

Write each one up under a Projects section using the same formula as a work bullet: the problem solved, the key skills used, and the expected achievement as outlined by the project. Side benefit: the brand name makes a skimming recruiter pause, and the job title, skills and company name lift your ATS match rate. A branded project "shows, don't tell" interest in a field like AI, versus keyword-stuffing.

### The flags you can't remove

Some red flags stay on the page — short tenures, gaps, a departure from a desirable company, five years at one level. The coach's interview-side guidance is that interviewers fear two profiles, the *flight risk* and the *low performer*, and that you pre-empt each with a story rather than hide it: "Sure, your resume has red flags. So does every resume on my desk. Just raise that flag and show me that I have nothing to be afraid of". That material belongs to the interview chapters; the resume task here is simply to know which fear your resume triggers so you're ready for it.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "You'll be limited by one of two things, your skills or your beliefs. Never let it be your beliefs."

**Common mistakes**

- Leaving "Beijing" or "Shenzhen" after every employer.
- Listing DingTalk, Xiaohongshu or other tools a Western reader doesn't recognise.
- Twenty years of history on one page, which reads as unfocused and old.
- Waiting to get a job before gaining the experience the job requires.

## 1.5 Reading the job description and deciding to apply

**The concept.** A JD is "a wish list, not a checklist". Hiring managers write unreasonable ones by copying what every similar posting asks for, so everyone ends up "chasing the same single unicorn." The coach's own JD for a strategy & ops role asked for investment-banking or consulting background, 5–7 years, people management and tech exposure; he hired someone with five years at a big bank and none of the rest — "the best person that I could get".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Screening candidates is HR's job, not yours."

### Three rules for whether to apply

1. **The one-third rule.** If you match about a third of the requirements, apply. That third may be the only part the manager actually cares about.
2. **Match the title *or* the industry, not both.** An accountant in banking can move to accountant in tech, or to FP&A manager in banking; FP&A manager in tech is a double jump and much harder. The coach applied this to his own corporate → tech pivot by keeping "strategy and operations" titles and changing only the industry.
3. **Order is priority.** The first bullets in the requirements list are what the manager cares about most; later ones are increasingly negotiable.

### Reading the JD the way the manager wrote it

The coach says to read a JD twice: once before applying and once before interviewing. The pre-application read looks for two things:

- **Order.** "What is listed first is what the hiring manager cares about the most."
- **Anomalies.** HR pushes JDs toward "complicated corporate speak" — in the coach's own case, "say no to a VP without me holding their hand" became "strong communication skills." Any plain, unusual, non-corporate sentence is where the manager fought to keep their real need. That line is the pain you tailor to.

The decision maker "is not an ATS system. It's a real human being who is in pain." So tailor the resume to remove that pain rather than keyword-stuffing — but do add the keywords for the top priorities and the anomaly, because the ATS runs first. This matches the coach's earlier advice that a serious application means roughly ten hours on the JD, talking to people in the role, and rewriting stories laser-focused on those skills, so the resume both passes automated screening *and* reads well to the human after.

The pre-interview read splits the JD into "can you excel?" signals (day-to-day, core activities, the predecessor's projects) and "will I like working with you?" signals (trait words like "thinks differently," "thrive in chaos") — that half is covered in the interview chapters.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Most candidates read the JD to decide if they should apply. The ones who get hired read it to decide who they should become."

**Common mistakes**

- Rejecting yourself because you meet six of ten bullets.
- Changing both title and industry in one move.
- Treating every bullet in the JD as equally weighted.
- Keyword-stuffing for the ATS and forgetting the human.

## 1.6 Applying: timing, LinkedIn filters, and the daily system

**The concept.** "The problem's not you. It's your filters." One applicant in a hundred is 1%; one in ten is 10%. The coach's insight comes from the hiring side: when he ran a team he could not get enough *qualified* applicants and had to source on LinkedIn himself, and he saw recruiters screen on a rolling basis — once the week's five interview slots were filled, later applications were simply never seen.

### Three LinkedIn tricks

1. **Filter early and fast.**
   - Set the Jobs filter to **"under 10 applicants."**
   - Set **"Date posted"** to past 24 hours, then go to the browser URL, find the parameter `r86400` (the number of seconds in 24 hours) and change it to `3600` for the last hour, `7200` for the last two hours. Pair this with under-10-applicants and you are "first in line." Apply immediately.
2. **Refresh your Open-to-Work status.** Recruiters (and the coach, when sourcing) filter for people who *recently* set Open to Work, because long-standing "open" profiles are often people who already have jobs and ghost outreach. Turn the status off at the start of the weekend and back on at the end to refresh your ranking.
3. **Search posts, not jobs.** Hiring managers post their own openings. Search the keyword "hiring" plus your target title and switch the filter from Jobs to Posts. Qualified people who comment or message under that post get seen instead of getting lost in the ATS. (The coach offers a cold-message template for this; it isn't read aloud in the transcript, so use the three-part pitch in 1.7.)

### The job search as a system

The coach frames the search itself as a job. Everyone wants the big-company resume; few will pay for it with 1,000 applications and 999 rejections. Successful candidates "rely on process, not motivation": an intentional resume, daily applications even through rejection, skill-building before invites arrive, mock interviews as a feedback loop, and a tracker that sets daily application targets from your own application-to-interview rate. "Discipline without a system is just called suffering".

He is equally direct about the Chinese reflex to study instead of apply — "we learn so that we can avoid doing." In 2020 at Uber, newly married with a mortgage, he took a Python course he hated before realising he didn't want to be a data scientist; applying, not studying, showed him nobody could tell stories like him. Even a failed application round is worth it because it reveals what the market wants and which gaps are real.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Stop filling up your head with more learning. Fill up your resume instead."

A brief note for underrepresented candidates: North American companies set voluntary diversity targets and actively source on LinkedIn — the coach watched a VP of engineering tell her team to "reach out to at least three candidates from underrepresented backgrounds." There are no quotas or laws; you still have to be the most qualified. But "job hunt with confidence because you're worth it and they know it".

**Common mistakes**

- Applying to a posting that already shows 100+ applicants.
- Leaving Open-to-Work on for months without refreshing it.
- Only searching the Jobs tab.
- Enrolling in one more course instead of submitting five applications.

## 1.7 Beyond applications: the core four, cold pitches, referrals, and keeping doors open

**The concept.** Think of the job search as a 2×2 grid: people who know you versus people who don't, and pitching one-to-one versus one-to-many. That gives four boxes — the coach's "core four" — and most people live in the hardest one.

| | One-to-one | One-to-many |
|---|---|---|
| **They don't know you** | Cold message to a decision maker — "almost nobody's doing it" | Applications — thousands of competitors |
| **They know you** | Networking / referrals — good, but getting oversaturated | Sharing expertise on LinkedIn — "learning in public" |

### Box 1: Referrals — what a real one is

"A true referral doesn't come from an employee filling out a form for you. It happens when that employee tells the hiring manager, I know this person, and you should interview them because of XYZ". The coach's own Google interview came through a friend's referral, and he says that route is common at brands inundated with applications — ask anyone connected, direct, second-degree or a networked stranger, to put in a word. He also cautions that Chinese candidates default to "coffee chats and referrals" while the Western workplace is less transactional; the most useful contact is often someone who *did the job* — a former direct report of the hiring manager, ideally in the same role, who has since left.

### Box 2: The cold message — "whale hunting"

Job boards are slow and full of fake, expired and no-sponsorship listings; with 30 days, "most companies are going to take longer just to respond to your application." So target the people who can actually help: directors, VPs, even CEOs. Results the coach cites: interviews or offers from the CEO of LVMH Americas (a candidate who DMed the CEO *after* being rejected), a VP at TikTok, and the CEO of Sonos — "that last one was me".

The 30-minute process:

1. Make a list of target companies.
2. Find decision makers at each — hiring managers, directors, VPs, executives.
3. Send a short pitch.

The pitch has three parts:

```
1. Your value proposition — what you can do for them.
2. Something specific about the company or role — proof you didn't send
   the same message to 300 people.
3. An easy call to action — "let's talk."
```

"Will this work 100% of the time? Of course not. But it's targeted and it's fast". "I know you can think of a hundred reasons why you don't want to do it. But just do it".

### Box 3: Learning in public

Something almost no Chinese professionals do but Western professionals do: share your expertise on LinkedIn — for example, post a thoughtful industry article with your own take so your network knows what you know. Especially useful for senior roles. A director the coach worked with was laid off, posted every day for a month, and later said the posts helped him land a VP role.

### The rule of twenty

"Every twenty days that you aren't getting the results that you want, move twenty percent of your effort away from job applications and into one of the other boxes." Start at 100% applications, then 80/20, then 60/20/20, then 40/20/20/20. The order and amounts matter less than staying flexible.

### Keeping a rejection warm

Rejections are also application material. Reply to every rejection within 24 hours, to a real person — if it came from a no-reply address, find the recruiter's or hiring manager's real email or add them on LinkedIn. Express brief disappointment, appreciation, continued interest, and ask permission to check in. Then, when a similar role opens, don't quietly re-apply: reply on the same thread. "This is the easiest yes that you will say among a pile of resumes because you liked me enough to interview me last time". The coach reports more than ten people wrote to say the email "changed their lives," including one who landed an offer at the same company right after the rejection. Both scripts are in the Templates section.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Humans hire humans that they trust and have a relationship with."

**Common mistakes**

- Spending 100% of hours in the applications box for months.
- Counting a referral-form submission as a referral.
- Sending the same cold message to 300 people with nothing specific in it.
- Letting a rejection end the relationship, then quietly re-applying to the same team later.

## Practice set

### Drills

**D1 — Boss test audit (1.2).** Copy your three most generic bullets. Under each, answer in one sentence: *If I nail this, why is my manager happy?* If your answer contains "because I finished it" or "because it was done accurately," write a second answer that names what changes for the business (headcount, close time, revenue, complaints). Use the coach's prompt: "What happens if [this] is two days late?"

**D2 — Three rewrites, two lines each (1.2).** Rewrite the three bullets from D1 as problem → skill → result. Rules: the business problem is the first clause; the tool or skill is named using the target JD's wording; the result is revenue up, cost down or efficiency up with an estimated number; exactly two lines at 10pt+. Check each against the reconciliation and Python examples.

**D3 — Level detection (1.2).** For every bullet on your current resume, label it L1 (responsibility), L2 (number without a problem) or L3 (impact story). Count. If more than a third are L1 or L2, schedule the rewrites.

**D4 — Six-second scan (1.1/1.3).** Give your resume to a friend for six seconds, take it away, and ask what they retained. If the answer isn't your best point, move that point into the top third and bold its keywords.

**D5 — Replace the summary (1.3).** Delete your summary paragraph. Write one line in the form *[descriptor] [role] with [N years'] experience leveraging [skill] to drive [impact] across [industry]*. Then write a second version tailored to a different JD in under 30 seconds.

**D6 — Format checklist (1.3).** Tick each: single column ☐ black-and-white ☐ font ≥10pt ☐ one page (unless 10+ years) ☐ work experience ≥ half the page ☐ projects section shorter than work section ☐ education positioned by recency ☐ skills lifted from target JDs ☐ professional file name ☐.

**D7 — Four-removal pass (1.4).** Go line by line and flag every company location, every non-global tool name, every job outside the relevant window, and every date on an education line. For each flag: delete, rename to the official English company name, swap to the Western-equivalent tool, or move to "Other professional experience".

**D8 — Just-in-time skill (1.4).** From one target JD, list the required skills you don't have. Add the most important one to your Skills section this week and book the weekend course that makes the claim honest. Note which ones would face a technical interview and plan accordingly.

**D9 — Manufacture one project (1.4).** Complete one company-branded virtual program this week. Write a single two-line Projects bullet: problem solved → key skills used → expected achievement as outlined by the project. Check it against the target JD's keywords.

**D10 — JD triage (1.5).** Take a JD you dismissed. Number the requirements in order. Highlight the first three. Circle any sentence written in plain, non-corporate language. Count how many requirements you meet — if it's around a third and you match title *or* industry, apply this week, and put the circled line's keywords in your top bullet.

**D11 — Filter setup (1.6).** Build and save two LinkedIn searches: (a) target title, under 10 applicants, URL parameter edited to `r3600`; (b) keyword "hiring [title]" with the filter set to Posts. Check both once a day. This weekend, toggle Open-to-Work off Friday and on Sunday.

**D12 — Twenty-day log and first whale (1.7).** Tally where your last 20 days of search hours went by box. If it's ~100% applications with no results, move 20% this week: list five target companies, find one decision maker at each, and send five three-part pitches.

**D13 — Rejection reply (1.7).** Pull up your last rejection email. Find a real human address for it. Send the 24-hour reply below (or a belated version). Set a reminder to watch that company's openings.

### Templates

**T1 — Impact bullet**
```
[Verb] [business problem] by [action using SKILL/TOOL from the JD],
[result: revenue ↑ / cost ↓ / efficiency ↑, with an estimated number].
```

**T2 — One-line candidate statement**
```
[Descriptor] [role] with [N years'] experience leveraging [skill] to drive [impact] across [industry].
```

**T3 — Projects bullet for a virtual program**
```
[Company] [program name] — Solved [problem] using [key skills]; delivered [expected achievement as outlined by the project].
```

**T4 — Cold pitch to a decision maker**
```
Hi [name],
[Value proposition — what you can do for them, one sentence.]
[Something specific about the company or role that shows you didn't mass-send this.]
[Easy call to action — e.g. "Would you be open to a quick chat?" / "Let's talk."]
```

**T5 — Reply to a rejection (within 24 hours, to a real person)**
> Thank you for getting back to me. Although I'm disappointed to not be moving forward, I sincerely enjoyed our conversations and appreciated the transparency throughout the process. It gave me an even deeper respect for the team and what you're building at [company]. I remain highly interested in working here someday. I'd love to stay connected. Would it be okay if I checked in again in a few months? If a future role aligns, I'd be excited to be considered again, especially given our positive conversations this time around. Thank you again.

**T6 — Re-open the thread when a new role appears**
> Hope you've been well. I wanted to quickly check in, as I noticed new openings on the team, specifically for [job title]. I'm still extremely interested in contributing to [company]. [Optional growth update.] I would love to pick our conversation back up if the timing works. Happy to share my updated materials if helpful. Warmly, [name].

**T7 — Message to someone who did the job**
> I'm interviewing for [role] under [manager] next week, and I genuinely value your perspective if you're open to sharing. No pressure at all, but I'm sitting where you sat not long ago. And as you can imagine, any insight would mean a lot.

## Cheat sheet

**What the resume is:** a sales document, not a legal document, not a history textbook, not a passport. Read in ~6 seconds by someone asking "is there any reason I shouldn't interview this person?"

**Bullet formula:** business problem → skill/action (JD wording) → result (revenue ↑ / cost ↓ / efficiency ↑, estimated number). Two lines. Boss test: *if I nail this, why is my manager happy?* — never "because I finished it."

**Three levels:** L1 responsibility (invisible) · L2 numbers without a story (unverifiable) · L3 impact (interview).

**Template order:** name (English if indifferent) → one-line statement or nothing → work experience (≥ half the page, 2–5 bullets each) → other professional experience (short) → education (top only if recent) → skills/certs from target JDs → interests (optional).

**Statement:** *[Descriptor] [role] with [N years'] experience leveraging [skill] to drive [impact] across [industry].*

**Formatting:** one column · black and white · ≥10pt (and if you're at 10pt you have too much) · one page under ~10 years · bold keywords, best points in top third · professional file name.

**Four removals:** company locations · non-global tools (→ Western equivalents) · old/unrelated jobs · graduation dates.

**Gaps:** missing skill → list it, learn it this weekend, never claim mastery. Missing experience → Forage-style branded project / one Upwork client / side project, written as problem → skills → achievement.

**Apply if:** you match ~1/3 · you change title *or* industry, not both · you can speak to the first three requirements and the plain-language "anomaly" line.

**LinkedIn:** under 10 applicants · past 24h → edit URL `r86400` → `3600` · Open-to-Work off Friday, on Sunday · search "hiring [title]" → Posts, not Jobs.

**Core four:** applications (hardest box) · referrals (real = employee tells manager "interview them because XYZ") · cold pitch to decision makers (value prop + something specific + "let's talk") · learning in public. **Rule of twenty:** every 20 days without results, move 20% out of applications.

**Rejection:** reply within 24h to a real person; re-open the same thread when a role appears.

**Do:** estimate numbers · tailor to the manager's pain · apply within the first hour · DM the CEO after a rejection.
**Don't:** list responsibilities · lie about achievements · use a designed template · keep every job since graduation · reject yourself · hide in another course.
