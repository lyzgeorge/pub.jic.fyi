# Chapter 5 — Offers, Salary & Career Decisions

> This chapter covers the stretch of the job search that most candidates treat as a formality and that the coach treats as the highest-stakes conversation of the whole process: the salary question, the verbal and written offer, the reply email, and the decision between offers. His central claim is that salary negotiation is not a single moment at the end but a three-round game that starts the first time a recruiter asks "what's your expected salary?", and that "not negotiating" is "the biggest financial mistake that you could make in your entire life". He also argues, from his own experience as a hiring manager who grew teams from zero to over a hundred people, that companies expect you to negotiate, rarely withdraw an offer over a respectful ask, and pay top dollar to the people they believe are top candidates. The chapter then turns to what is actually in an offer — title, level, guaranteed versus variable pay, benefits — and how to choose between offers on what is durable rather than what is shiny.

**What you'll be able to do**

- Answer "what's your expected salary?" without giving a number first, and get the recruiter's range instead — including when they claim there is no budget.
- Recognise the three rounds of salary negotiation and the one phrase ("I accept") that kills your leverage at every round.
- Ask for the top of the range after an offer and close with the conditional "If you can make it X, I will sign".
- Use a competing offer, or your current job, as leverage while revealing only the "shiniest" component.
- Decode an offer letter: internal level, title, guaranteed base versus bonus/equity, clawbacks, insurance, vacation and real perks.
- Compare offers on what cannot be taken away, and investigate a future manager before you sign.
- Write the four offer replies — negotiate, delay, decline, accept — before you need them.

## 5.1 The mindset: salary talk is the start of a negotiation, not a hiring filter

**The concept.** Most candidates avoid talking about money because they fear sounding greedy or upsetting the interviewer. The coach's position is that this fear is misplaced: "There's no real risk. Salary talk isn't what gets you hired or rejected. It's just the start of a negotiation".

**The coach's reasoning — from the other side of the table.**

- *Recruiters are not trying to trick you.* The recruiter already has the pay bands and "isn't trying to scam you"; they need to tick the box "okay with our salary" for the hiring manager. HR is not lowballing you to pocket the savings; they want to avoid discovering after five rounds that you want $50K over budget.
- *Hiring managers like candidates who aim high.* "As a hiring manager, I really like candidates who want the most money because it tells me two things. First, that they're confident enough in their skills to ask for the max, and that they're ambitious. I mean, sure, I have a salary range, but it's not my money at the end of the day. And I know that top candidates want top dollar".
- *An offer means they think you are the top candidate.* "If you got the offer, the company believes that you are the top candidate, and top candidates deserve top compensation". "No company is gonna say, we like you the most, but if you ask for the max, then the deal's off, and we'll go with option B".
- *Most offers have room.* "Most offers have at least 10% room for negotiation, but offer negotiation is a game, and you need to know the rules to win".

**The cultural trap the coach names explicitly.** He has watched this "time and time again" in coaching: "Chinese professionals tend to think that this stage is non-negotiable. When an authority figure extends an offer, accepting gracefully is the respectful move, and pushing back feels inappropriate. But you will never have more leverage than you do right now, because their cards are all on the table. They've revealed that they want you". The same humility shows up in young candidates who say "money's not the most important thing to me, I just want to learn" — which undersells them.

**The scarcity trap.** A second failure mode comes from a long run of rejections. Candidate "M" had failed over 20 interviews and "was ready to take any job." Her skills were strong; her one red flag was opening her self-intro with "Hi, I'm M. I have five years of experience. And most recently, I was laid off." Once her storytelling was rebuilt she got the offer — and then "signed the offer right away," selling herself short. The coach's diagnosis: "all those rejections had taught her to believe that she was worth less than she actually is. That is the scarcity trap. When the fear of losing makes you forget what you deserve". A related story: a candidate with an Ivy League master's paid minimum wage at a small company, told repeatedly she was worth little, later received a verbal offer from a mainstream company at more than double. The reframe: "Your price does reflect your value. Make that the motivation to ask for what you deserve".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Rejection doesn't shrink your value, but believe me, scarcity will shrink your vision. Never let scarcity set your price."

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Not negotiating … Many people leave thousands on the table here by just accepting. But if you don't ask now, you'll work the next few years living with the regret that you could have, should have asked for more."

**Common mistakes**
- Treating the salary question as a test you can fail, and answering defensively.
- Signing on the spot out of relief or fear.
- Leading your self-intro with a loss (layoff, gap) — "The whole point of interviews is to focus on your strengths". (When a recruiter does ask about a gap, state it plainly — "I was laid off" — and stop; over-explaining signals you think it is a problem.)

## 5.2 Round one: "What's your expected salary?"

**The concept.** The salary conversation has three rounds — the expected-salary question, the verbal offer, and the written offer — and "round one starts the moment they ask, What is your expected salary?". In round one your job is to stay at the table and reach alignment through *their* number, not yours.

**Why not give a number.** "They already know how much they want to pay you … you don't. Speaking first is the fastest way to lose". "You don't know anything about your value in the market and what they're willing to pay. But guess who does? Your interviewer". Saying "my expectation is X" locks you into a low number you will regret.

**Why not refuse.** This is where the coach departs from the "classic advice: don't give them a number." "In reality, it's not so simple. Saying I prefer not to share can come across as uncooperative. And in reality, HR needs to know that the team can afford you before moving you on to the next round. So if they don't have that certainty, they could eliminate you". "They don't want to hear 'I don't know'". The recruiter's real question is simply "can I afford you?".

**The move: decline with a reason, then ask for their budget.** The coach frames this as the one "lie" you are allowed: "Don't lie about what you want; lie about why you can't tell them," then ask for the range. The reason you give — you are still determining mutual fit — does real work: "you're subtly shifting the balance of power to indicate that you don't feel pressured. You recognize that interviews are a two-way evaluation process, and you'll be evaluating them based on how good of a fit they are with you, salary included". "In one move, you're putting pressure on them to speak first, while framing it as a mutual agreement. That is sophisticated negotiation".

**The three-step script (the "only one right answer").** The coach's best-ever example came via his recruiter, Maddie, from a candidate three years out of school, an ex-management-consultant:

1. **Decline with a reason and ask for the budget.**
   > "Honestly, I'm not in a good position to understand that, because we haven't discussed what the team needs and how my skills can contribute. So my goal is to find out if we're a fit for each other first. Do you know what the budget for this role is?"

2. **If they claim there is no budget, ask for the range of people already in the role.** Maddie, who "likes to play hardball," replied: "Oh, we're still figuring out the budget for this role. I just need to write something down here. Can you give me something?" The coach: "Which is obviously a lie. If this role is posted, you better believe we know the pay scales by level and by band." The candidate stayed cool:
   > "Oh, no problem. Could you share a rough salary range for people who hold this position on this team already?"
   
   "That was a very reasonable question and hard to refuse," and she gave him a range. The same fallback appears elsewhere: if they say there is no budget, "they are also lying" — ask for the range of current holders of the title.

3. **Anchor to the top of the range, citing other roles under consideration.**
   > "That sounds about right to me. I definitely target the top of that range, which is more in line with other roles that I'm considering, and I'd be happy to move forward with that."

   Maddie's report afterwards: "John, this guy's sharp. I could tell just based on our salary conversation." What stood out: "he persistently gathered information" and "he aimed high".

**Equivalent deflection lines from other videos** (same structure — fit first, trust we'll land a number, ask their range):

> "Currently, I'm still gathering information to understand if we're a mutual fit. I believe your team has a better sense of the pay scale for this role than I do. Could you share your budget? I can tell you right away if we're in the same ballpark."

> "At the moment, I'm focused on determining a mutual fit. I'd be happy to share my desired salary at a later stage after understanding more. While we're on the topic though, do you mind sharing your budget?"

> "Right now I'm focused on finding the right fit. Once we're both confident about that, I trust that we'll land on a number that works. Since we're on the topic, what is the range budgeted for this role?" → if refused: "I understand. Could you share the rough range for employees who currently hold this title within your company?"

> To a warm, hyping HR "politician" type: "I hear you. Right now I'm focused on finding a mutual fit. But if you can share your range, I'll tell you if we're in the same ballpark."

> The one-sentence version for a phone screen, once you know their band: "I am aligned with the upper end of your range and would be happy to discuss specifics once we've identified a mutual fit."

**If they still pressure you for a number.** Give a range with an explicit reservation to revise:

> "Based on my limited understanding at this point, I believe a range of X to Y is reasonable, but I would be able to give you a more specific number later on in the process."

"This ensures that you move the process forward without being too difficult while reserving the right to change your mind later".

**The companion question: "Are you interviewing elsewhere?"** This belongs to round one because it sets up your leverage later. The recruiter is really asking "do we have competition and should we move fast?" Chinese humility ("no, you're the only one") hands over all leverage. The coach's answer: "I'm in the early stages of my search and I'm pursuing a few promising opportunities. That said, this is my top choice because of X reason." "Pursuing a few promising opportunities" can simply mean you applied to Google.

**Common mistakes**
- "My expectation is X" — locks you low.
- "I don't have an expectation" / "I prefer not to share" — uncooperative; you may be eliminated.
- "Money's not the most important thing, I just want to learn".
- Cracking under a second push. "I know even I might crack and I might give a number under pressure" — the answer is the *second* question, not a number.
- Telling the recruiter they are your only process.

## 5.3 Rounds two and three: the verbal offer, the written offer, and the conditional close

![Offer stages: check range alignment, negotiate a verbal offer, verify the written terms, then accept.](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-5/01-flowchart-offer-stages.png)

**The concept.** Once you have an offer, the game changes: "ask for the max" and then close with "I will sign if". The one rule across all three rounds is never to say "I accept" until you mean it as the final word.

### Round two — the verbal offer call

HR calls. "They sound warm, excited, happy for you. So nice, right? No. They're calling you to align on several things. And one of the most important things is salary." You will hear something like: "So I know you said that you wanted a hundred K earlier. It looks like we can give you 95K. Is that okay?" What you do not say is "yes, that's okay".

Instead, hold a range:

> "I'm excited about this offer. Having learned more about the scope of this role through the process and given [your strongest value], I think the right range for me is X to Y. I want to make sure we set us both up for success."

"The range is intentional here, because they will likely start off the offer near the bottom, and then with your range, you've just given yourself room to negotiate further".

### Asking for the max

"I know this is a bold approach, but no company is gonna say, we like you the most, but if you ask for the max, then the deal's off … Companies know that they need to pay top dollar for top talent. And if you got that offer, they think you're top talent. Now, I'm not saying that you should expect to always get the max, but you should always ask for it at the start of negotiations". The negotiation email itself should "use your experience and market data and confidence in one email to name your number. This short email has earned many of my candidates over ten thousand dollars more".

### Round three — the written offer and the conditional close

"The wrong phrase here is, 'of course, I accept.' … If at any of the three stages of negotiation you say, I accept, you just kill your negotiation power. Because HR will take that number right back to the team and align on it. And if you try to raise that number later, it looks like you're negotiating in bad faith because you went back on your word".

Why you have nothing to lose here: "They won't withdraw the offer that they've worked so hard to put together as long as you're negotiating respectfully and in good faith. And in fact, they expect you to negotiate here. So if you've given a range in round two, this is the time to push for the higher end of that range".

**The coach's own failure, and the line he should have used.** Early in his career, going for "what felt like my dream role," he asked HR, "hey, any chance you can go higher?" The HR paused, "almost sounded like he was sighing," and said: "Well, I suppose I could go back, though the approval process is long. And let me tell you, I'm gonna have to tell your future manager that you want more money." The coach's analysis: "He was creating urgency here, right? Making me feel like asking for more was wasting everyone's time, energy, their goodwill, maybe even annoying enough to threaten my offer. And it worked. Like I just jumped to fill that silence. And I was like, no, no, no, it's okay. I'm fine with the current number".

What he should have said:

> "If you can make it X, I will sign."

"That one line pushes the urgency onto them. It shows that you're serious about this and just one small step away. And they will hustle when they know that they're close". The same close appears in two other videos: "chances are the company will try to negotiate you down from the max. So here's my favorite trick to lock in a few extra grand in the final rounds of negotiation. We use the phrase, I will sign if. This signals that you're close to agreeing and HR will hustle to meet your final ask"; and "If we can agree on this number, I will sign".

**Worked case.** A candidate "made a mistake early in her interviews by lowballing herself. She regretted this later when she got the offer." The coach drafted her recruiter messages — first asking for the max, then "I will sign if" — and "even after her early mistakes, she walked away with 10 grand more per year using this strategy".

**The three-round summary, in the coach's words:** "Round one, stay at the table. Round two, hold your range. Round three, hold them by the balls. That is how you win the game".

**Common mistakes**
- "Yes, that's okay" on the verbal call.
- "Of course, I accept" on the written offer — or "I accept" at any round before the final number.
- Asking an open question ("any chance you can go higher?") that lets HR fill the silence with pressure, instead of naming the specific number you will sign at.
- Filling the silence yourself after HR sighs.

## 5.4 Leverage: competing offers, your current job, and knowing when "we can't go higher" is true

**The concept.** "Your greatest leverage will always be a competing offer" — and if you are employed, your current job counts as one. The corollary for people juggling processes: "Don't make the company that actually wants you wait longer. Make the company that doesn't want you yet hurry up".

**The hardest negotiation the coach ever faced (from the hiring manager's side).** A candidate told him during the process that she was interviewing with Meta and Amazon. "Was she lying? I don't know. But I believed her because I could tell that she knew her stuff." He compressed two future rounds into a single superday "just for her," then told HR: "she's a superstar, so let's offer her close to the top of her salary band, get on a call with her, and try to get her to sign the offer today." She did not sign. "She told us that she also received a competing offer and she was considering staying put in her current company as well." He went to HR "to ask for an exceptional increase above our budget … I had to provide all of this documentation to explain why she deserved this exceptional increase beyond the defined pay band." She signed for 15% more.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Nothing motivates a company faster than the fear of losing you. And a competing offer does that instantly."

**The three rules he draws from it**
1. **Leverage = a competing offer, and being employed counts.** "Your current job signals, hey, I'm not desperate. If you don't pay me well, I'm just gonna stay here."
2. **Don't reveal the full competing offer or your current comp; highlight the shiniest component.** "That savvy candidate didn't share everything about the other offer. She just shared that the salary band was higher, the base salary, right? And that's what's important to her. Who knows? Maybe our total comp was actually higher, but that got us to move on the base salary for her."
3. **When close, say: "If we can agree on this number, I will sign."**

**The limit of leverage.** "If she had asked for any more than that, I would have had to pass on her and go to our option B candidate. Remember, if they tell you we can't go any higher, sometimes that's not a bluff. It might actually be the truth". This is the counterweight to "ask for the max": ask for the top, but recognise a genuine ceiling when you have already pushed above band.

**Using an offer to speed up your preferred company.** The common dilemma: "You have one offer, but you're still interviewing with this other company that you like more, and you're torn between saying yes too soon and missing your shot with your top choice. But if you wait the wrong way, you could lose both offers." The answer is to tell the undecided company "I have an offer." "If they like you, they will speed up. I've even seen companies skip entire rounds to accelerate a decision. And the best part is you also get to increase your value instantly through social proof because now you're a valuable candidate that another company wants".

**Interview skill converts to money.** "Interviewing well and being able to communicate and tell great stories, that is a skill that doesn't just pay off in more offers, it pays off in higher paying offers too". And "the more offers you have, the more leverage you have to negotiate them".

**Leverage inside your current company.** The same mechanism drives internal promotions. The coach's managers only offered him promotion when he had an outside offer: "another company has to discover your value first before your own company will suddenly recognize it." When asked "John, is there anything we can do to make you stay?" his reply was "Maybe getting me that promotion that I've been asking for." His general rule: go back to the market roughly every two years and "never let one company decide my pace".

**Common mistakes**
- Signing "today" because HR asks you to; "Congrats, you got the offer. Can you sign today? Not so fast".
- Disclosing your entire competing package or current salary, which lets them match the weakest part.
- Making the company that wants you wait while you chase the one that hasn't decided.
- Pushing past a real ceiling and losing the offer to option B.

## 5.5 Reading the offer: title and level, direct comp, indirect comp

**The concept.** "You need to understand just three parts of your offer to avoid making a bad decision": title/level, direct compensation, indirect compensation. A second video slices the same territory into six dimensions — total comp, level, title, insurance, vacation, perks — with the same priority rule: "always lock down the big stuff first, like comp, title, level," then perks. Both are consolidated below.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "The company spent weeks interviewing you before sending that offer. So you are allowed to spend a few days evaluating it."

### Part 1 — Title and level

- **Check the title against the JD.** "I've seen companies post one title in a job description and an entirely different one in the offer letter. It happens more than you think. And once you sign that contract, it is formal on your resume, on LinkedIn, forever". "If the job title on your offer doesn't match the job description, ask why".
- **Ask for the internal level.** "Ask HR, what internal level does this role map to? Because at large companies, levels determine your salary band and your comparative level at other companies". A cautionary case: a candidate "interviewed for a senior researcher role. She didn't read the offer carefully, ended up signing on at the entry level. Now she's stuck. And devastated, and she is not the first candidate that I've seen getting fooled like this".
- **Get promises in writing.** "If anyone promises you we'll start you lower, but we'll promote you in a year, ask them to put it in writing. They won't".
- **Title is negotiable.** "Most people don't ask because they're afraid that it'll seem like too much. But I negotiated my own title once. The company agreed, and that one conversation changed my entire career trajectory". "In the long run, it could be even more valuable than a salary bump".
- **The red flag.** "If anyone on that team ever tells you, ah, titles don't matter here, take note of that red flag. Titles always matter. After you leave, you have to live with that title on your resume. They don't".

### Part 2 — Direct compensation (base, bonus, equity)

"Your base salary is the only component that is legally guaranteed. And everything else is variable. They build the offer to look as big as possible by mixing income that you'll definitely receive with income that might never materialize".

**Bonus — three questions to HR**
> "How consistently has this bonus been paid out over the past few years? Is any portion guaranteed or is it fully performance based? And what percentage of employees at my level typically receive the full amount?"

"Their answers are gonna tell you everything. So if they hedge or get vague, that is a signal." The blunter version: "confirm the target percentage, criteria, and historical payout rates. Hint, if they say it's discretionary, assume it's zero".

**Sign-on, relocation and location bonuses.** They are negotiable — "I've helped candidates negotiate relocation bonuses, signing bonuses worth up to 40k" — but "most companies claw it back if you leave within a year". "This can turn from a bonus into a pair of handcuffs real quick".

**Equity (RSUs, options).** Ask: "What is the vesting schedule? Is there a cliff? When does the first tranche vest? When do you get a refresh grant?" Then "back out the annual dollar value, not just the total grant number, and … discount it based on your own confidence in the company, not what the recruiter tells you". Two personal warnings: a recruiter once told him "with full confidence that their stock was only going up. It dropped over 70% from the peak very shortly after I signed, and the vast majority of my equity was just wiped out"; another recruiter said "We pay less, but our stock has 10x in the past few years. Imagine how much more it can grow. … A few months later, that stock had dropped by fifty percent and it still hasn't recovered". "HR and the hiring manager will often use equity and RSUs to lure you into the offer. So think for yourself".

### Part 3 — Indirect compensation

"Everything that doesn't show up under that salary number, but it can still easily add up to tens of thousands of dollars a year. Most people skip this section because they're just fixated on the cash, and that is a mistake".

- **Retirement matching.** "401k matching, because this is free money, almost as reliable as your base salary". "A few percent can mean thousands of dollars a year".
- **Insurance.** "Not all policies are equal." Ask what is actually covered: mental health, dental, fertility, health spending accounts. His wife's previous employer covered fertility treatment, full mental-health spending and a generous health spending account; "in her last year, she used about thirty thousand dollars in insurance benefits".
- **Vacation.** "More is better. But be careful if you see the words unlimited vacation. It is never unlimited. In fact, studies have shown that employees in those companies take less vacation on average." The question to ask: "How much vacation did you personally take last year?" — "That answer tells you more than any policy doc".
- **Real perks.** "Not a ping pong table or a stocked kitchen." Examples from his own jobs: Uber gave $300/month for rides or Uber Eats; Shopify a $2,000 baby bonus; another company $5,000/year for personal development; another an extra day off every long weekend, "which adds up to like two weeks of vacation". "You won't find many of these written in the offer, but they can make a difference".

**Sequence.** "Lock down the big stuff first, like level, title, and comp and then talk about perks".

**Common mistakes**
- Not reading the level on the offer letter.
- Counting a discretionary bonus or a recruiter's equity story as income.
- Getting excited about a sign-on bonus before reading the clawback.
- Taking "unlimited vacation" at face value.
- Accepting a verbal "we'll promote you in a year".

## 5.6 Choosing between offers: what can't be taken away, and who you'll work for

![Compare offers by role and skills, guaranteed pay, manager and autonomy, and sustainability; name the benefit, trade-off and open question.](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-5/02-framework-offer-decision.png)

**The concept.** "Smart candidates don't chase offers. They choose them". Two lenses from the corpus: which attributes of an offer are *durable*, and who the manager really is.

### Lens 1 — Durable versus revocable: why the coach prioritises title

Between comparable companies, the coach says he "mostly chased title," and admits this may have been "very short-sighted," but explains the logic honestly: "title is one of those things that nobody can take back. You can pursue a job for the work-life balance, but suddenly the company can struggle. And then they can ask their employees to work overtime. That goes away. You could have an amazing manager that you love, a team that you love, and a restructuring could tear that away from you. That has happened to me before. And I've learned that the hard way".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Title is something that cannot be taken away from you. When it's on your resume, it's on your resume."

The second half of his argument is that the other things follow: "as I gain higher and higher titles, I have the opportunity to grow. That comes with a title. I have the salary. I have the chances to learn new skills and test myself in new ways. So all the other nice things that I look for in a career, the meaning and the interesting things, they come with a title". This is consistent with the offer-reading rule that a title negotiation "could be even more valuable than a salary bump".

**The implicit test:** for each attribute of an offer — title, salary, manager, team, work-life balance — ask whether it could be lost within a year through no action of your own.

### Lens 2 — Investigate the manager before you sign

"No signal is certain. But relying on instinct alone is not a strategy." "Don't prepare to impress. Prepare to investigate". Three moments:

1. **Recruiter screen.** Ask: "What's the leadership style of the hiring manager and what kind of person tends to work best with them?" Then listen for the answer's *shape*: "hesitation is your information" — pauses, hedging, vagueness are warning signs. The coach's worst manager was foreshadowed by a recruiter saying the manager is "really results-driven and will call you out on small mistakes".
2. **Interview Q&A.** Ask the manager a behavioral question — the same technique they use on you — and watch whether they struggle, go vague, or light up with specifics. For micromanagement: "Can you share a time when you delegated significant responsibility and trusted your team member to deliver?" For development: "Can you tell me about the last time you supported someone's professional growth?" "Don't listen to what people say. Watch what they do".
3. **Post-offer backchannel.** Find people on LinkedIn who reported to that manager and have since left — they have less to lose:
   > "Hi [name], I recently received an offer to join [team] under [manager]. I'd really appreciate your honest perspective on the team culture and leadership style. I completely understand if you'd prefer not to share, but any insight would mean a lot as I make this important career decision."

**Common mistakes**
- Choosing on work-life balance or a beloved team as if they were permanent.
- Ignoring the recruiter's hesitation when describing the manager.
- Deciding by instinct alone without a single backchannel conversation.

## 5.7 The reply: negotiate, delay, decline, accept

**The concept.** "Got the offer? Yay! Now, don't blow it with your reply." There are four offer situations, and "how you reply decides how you get more" — as well as your leverage with other companies and your reputation on day one. The coach's practical instruction is to draft all four now, so you are not writing them under pressure.

| Situation | What the reply must do | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Negotiate** | Name your number in one short email, backed by "your experience and market data and confidence." Has earned candidates "over ten thousand dollars more." Never reply with "I accept" until the number is final. | |
| **Delay** (you want another company more) | Don't stall the company that wants you; write to the undecided company: "I have an offer." Companies "skip entire rounds to accelerate," and you gain social proof. | |
| **Decline** | Never ghost. "One careless rejection email can leave a poor impression, and the work world is a small one." Thank them, give a clear reason, leave the door open — "You'll be surprised how often they will come back to you." | |
| **Accept** | "Your reply is your first impression for your new role." Lead with gratitude, confirm your yes, show excitement. "Short, confident, future focused" — three lines. | |

**A note on the decline.** The graceful-rejection structure is "how you say no without ending the relationship". Given that the coach also recommends returning to the market every two years, the company you decline today is a plausible employer in a future cycle.

**A note on the accept.** "Get it wrong, and you could set the wrong tone for the entire onboarding phase". The accept email is only sent *after* the conditional close has been met — once you have said "If you can make it X, I will sign" and they have made it X.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Don't just sign the offer. Sign your name to the kind of career that you are gonna build for yourself."

**Common mistakes**
- Accepting immediately out of relief.
- Ghosting the company you turn down.
- A long, hedging or apologetic acceptance instead of three confident lines.

## Practice set

### Drills

**Drill 1 — The round-one role-play (5.2).** Work with a partner who plays a hardball recruiter. Their lines: (a) "What's your expected salary?" (b) "We're still figuring out the budget for this role. I just need to write something down. Can you give me something?" (c) *gives a range*. You must (1) decline with a reason and ask for the budget, (2) ask for the range of current role-holders, (3) anchor to the top citing other roles. Rule: you may not give a number before step 3. Repeat until the partner refuses twice and you still don't crack.

**Drill 2 — The pressure fallback (5.2).** Same partner now refuses three times and says "I need a number to move you forward." Deliver the reservation-range line ("Based on my limited understanding at this point, I believe a range of X to Y is reasonable, but…") with real numbers for your target role.

**Drill 3 — Three-round rehearsal (5.3).** Partner plays HR across all three rounds: the expected-salary question, a warm verbal offer ("It looks like we can give you 95K, is that okay?"), and a written offer followed by a sigh and "the approval process is long, and I'll have to tell your future manager you want more money." Constraints: never say "I accept," never give a first number, hold a range in round two, and end with "If you can make it X, I will sign." Have the partner deliberately leave a long silence after the sigh; your job is not to fill it.

**Drill 4 — Write the negotiation email (5.3, 5.4).** For a pending or hypothetical offer, write the three-part reply: (1) a leverage statement (competing offer or current job), (2) one highlighted comp component only (e.g., "the base salary band in my other offer is higher"), (3) the close "If we can agree on this number, I will sign." Keep it short.

**Drill 5 — "I will sign if" in two sentences (5.3).** Write a two-sentence email to a recruiter naming the one specific concession you would trade for signing today (base, sign-on, title, start date, vacation).

**Drill 6 — "Interviewing elsewhere?" (5.2).** Write your one-sentence answer using the "pursuing a few promising opportunities … this is my top choice because of X" structure; say it aloud until it sounds conversational rather than rehearsed.

**Drill 7 — Offer scorecard (5.5).** Build a one-page scorecard with three sections — Title/Level, Direct comp, Indirect comp — and list every HR question from 5.5 beneath each. Fill it in for your current or last offer and mark each line **G** (guaranteed) or **V** (variable). Total the G column: that is your real floor.

**Drill 8 — Six-row comparison table (5.5).** For two or more offers (real or hypothetical), build a table with rows: total comp, level, title, insurance, vacation, perks. The equity cells must contain vesting schedule, cliff, and refresh policy; the bonus cell must contain target %, criteria and historical payout. Any cell you can't fill is a question to email the recruiter today.

**Drill 9 — Durable vs revocable (5.6).** For two offers, list each attribute (title, salary, manager, team, work-life balance, perks) and mark which could be lost within a year through no action of your own. Compare only the surviving rows.

**Drill 10 — Investigate the manager (5.6).** Write two behavioral questions for a hiring manager targeting the two traits you most fear (e.g., micromanagement, no development). Then identify two former reports of that manager on LinkedIn and send the backchannel message.

**Drill 11 — Draft the four emails now (5.7).** Write your negotiate, delay ("I have an offer"), decline (thank / reason / door open) and accept (gratitude / confirm / excitement, three lines) emails as templates before you have an offer in hand.

**Drill 12 — Self-intro scarcity audit (5.1).** Record your current 30-second self-intro. Strike any sentence that leads with a loss (layoff, gap, failure) and rewrite it to lead with a strength. Then write one sentence stating the number you will ask for on your next offer and why.

## Cheat sheet

**The three rounds**
| Round | Trigger | Never say | Do say |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "What's your expected salary?" | "My expectation is X" / "I don't have one" / "I prefer not to share" | Fit first → ask their budget → if refused, ask range of current role-holders → target the top |
| 2 | Verbal offer call ("95K, is that okay?") | "Yes, that's okay" | "I'm excited… the right range for me is X to Y" |
| 3 | Written offer | "Of course, I accept" / "Any chance you can go higher?" | "If you can make it X, I will sign" |

**Formulas**
- Deflect = fit first + trust we'll land a number + "what's the budget?"
- Post-offer = ask for the max → "I will sign if…"
- Leverage reply = competing offer (or current job) + shiniest component only + "If we can agree on this number, I will sign"
- Offer = Title/Level + Direct comp (base G; bonus/equity V) + Indirect comp (401k, insurance, vacation, real perks). Lock down level, title, comp first
- Choose = what can't be taken away (title) + who the manager really is
- Reply = Negotiate / Delay ("I have an offer") / Decline (thank, reason, door open) / Accept (gratitude, confirm, excitement)

**Do**
- Get their number before yours; ask twice if you have to
- Ask for the top of the range — "top candidates want top dollar"
- Ask "what internal level does this map to?" and read the level on the letter
- Ask HR "how much vacation did you personally take last year?"
- Get "we'll promote you in a year" in writing
- Take a few days to evaluate — they took weeks to interview you
- Backchannel former reports of your future manager

**Don't**
- Sign on the spot
- Say "I accept" before the final number
- Reveal your full competing package or current comp
- Count discretionary bonus or recruiter-hyped equity as income
- Believe "titles don't matter here"
- Ghost a company you decline
- Push past a genuine ceiling once you're already above band

**Lines to keep**
- "Never let scarcity set your price."
- "You will never have more leverage than you do right now."
- "Nothing motivates a company faster than the fear of losing you."
- "After you leave, you have to live with that title on your resume. They don't."
- "Smart candidates don't chase offers. They choose them."
