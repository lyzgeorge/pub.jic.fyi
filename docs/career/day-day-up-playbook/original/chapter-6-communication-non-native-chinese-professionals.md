# Chapter 6 - Communication for Non-Native & Chinese Professionals

> This chapter is about the gap between what you know and how you are heard. The coach's recurring diagnosis, drawn from a decade of hiring, managing a 500-person data organization and coaching Chinese professionals in North America, is that the feedback "stronger communication" or "poor communication skills" is almost never about English. It is about structure (you give context first and the conclusion last), calibration (you hedge what you are sure of and over-claim what you are not), voice (flat, quiet, rising at the end), and a cultural default of deference (sorry, just, try, "hard worker") that Western managers read as "not ready to lead." His thesis: "Stop competing in English, start competing in clarity". Each lecture below takes one of these gaps, explains why the coach says it costs you, gives the method, and shows the exact words he uses.

**What you'll be able to do**

- Triage which conversations need careful English and which need fluent-but-imperfect English, and stop exhausting yourself on the wrong ones.
- Deliver any update, answer or recommendation conclusion-first using SCQA, the scissor method, the so-what sandwich or AIRBUS.
- State a recommendation without hedging, and separately calibrate claims about people, truth and events so you sound accurate rather than aggressive.
- Remove "sorry," "just" and "try" from your workplace English and replace them with the coach's substitutions.
- Turn the three vocal dials (tone, speed, volume), end statements with a falling pitch, and use pauses instead of fillers.
- Speak up in fast meetings using the three-level ladder, the recap scripts and the meeting-host phrase set.
- Translate Chinese virtues ("hard worker," "team player," "responsible") into the traits Western managers actually reward, and ask for help, push back and decline without sounding weak.
- Answer "How are you?" and run office small talk with prepared openers, "ask for the best," and the therapist-derived connection playbook.

## 6.1 The real gap is not English - clarity, comprehension and the System 1 switch

### The concept

The coach's starting point is blunt: after ten years of studying English, a few more months of grammar will not change your outcome, and competing with native speakers on fluency "is just not worth it". Hiring managers do not grade English unless the job requires it; they judge whether you think clearly and speak logically. He offers a motto for the ESL professional: "I know my English bad. No problem. You understand me, so let's go".

The reverse mistake is trying to sound polished. "Polish serves the speaker, not the audience. The most effective communication isn't impressive, it's understandable". Jargon creeps in ("let's talk later" becomes "take it offline, circle back, close the loop"), and the coach argues a limited vocabulary is a superpower because simple words plus strong logic guarantee everyone understands.

### Why it works (the coach's reasoning)

1. **Your brain is overwhelmed, not slow.** Citing research by Volk and colleagues, the coach says non-native speakers are "cognitively depleted faster and more often," which amplifies job strain. Using Kahneman's two systems, he observes that native speakers live in fast, automatic System 1 and reserve slow System 2 for high-stakes moments (presentations, manager conversations). ESL professionals do the opposite: they run everything in System 2 to keep grammar perfect, so the brain is always tired. His own freeze came during a Chinese-language presentation at Peking University, where he sounded "like a grade one kid" because he was translating and worrying about wrong words.
2. **Monotone is a symptom of over-editing.** Expressionless delivery "isn't bad speaking - it's over-dedication to grammar leaving no bandwidth for tone, pace, expression, and reading the room". (Lecture 6.5 fixes the voice; this lecture frees the bandwidth.)
3. **Comprehension beats conciseness.** Interviewers already carry cognitive load from your accent and grammar. "You can be boring, you can be nervous, heck, you can even answer poorly, but you cannot be incomprehensible". A data analyst's AI-polished "greatest achievement" answer was understood only ~60% by the coach; the content, not the accent, was the problem.
4. **If you don't understand them, that is not your fault.** A people-analytics client asked "Am I just slow?" after her colleague talked about "seeing the forest from the trees... noodle on ideas... double click on the rigor." The coach: "If someone speaks and you don't understand them, that is not your fault. They need to communicate clear".

### The method

**A. Triage every communication (System 1 vs System 2)**
1. Before speaking or writing, ask: *"Does my performance depend on communicating carefully here?"*
2. If yes (presentation, recommendation to a manager, a written decision): use System 2 deliberately - slow down, structure (Lecture 6.2).
3. If no (the ~80% of low-stakes talk: hallway, chat messages, quick questions): switch to System 1 and be imperfect. "It's better to be fluent with flawed grammar than stuck and silent."

**B. Build System 1 with "10x daily practice"** (developed with a speech therapist)
1. Pick ten moments per day (walking to lunch, elevator, groceries, each phone pickup).
2. In each, speak English as clearly and naturally as you can for 30–60 seconds - no pausing, no grammar or pronunciation checks.
3. Tie each to a fixed trigger so it does not slip.
4. Keep it under ten minutes a day for 30 days; the coach says a month yields dramatic fluency gains.

**C. Keep it comprehensible**
1. Paste any prepared script into a Flesch–Kincaid grade calculator.
2. If it reads above grade 8 (grade 16 = university graduate), rewrite in shorter words and sentences until it sits at grade 5–8 spoken English. "Concise doesn't mean simple."
3. Test it aloud on a listener.

**D. Ask for clarity, then provide it**
- Ask: "Can you expand on that?" / "Could you rephrase that?" - "if you don't understand, someone else probably doesn't either... speaking up itself demonstrates leadership."
- Provide: restate what they seem to be asking → confirm → propose specific changes → state effort/timeline → ask for a decision.

### Worked example - providing clarity to "HR Lady"

> "It sounds like you're asking for more story to explain technical details to a non-technical audience. Is that right? Great. Well, then I propose two changes. First, we'll create a key insight title for each slide, and second, we'll add an executive summary so leaders will know what needs to change from the very first slide. These changes will take me two days. Are you okay with meeting in two days to review it again?"

🗣 **Coach's words:** "Your brain isn't slow in your second language. It's overwhelmed."

🗣 **Coach's words:** "Stop letting English kill your confidence at work... simple is best."

### Common mistakes

- Running every Slack message through System 2 (the coach's wife asked him to check every message until ChatGPT replaced him).
- Making simple ideas more confusing to "compete" with jargon-heavy colleagues.
- Asking AI to make an answer "concise" without strong English, which produces "a university essay, definitely not real spoken English". (See 6.2, part E, for the coach's rules on AI.)
- Nodding through jargon you did not understand instead of asking for a rephrase.

## 6.2 Sound like a leader, not an intern - conclusion-first structures

![Scissor method: replace a tangled status history with the main point, cause, and decision request.](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-6/01-comparison-scissor-update.png)

### The concept

The coach's own turning point was a manager's note after a VP presentation: "John, you're smart. But we need to work on your executive communication." On his team, unstructured talk made the manager pull out his phone and pretend to scroll. The principle: "Leaders don't care about your vocabulary. They care whether your ideas are clear, structured, and help them to make decisions faster... Stop trying to impress. Start helping them decide".

The cultural root he names is the Chinese "pu dian" habit - context first, conclusion last - which frustrates Western audiences. Most people, including natives, "speak in the order they think," which produces panic, freezing and rambling. Every structure below forces the main point to the front.

### Why it works

- **Memory is beginning-and-end.** "Think about the last movie that you watched. You remember the beginning. You remember how it ended for sure. But what about everything in the middle? Blurry". People lose ~50% of new information within an hour and ~90% within a week, and working memory holds 3–4 chunks, hence the rule of three.
- **"Be more concise" means they stopped listening a while ago.** Conciseness "is not a writing skill. It is a decision-making skill" - the Cambridge definition hides two jobs, deciding what needs to be said and cutting words, and almost everyone (second-language speakers most of all) jumps to cutting, which "just makes you incomplete".
- **Everything at work chases alignment or action.** Great thinking "starts in their head": what's in it for them, the one thing they need to know, what they need to do.

### The method - four structures, one rule

**A. SCQA (McKinsey), for status updates and answers to leaders**
1. **Situation** - where things stand.
2. **Complication** - what changed / the issue.
3. **Question** - the decision to be made.
4. **Answer** - your recommendation and why.

> Director: "Is your project on track for Q4?"
> Rambling: scope creep, late nights, maybe we reprioritize... (the director leaves uneasy).
> SCQA: "We're off track for Q4 delivery. The issue is that several new features were added mid cycle. So we need to decide whether to reduce scope or add more resources. My recommendation is to reallocate headcount temporarily because..."

**B. The scissor method, for written or spoken updates**
1. Open with **"The main thing is..."** - blocks opening with context; forces you to decide what matters to them.
2. One line of cause.
3. Close with **"The reason I'm telling you is..."** - blocks ending on information; forces an action for them.
4. Only then cut words.

> Client Bing, before: "So we had this vendor issue in week two, and then the data migration took longer than expected because the schema didn't match... and QA found a few things too... I think we're mostly okay, but the timeline might move a bit."
> After: "The main thing is we're two weeks behind and the launch date is at risk. The cause was the vendor delay in week two. The reason I'm telling you this is that I'd like your input on whether we hold the date and cut the reporting feature or move the date."

**C. The so-what sandwich + an ask, for meeting contributions**
1. So what (the destination).
2. Supporting points.
3. So what again.
4. End with a clear ask - "What do you need from this room?" As a manager the coach answered every status update with "Thanks for the update. So how can I help?" because without a request the room has nothing to engage with.

> Before: "It wasn't easy because we had lots of dirty data, but I looked at the numbers and the referral clients tend to retain longer... so maybe we should try..."
> After: "We should stop prioritizing ads and focus on referrals. Referral clients retain longer and onboard faster. That's the customer segment we should focus on." Then: "I'd love to hear thoughts from the room, can we move forward?" (not "So that's it.")

**D. AIRBUS, when your mind goes blank on an unexpected question**
- **A**cknowledge the question.
- **I**dentify why it matters.
- **R**econfirm what they're really asking.
- **B**roadcast your main point up front.
- **U**npack your proof.
- **S**ummarize the key takeaway.
The first three steps buy time to structure; the fourth forces the conclusion first.

> Boss: "What's the campaign budget?"
> "Thanks for checking in. I know good budgeting drives good ROI. You want to know about upfront CapEx only, is that right? Okay, great. I'd recommend starting small around 50K. That covers the essentials for launch and leaves some budget for later if we want to iterate or scale. For this phase, 50K is the sweet spot."

**E. Write it yourself; let AI fix only grammar**
AI-written English "is obvious to everyone but you" - the coach's wife cringed at a Chinese travel agent's AI message that he, a "CSL" (Chinese-as-a-second-language) speaker, could not see as slop; the reverse happens to you. Using AI to compose is "performing competence instead of building it," and "the skill you lack is the same skill needed to evaluate the output." Steps:
1. Answer three questions: What am I trying to say? What do I want this person to do? What do I want them to feel or know?
2. Write it in your own English, mistakes and all: "I think we should delay the launch. The data is not ready. Can we discuss Thursday?"
3. Let AI fix grammar, word choice and tone only, using the fencing prompt (Templates).
4. Out-loud test: "Would I have naturally said this sentence out loud to this person's face?" Rewrite failures yourself. Editing check: "What new information does this add? None? Delete it."
Three AI-slop signs to strip: "It's not X, it's Y" (just say Y); triplets ("streamline, empower, drive" - "you're a humble worker, not a politician"); important-sounding empty sentences ("translate emerging technology into scalable commercial opportunity" vs. "bring tech to market").

**F. For presentations: GPS**
A presentation is for the audience, not the presenter (the coach's P&G manager called his intern deck "a waste of time for everyone in the room"). **G**uide - one sentence they must remember (leadership: decision first, bottom line up front, an executive summary that works even if they leave after slide one). **P**ath - three milestones. **S**upport - one idea per slide, slide title = the conclusion ("Margin trending upward in Q4 driven by higher-profit customer segments," not "Q4 performance analysis"), Guy Kawasaki's 10-20-30 rule. Design "is a vanity game"; he spent 99 of 100 hours on content.

🗣 **Coach's words:** "If you can't distill your main message into a single sentence, you don't have a main message."

🗣 **Coach's words:** "The performers stay perfectly polished and perfectly invisible... The builders sound ESL for a few months, then they sound like leaders."

### Common mistakes

- Journey without destination: walking through every detail while "they were getting lost behind you".
- Cutting words before deciding the message, which produces incompleteness, not concision.
- Ending on information with no ask ("So that's it.").
- Polished AI emails your boss leaves unread; scripts that produce an awkward silence when read aloud.

## 6.3 Calibrating claims - commit to your recommendation, don't claim more than you know

![A precise recommendation links an evidence limit, a directional signal, and a next step.](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-6/02-framework-calibrated-recommendation.png)

### The concept

This lecture holds two ideas that look contradictory until you see they are about different objects. On **your own recommendation**, the coach says stop hedging: "leaders don't present perfect answers, they present decisions". On **claims about other people, about the truth, or about what happened**, he says never claim more than you know - "accuracy is not softness". Confidence about your direction; precision about your evidence and about others.

### Part A - Commit to the recommendation

**Why hedging costs you.** A Chinese FP&A manager seeking a director promotion was told to improve "leadership communication." In meetings she said: "I recommend targeting a five percent reduction in employee expenses. But of course, if we do that, here are some things that could go wrong." The coach: "that is a moment when she lost credibility... you think that you're being thorough, but actually, you're giving people reasons to doubt you. You're opening the door to a debate, and your opponent is yourself". The room hears uncertainty the moment "but" appears.

The root cause he names: Chinese education systems "where correctness was everything," so hedging reduces the chance of being wrong. That habit "starts working against you" at senior levels, where "there is rarely a right answer" - only trade-offs, incomplete data, competing priorities. As a director at Shopify he made recommendations to the CTO, some wrong, but "the greatest risk wasn't making mistakes now and then. The greatest risk was allowing uncertainty to paralyze us" - and eventually his recommendation built a 100% data engineering team that positioned the company early in AI production. Hedging words (try, maybe, hopefully, I think) get people rated less competent, less confident, less ready to lead; "uncertainty kills careers faster than being occasionally wrong".

**The recommendation formula**
1. State the recommendation.
2. Give the basis: "Based on X and Y..."
3. State the payoff / metric.
4. **Pause. Let them react.**
5. If challenged - "good. They're doing their job" - show the risk analysis you prepared, only when asked.

> Weak: "I recommend targeting a five percent reduction in employee expenses. But of course, if we do that, here are some things that could go wrong."
> Strong: "My recommendation is a five percent reduction in employee expenses. Based on X and Y, this gives us the fastest path to margin improvement." (pause)
> Variant: "My recommendation is to prioritize feature A. Because of X and Y, this gives us the fastest path to improve metric Z." (pause)

**The commitment sentence.** Before speaking, finish "My understanding is..." in your head and lead with it. "You don't need to be 100% sure to be 100% confident" - an executive the coach worked with needed only 30% certainty to speak with full confidence, "and I believe that is why she grew to an executive level". Never open with "I might be wrong here, but maybe..." because "the moment they hear the first part, they will agree with you".

**Report → Advise → Commit.** Reporting facts is where most Chinese professionals live and where AI now competes; advising is a step up but someone else still carries the risk; committing is "make the call and stand behind it." Habit line: "I'm seeing [insight]. And if it were my call, I'd [decision] because [reason]".

**Where the corpus differs - nuance vs hedging.** In the ACT persuasion video the coach *endorses* acknowledging limitations: "Given the small sample size, I'd caution that results aren't statistically definitive. That said, we're seeing a strong directional signal that aligns with last quarter's trends" - "when you lead with nuance, people lower their defenses". Reconcile the two this way: nuance about the *evidence* (stated first, then the direction) builds credibility; a "but" *after* your recommendation that lists everything that could go wrong dismantles it. The difference is order and object.

### Part B - Don't claim more than you know

The coach's father read a report card full of straight A's and said only "You're too quiet" - direct, but not objectively accurate. Chinese upbringing trains "indirect praise, direct criticism"; Western culture does the reverse. Strong opinions are fine; "deterministic judgments about other people" trigger defensiveness and endanger relationships. Three claims to stop making:

| You claim to know... | Sounds like | Swap to |
|---|---|---|
| **People** ("You're not detail-oriented") - "one of the fastest ways to destroy trust... you've already decided who they are"; it could be three things seen on one deliverable on one day | a verdict | "When you miss the daily update to the client, it can come across as a lack of attention to detail." (When X happens, you appear Y) |
| **The truth** ("This is wrong," "actually," "this won't work") - the thermostat fight at 22°C: "it's cold" is perception spoken as truth, "a report on you" | a ruling | "I see where you're coming from. Here's what I see differently." |
| **What happened** ("Why did you do that?") | an interrogation | "Can you walk me through your thinking?" |

A fourth, hidden claim: reflexive "I'm sorry" whenever a manager sounds unhappy claims responsibility you have not yet established (Lecture 6.4).

**Related tools from the corpus.**
- **Contrasting** (mutual respect): say what you *don't* mean, then what you *do* mean. "I don't mean to say your idea isn't good. Your ideas are really valuable here. I just want to make sure that we present it in a way that lands as clearly as it deserves". A salesperson's calm contrasting line made "all of my defensiveness just melt away".
- **Mutual purpose**: disagreements are "almost never about the actual goals and almost always about how to achieve them"; ask "why is this important to you?" and go one level higher until you find a shared goal.
- **Care before convincing**: trust = competence × care, "not added, multiplied." Client Sam, a commodity trader "paid to be right," got his first poor review in ten years; the fix was one curiosity question ("Why is cost the most important thing right now?") and one line naming what the COO carried: "Wow, it sounds like you are under intense pressure right now. I want to make sure whatever we decide actually helps you with that".

🗣 **Coach's words:** "Leadership is not about never being wrong. It's about choosing a direction and owning it."

🗣 **Coach's words:** "The most skilled communicators... sound more calibrated."

### Common mistakes

- Presenting the risk list unprompted, in the same breath as the recommendation.
- Waiting for 100% certainty before speaking.
- Telling colleagues who they are ("you're not detail-oriented") instead of what you observed.
- Treating your perception ("it's cold," "this is wrong") as fact.

## 6.4 The weak words - sorry, just, try

### The concept

"The most polite person in the room has the least power". Three words quietly signal that you are asking permission to exist. Even the Canadian-raised coach apologized for everything as an intern, knocking softly on his manager's door with a form: "Sorry to bother you, could you sign this?" The manager's reply became the lesson: "You don't need to apologize to me. You don't need permission to take my time".

### Why it works

- "Sorry" signals you do not believe you are allowed to inconvenience others; follow-ups are your job, not an imposition.
- "Just" pre-softens every request ("I just want to check in," "I just have a quick question") and leaves room for rejection. In Western culture "authority comes from taking up space"; people sense the imbalance and peg you as lacking leadership potential. The coach had a brilliant Chinese direct report who prefaced everything with "just".
- "Try": in Chinese culture leaving leeway is smart; Western managers hear doubt.

### The method - three substitutions

| Situation | Skip | Say |
|---|---|---|
| A mistake is pointed out | "Oh, so sorry I missed that." | "Thanks for pointing that out. I'll make that change." |
| Asking questions | "Sorry for asking so many questions." | "Your insights are super helpful. Fully understanding this helps me to deliver accurately." |
| Following up | "Sorry to bother you, can I get an update?" | "Hi, do you have any updates? Thanks for keeping us informed." |
| Checking in | "Hey, just checking in to see how things are going." | "Hey, checking in. How are things going?" |
| Committing | "I'll try to finish this by tomorrow." | "I expect to have this done by tomorrow. I'll update you by end of day if anything changes." |

Rules: **sorry → thanks** (shift from "I'm so clumsy" to "I appreciate you"); **just → delete**; **try → commit to the outcome, add an update clause**.

🗣 **Coach's words:** "Uncertainty kills careers faster than being occasionally wrong."

### Common mistakes

- Apologizing for telling the truth. The coach once wrote "Hey, I'm sorry about that. It won't happen again" to a boss who berated him for telling a candidate the team worked overtime; his wife asked "why did you apologize for telling the truth?" - and the apology trained the boss that disrespect was acceptable.
- Saying "sorry" whenever a manager *sounds* unhappy, which claims responsibility you have not established.

## 6.5 Voice - the three dials, the falling pitch, rhythm and the pause

### The concept

"Strong speaking = what you say + how you say it," and only style improves quickly - "overnight," in the coach's phrase. Co-workers make basic ideas sound like breakthroughs through delivery. Chinese professionals are ignored "not for their ideas but for flat delivery"; English is rhythmic and native speakers "use beats".

### Why it works

- **Humans are pattern-hungry.** Once your delivery is predictable, attention drifts; keep turning the dials.
- **Speed feeds nerves.** Nervousness speeds you up, and speaking fast makes you more nervous. "When we speak slowly, everything in the room slows down. We are in control". Also: "When you feel like speeding up, that is the exact moment when you need to slow down".
- **Rising pitch asks permission.** At P&G the coach told a VP "I recommend that we increase our brand budget" with a rising intonation and did not get the budget; his manager said "You had the right idea, John, but the wrong voice." A rising "We can deliver on Friday?" invites "wait, I'm not so sure about that, tell me everything"; a falling pitch "sounds like a complete statement. It sounds final, and that's how you get to okay".
- **Filler words are weak; pauses sound powerful**. "Silence is not scary. It is beautiful" - especially for Chinese candidates who want accurate English.

### The method

**A. Three dials - never leave them at factory default**
1. **Tone.** Chinese professionals tend toward monotone because "we do not wear our emotions on our faces." Fix: act out the emotion with your body - chest out, arms open, palms out, wide smile; it is hard to be monotone while doing so. Warm-up: say "Hello everyone, thanks for coming today" flat, then again with the body.
2. **Speed.** Pre-decide: "How do I want to feel? And how do I want the room to feel?" Fast for emotion/excitement, slow for careful thought/seriousness, pause to let it sink in.
3. **Volume.** Drama-teacher rule: "your voice must fit the room and the scene." Chinese professionals "speak more quietly than we should... the drowning out is happening quite literally." Turn it up even though it feels "too much, too loud, too aggressive." If your voice can produce it, that is the authentic you.

**B. End down, not up**
Drop pitch at the end of every recommendation and status update: "I recommend that we increase our brand budget." / "We can deliver on Friday."

**C. Rhythm**
Add intentional pauses; speed up to signal excitement, slow down to signal seriousness; pause and "everyone leans in." Demo sentence: "This solution will cut wasted time. I know you're still figuring out your budget, but at this point, [pause] can you really afford to wait?"

**D. Pause instead of filler**
Slow down; when an "um" is coming, pause instead. If you need time: "Can I take a moment to collect my thoughts?"

**E. Hear yourself**
Record a mock answer and review it in three isolated passes: audio only (fillers, speeding up, shaky voice - "most people never actually hear themselves"), video only (fidgeting, frowning, eye contact), transcript only (is it structured and easy to follow?).

🗣 **Coach's words:** "A powerful statement ends down, not up."

🗣 **Coach's words:** "Turn up the damn volume."

### Common mistakes

- Sounding sarcastic without meaning to: "you're so smart" as admiration vs fighting words is purely tone.
- Filler every few seconds ("um, uh"), which sounds unconfident and junior.
- Saying "wow, that's a great question" after every question - the interviewer starts to doubt your judgment; pause instead.

## 6.6 Speaking up in meetings - the three-level ladder, keeping up, and running the room

### The concept

Chinese professionals enter meetings doubly disadvantaged: by language and by an education system that never rewarded speaking. In business school the coach (a Canadian-born Chinese) was graded on contribution = times spoken × quality; never speaking scored zero, so he raised his hand for every professor question, even with nothing to say. Lesson: "raise your hand first and then figure out what you are going to say." "The opportunity to speak is a gift that you have to fight for".

Underneath is an identity chain: speak only when you have something "worthwhile" (almost never, as an ESL speaker) → used to staying quiet → "someone who doesn't have much to say" → "invisible in the room." The rebuilt chain starts with a smaller action: "I speak when I have an idea, even if it's just average".

### Part A - The three levels

**Level 1 - Just speak.** When frozen, breathe for three seconds, then repeat the question back to set the brain in motion - "zero thinking is required."
> "Why is churn higher than we expected? First, I'll collect my thoughts."

**Level 2 - Speak with structure.** Zoom out with "The main thing is..." (the *first* point that comes to mind, not the best), then zoom in with "For example..." giving exactly one: a number, a moment, or a name. Two sentences is enough to stay in the discussion.
> "The main thing is we're missing data from web users. For example, web users tend to be more engaged, so not having their data may make our churn look higher than it actually is."

**Level 3 - Speak with influence.** Identify the question behind the question - a **worry** ("do I need to be concerned?"), a **decision** ("what should we do?"), or an **understanding** ("how does this work?"). If unsure, check your guess aloud, then aim the answer at it - start with the worry, not the data.
> "It sounds like we're concerned that churn is actually getting worse. Is that right?" → "The main thing is, I don't believe churn is actually rising. For example, we're missing data from web users who tend to be more engaged, so our churn looks worse than it actually is. I can validate that by end of next week."

### Part B - Keeping up when they are faster than you

A senior AI PM (ex-data scientist) felt junior beside his white ex-consultant manager and teammate who spoke fast and polished. The coach's honest reframe: "Of course they sound smart. That's been their job for decades." They are better at running meetings; "don't try to out-talk them... at least not yet." Learn from them, and "use meetings to do what you already do well, which is to bring clarity." Decide: **can it wait?**

- **Yes → follow up after the meeting by message** (frames as care for quality, not weakness):
  > "Hey, I wanted to follow up on what you said about X in the meeting. Things moved really fast, and I want to make sure that I understood it correctly so I can reflect it in next steps. Would you mind recapping your point here?"
- **No → ask for a recap in the meeting**, naming the speaker and the topic. It "adds value," "makes the other person feel respected," and "shows quiet leadership... without needing to contribute anything smart yourself":
  > "I want to revisit what [name] said about X, because I think it's important. Would you mind recapping it so that we're fully aligned?"

Avoid the time-wasting version: "Oh, I missed that. Can you repeat that?" In interviews, the equivalent is "Could you please repeat or rephrase your question? I want to make sure that I can fully address it".

🗣 **Coach's words:** "You aren't failing. You're becoming."

### Part C - Five phrase upgrades for any meeting

| Skip | Say |
|---|---|
| "Let's wait five minutes for everyone to join." | "I appreciate everyone joining on time. While others filter in, let's cover the agenda and a few announcements." |
| "Sorry, I was multitasking. Could you repeat that please?" | "Could you clarify your point for me? I want to make sure I fully understand." |
| "Let's take this offline." | "I'll follow up individually after the meeting to explore this in more detail." |
| "I'm sorry, but I disagree, and here's why." | "I see your perspective. I'd like to offer another viewpoint for us to consider." |
| "I think we're going off topic." | "Good discussion. I suggest that we revisit topic A so that we can achieve our goals for this meeting before coming back to this." |

### Part D - Hosting with authority: the meeting arc

The coach role-plays an entire dashboard-review meeting (with Jen, Jerry and Lynn) to show the phrases at each stage:

1. **Wait / small talk:** "Hey everyone, thanks for joining. I cannot believe it's already September." / "We're gonna give folks a couple more minutes to join."
2. **Agenda:** "Looks like everyone's here. We've got lots to cover today, so let's jump right in. First up is..., then..., and finally..."
3. **Hand-off:** "To kick things off, I'm gonna pass it over to Jen, who is our resident data rock star... Jen, over to you."
4. **Tech / interruptions:** "Sorry to interrupt, Jen. I think you're on mute." / "Lynn, are you still with us? Or was it just me?... I can hear you loud and clear."
5. **Open discussion:** "Let's open this up now for some discussion. Any initial thoughts from the team? We're just spitballing here, so feel free to jump in."
6. **Push back without hostility:** "I see your point. Lowering prices could be a great incentive to retain customers. Although I am concerned that the hit to our margin might offset any gains in retention, so could we consider other options first?"
7. **Park an idea:** "That's an interesting idea, Jerry. But unless I'm missing something that won't move the needle on churn, right? So could we table that for next week's sync, or take it offline?"
8. **Clarify:** "Sorry, Lynn, could you clarify what you meant by funnel optimization?"
9. **Time check + next steps:** "Quick time check. We're almost at time, so I'm gonna wrap this up shortly. Here are the next steps that I captured, and I will distribute these in our group after the call so we can review and comment async."
10. **Close:** "Thanks again, everyone. Great energy, great takeaways as usual."

(Note that the host script does use "sorry to interrupt" and "take it offline" as neutral meeting mechanics; the substitutions in 6.4 and Part C target the apologetic and vague uses, not every instance.)

### Part E - Introverts can lead the room

"I don't need to be loud to lead. Introverts bring depth, not noise." Observe what others miss, ask deep questions, listen with intent; "when you speak quietly, the whole room becomes quiet." Protect your calendar from "let's hop on a call": "Is there a TLDR version that we can discuss async to protect our calendars?"

### Common mistakes

- Waiting for the best point before speaking; the coach says use the first point.
- Answering the data question when the real question was a worry.
- Trying to out-talk polished colleagues instead of slowing the meeting down.
- Vigorous nodding and "yes, yes, okay, I got it," which reads as impatience.
- Reading every reaction as rejection. "Silence, pushback, a subject change, all of it is information about where the other person is standing." Get curious and "you stop needing the room to validate you".

## 6.7 Cultural translation - virtues that don't travel, and asking, declining and pushing back like a peer

### The concept

Some of what Chinese professionals are proudest of does not translate. The coach's father made him recite Confucius; his Canadian school "didn't even give us first place prizes because everyone's a winner". As a director over 500 people he saw Chinese teammates become the go-to for on-call and code-reds ("Ask Xiaoming for on-call because he's reliable; Xiao Chen and Xiao Tong will stay late for a code red") while strategic decisions were made at tables that were "almost exclusively white" - "not because of discrimination" but because "many Chinese professionals focus on execution over impact". The related stereotype he wants you to stop feeding: deferential executor rather than peer and strategist.

The second cultural default is fear of authority. Tension around senior leaders "is not respect, it's fear in a suit," trained by hierarchical upbringing; compliance is self-protective and blocks connection. Being comfortable around leaders signals "I can operate at your level". And Western culture "values equality, not authority" - "I want a peer... it is a mutual opportunity".

### Part A - Translate the virtues

"You need to translate your strengths into the right language." Each new trait must be followed by one concrete example with a measurable result.

| Chinese virtue | What the Western manager hears | Say instead | Proof line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Team player | Everyone in the West is a team player; being agreeable is expected | "I lead with influence" / "I'm skilled at influencing without authority" | "For example, I led a cross-functional project where I helped two teams align on a conflicting timeline, saving the project from delay." |
| Hard worker | Busy ≠ productive; "the last hard-working Chinese colleague with unclear impact" | "I'm results driven" / "I'm impact driven" | "I prioritized high-impact tasks and led the team to deliver two weeks early, under budget." / "During busy season, I built an automated report that saved my team 10 hours a week." |
| Fast learner | "They're not paying you to learn, they're paying you to deliver" | "I'm highly resourceful" | "When we switched to Salesforce, I binge-watched tutorials and migrated our entire customer base with zero errors." |
| Responsible | "Another Chinese candidate who likes to follow orders" | "I take ownership" | "When we lost our project manager, I stepped up outside of my responsibilities to lead the team and help us launch on time and on budget." |

Influence, concretely, means "saying the quiet part out loud in a tense meeting" and gathering allies by aligning your project with stakeholders' incentives.

### Part B - Ask for help like a peer

Asking for help is a skill, not a weakness. Three moves:
1. **Literally say "help."** "Can you help me sharpen this before end of day?" feels collaborative; "Can you review this by end of day?" sounds like ordering. People want to be helpful when their support is valued, not demanded.
2. **Be specific and show your work.** Broad requests are mentally expensive (the helper must guess the problem, time and whether they are the right person). Ask for guidance, not answers.
3. **Check timing.** "Got a sec?" at 5:45 pm Friday says "my needs matter more than your time." People who feel respected want to help.

> "I'm stuck on X aspect of this project. I've tried A and B, but I'm still seeing issue Y. Could I get 15 minutes of your time to brainstorm a solution?"
> "Would now be a good time for a quick question about X, or should I circle back later?"

Then close the loop when they help - "Last time you told me to try XYZ. I actually did, and it helped so much. Here's how it made things better" - which makes them want to help again.

### Part C - Hold your position warmly: the three-beat pushback

One reusable pattern: (1) acknowledge/validate ("I can tell," "I totally get," "I hear you"); (2) state the constraint or feeling tied to a shared goal (quality, profit, staying on track, mutual respect); (3) offer a path or invite alignment.

> Colleague dumps their reports on you: "Wow, I can tell you are slammed. Unfortunately, I can't help you with that since I have a full plate as well. But you're resourceful. I'm sure you'll find a way. And I'm rooting for you."
> Deadline pulled in: "I totally get the urgency. I want to get this done faster too. But with the current scope, delivering tomorrow means cutting quality. And I know we both want to deliver something that we're proud of. So I'm happy to discuss how we can do that."
> "Why is this late? Unacceptable": "I hear you, and I want to reassure you, staying on track is my top priority. Transparently, the additional scope added over the past week has extended the timeline. I know there were good reasons for these additions, and in fact, I'd like to take this opportunity to align on future expectations."
> Told to "relax, stop overreacting": "We respect each other, right? So I'm sure you didn't mean it that way. See, when you attacked me instead of the problem, it felt personal. And it made me uncomfortable. But I'd like to reset because I value working through disagreements with you, not against you."

### Part D - Say no to your manager without capping your career

The coach's final-round director interview ended with HR feedback that "the team found you to be a bit too agreeable" - a senior manager offer instead. "Saying yes was how you survived, but now saying no is how you're gonna show leadership." Sandwich: acknowledge their pressure → decline anchored to *their* top priority → offer a path (delegate, deprioritize, postpone).

> "I know the product launch is your top priority right now. I want to make sure it gets every attention it needs. If I take on this reporting project this week, then I won't be able to give the launch my full attention... Could we revisit this project in two weeks when priority Y will be wrapped up?"

(Upward feedback to a manager - permission, observation, "What are you seeing from your side?" - is covered in Chapter 7; the calibrated-claim rules in 6.3 are its language foundation.)

🗣 **Coach's words:** "Your head down work got you here. Your head up leadership will take you further."

🗣 **Coach's words:** "Each well-planned request for help turns colleagues into allies and hard problems into shared missions."

### Common mistakes

- Opening a self-intro with "Thank you for the opportunity," which places you below the interviewer.
- Flattery ("wow boss, you were so smart") - "textbook 拍马屁"; use curiosity instead: "I noticed how you advocated for more resources for our team in that meeting. How do you communicate persuasively like that?".
- "Can you help me with this project?" with no stuck point, no attempts, no time ask.
- Saying yes and resenting it: if you cannot decline, commit fully, because resentment "just makes the work heavier".

## 6.8 Small talk and "How are you?" - the openers, the party, and being interested

### The concept

"How are you?" is the first interview question, and interviewers "aren't judging you as a candidate based on your answer... but they kind of are". "Fine, how are you?" wastes "the first impression that you will never get to redo". More broadly, "politeness creates distance" - we are most polite to people we do not want a relationship with, and offers come from "closing that distance fast". Small talk is "a verbal handshake" - pick up their hand; "my commute was good" gives nothing to build on.

At office parties the diagnosis is sharper: "You're not invisible because they don't care. You're invisible because you're afraid to be seen". And the master key, borrowed from the coach's wife's psychotherapy training: "true connection comes from being interested," not interesting.

### Part A - Answering "How are you?"

Template: **positive state + one specific hook** (gratitude / connection to the company / an observation / recent news / a slice of you), optionally a reciprocal question. Say their name - "like flipping a switch in their brain" - and smile, even on the phone; it changes how your voice sounds.

- Gratitude: "I'm doing great. Thanks for asking. I'm excited to chat with you today, and I'm particularly grateful for your time, as I figure it's a particularly busy time of year for you and the team."
- Connection: "I've been a loyal client for many years, and it's a real treat to meet you and discuss how I might become a member of the team one day."
- Observation: "Couldn't be better. By the way, I love that painting in your background. I've been looking for some artwork for my office as well."
- News: "I'm great. I was thrilled to read about your launch of the [product] in the news lately. I think you and your team must be so thrilled and maybe a bit exhausted."
- Slice of you: "Another day in paradise. I just came back from my morning jog, which I've recently been trying out as a replacement for my usual cup of coffee... How are you?"
- Common ground: "I noticed that you're also based in Toronto. I actually live just a few blocks from the CN Tower. Which part of the city are you in?"
- Seasonal: "Feels like August is flying by already. I cannot believe it's almost Q4. Is it a busy season on your end too?"
- Vulnerability + recognition ("interviewer empathy"): "Honestly, I was a nervous wreck last night, but this is probably just another Monday for you." / "Appreciate you taking the time this afternoon. I hope I'm not standing between you and your lunch." / "Did you actually get to finish a hot cup of coffee before jumping on this call, or are we fueling this interview on sheer willpower?"

Close the same way - specific, not generic: "Thank you for taking the time to meet with me today. I especially appreciated our discussion of your team's goals for the next year, and I'm looking forward to exploring how I can help". Never send a copy-paste thank-you email; the coach has received hundreds.

Interview-specific rules: find common ground via LinkedIn first, with the company itself as the guaranteed fallback; let the interviewer set the level of personal disclosure and never exceed it; keep it positive; listen for tidbits and build on them.

### Part B - "Ask for the best"

Factual follow-ups ("How's the weather there? Which cities did you go to?") get factual replies: "You're not immigration and border control. He passed that at the airport." Asking for the highlight "activates the desire in your coworker to share something extreme."
1. They answer a small-talk question.
2. Instead of a fact question, ask for the best part: "Oh, wow, what was the best part of the trip? Any highlights?" / "So, what's the best part of having an alcohol-free lifestyle?"
3. When asked, volunteer your own best part: "Actually, I have to tell you about my favorite part of this weekend, which is that I discovered this amazing new restaurant..." - not "laundry and Netflix."
4. Return questions with "how about you," since people ask what they want to answer.

🗣 **Coach's words:** "Don't treat small talk like QA."

### Part C - The office-party playbook

**Three-step script**
1. **Observation** on a detail (drink, music, watch): "That's an interesting drink you have. What is it?" - "You don't need charisma... perfect English. You just need an eye for the interesting details."
2. **Go deeper and offer a piece of yourself**: "What's the story behind you joining this team?" / "Big celebration for a big quarter yesterday. I was so exhausted at the end. How was it for you?" / "I'm curious, what's the best office party that you've ever been to? I can start."
3. **Exit gracefully, then circle back before leaving the event**: "I'm gonna go get a refill. See you later." → "I'm heading out, but it was such a pleasure chatting with you." "You're choosing the final note that they will remember you by."

**Five-step therapist playbook**
1. **Observation** (a "verbal wave"; if they don't wave back, fine): "That's such an interesting color. I'm going to be wrong here, but I'm guessing that's a blue margarita."
2. **Reflection** - paraphrase plus a small reaction: "Oh, a blue lagoon. That's definitely new to me. It sounds like you got what you asked for."
3. **Validation** - "conversations run on emotion"; watch for a pause, a tone shift, a glance down, then name and normalize: "It makes sense to feel..." / "I can totally relate" / "It's understandable that..." Contrast: "I totally get you. Socializing with co-workers and managers isn't exactly like hanging out with your friends, is it?" vs the invalidating "Don't be nervous. Just be cool."
4. **Open question** - ask for the best, why/how: "What do you do here, and how are you liking it?"
5. **Strength ID** - observation + effect, "a genuine observation that says, I see you": "Well, that shows how reliable you are. No wonder you're trusted with month end." / "You take a lot on your shoulders because you're always thinking about everyone else. Your team is lucky to have you."

Introvert option: say it out loud - "Hey, I'm an introvert. So if I disappear early, it's not you, it's me" - then have the conversations you planned and leave without a farewell round.

### Part D - Prepared topical small talk ("melon break")

Across his podcast episodes the coach models a two-register pattern: a casual version for co-workers and a reflective version for a boss that ties the topic to a work idea.

- Spotify Wrapped - co-worker: "Hey, Spotify Wrapped just dropped. Have you checked out yours yet?" Boss: "Spotify Wrapped this year is another fun step up from last year. The new features are a clever way of visualizing habits."
- A meme - co-worker: "Apparently there's this meme where people say they're in a very Chinese time of their life. As a Chinese, I find that to be..." Boss: "...It's interesting how pop culture shifts can naturally stem from subtle cultural shifts."
- Something personal - "Did you know that when I first came here from China, I just couldn't put up with the bread here? I grew up on super soft, fluffy milk bread... Have you ever tried Chinese bread? Next time I'll bring you some to try."

The structure is what to copy: a specific, current hook; a short reaction; and, for leaders, one sentence connecting it to how you think about work.

### Common mistakes

- Autopilot "I'm good, how are you?".
- Interrogating: who/where/when questions instead of "what was the best part?".
- Trying to look important. Corporate parties feel forced because "everyone is on the surface trying to look important".
- Exceeding the interviewer's level of disclosure or bringing negative topics.
- Leaving without circling back, so the last note is the mid-conversation exit.

## Practice set

### Drills

**Drill 1 - Triage log (6.1).** For one week, before each spoken or written communication, write S1 or S2 next to it after asking "Does my performance depend on communicating carefully here?" At the end of the week, list the everyday situations you stopped over-editing.

**Drill 2 - 10x daily (6.1).** Schedule ten triggers for tomorrow (each phone pickup, each co-worker greeting, the lunch walk, groceries). At each, speak English for 30–60 seconds without self-correcting. Track for 30 days.

**Drill 3 - Readability check (6.1).** Paste your "greatest achievement" or self-intro script into a Flesch–Kincaid calculator. If it is above grade 8, rewrite with shorter words and sentences until it passes, then read it to a listener.

**Drill 4 - Scissor rewrite (6.2).** Take your last long status message to your manager. Rewrite it in exactly three sentences: "The main thing is ___." / one line of cause / "The reason I'm telling you is ___."

**Drill 5 - SCQA in 60 words (6.2).** Take your last update to a leader and rewrite it as four SCQA sentences under 60 words. Read it aloud with a falling pitch on the Answer.

**Drill 6 - AIRBUS under 60 seconds (6.2).** Have someone fire three unexpected work questions at you. Answer each aloud with all six AIRBUS steps, timed under 60 seconds. Role-play lines for your partner: "How do you stay up to date in this field?" / "What's the budget for this?" / "Is your project on track?"

**Drill 7 - De-hedge (6.3).** Write your next recommendation as one sentence plus a "Based on X and Y, this gives us ___" clause. Put the risks on a separate page you only show when asked. Then rehearse the pause: say the two sentences, count silently to three, and let your partner play the challenger ("What about the downside?") - respond with the prepared analysis.

**Drill 8 - Calibration log (6.3).** For one day, log every time you say "you're...", "this is wrong / actually," "why did you...," or "sorry." Rewrite each in the calibrated form ("When X, you appear Y" / "Here's what I see differently" / "Can you walk me through your thinking?").

**Drill 9 - Three-word search (6.4).** Search your last 20 sent messages for "sorry," "just," and "try." Rewrite each with the substitutions. Count how many survive a second reading.

**Drill 10 - Two recordings (6.5).** Record a 30-second update twice: once at factory default, once with chest out and arms open, deliberate pauses, 20% more volume, and a falling pitch at the end of each statement. Then do the three-pass review (audio only, video only, transcript only) on the second take.

**Drill 11 - Hand up first (6.6).** In one meeting this week, raise your hand before you know what you will say. Use "The main thing is... For example..." Afterwards note whether the question was really a worry, a decision, or an understanding. In the same week, use the in-meeting recap script once and send one post-meeting recap message.

**Drill 12 - Translate and ask (6.7).** Find every "hardworking / team player / fast learner / responsible" in your resume and self-intro; replace each with an influence, results, resourcefulness or ownership statement plus one example with a number. Then draft your next help request with the "stuck on X / tried A and B / seeing Y / 15 minutes" template, led by a timing check.

**Drill 13 - Party prep (6.8).** Before your next event, pre-write one observation opener, two "ask for the best" questions with your own answers attached, one validation line, one strength-ID line, and the circle-back closure. Use all of them on one person and afterwards write down which step you skipped.

**Drill 14 - Four "How are you?" answers (6.8).** Write one per hook (gratitude, personal, environment, company news) plus one vulnerability-plus-recognition version; say each aloud with the interviewer's name and a smile, under ten seconds.

### Templates

**Scissor update**
> The main thing is ______ [status / point]. The cause was ______. The reason I'm telling you is ______ [decision or action I need from you].

**SCQA**
> We're ______ [situation]. The issue is ______ [complication]. So we need to decide whether ______ or ______ [question]. My recommendation is ______ because ______ [answer].

**Recommendation without hedging**
> My recommendation is ______. Based on ______ and ______, this gives us the fastest path to ______. [pause]

**Commit line**

> Thanks for pointing that out. I'll ______. / Your insights are super helpful. Fully understanding this helps me to ______. / Do you have any updates on ______? Thanks for keeping us informed.

**Calibrated feedback**

> I wrote the message below myself. Do not rewrite it. Only fix grammar mistakes, unnatural word choices, or imprecise word choices. Keep my sentence structure, my tone and my words wherever possible. Do not add sentences. Do not make it more formal. Do not use any word I wouldn't say out loud. If something is unclear, ask me instead of rewriting it. Context: this is for ______ [person] and I want them to ______ [action].

## Cheat sheet

**Mindset**
- "Stop competing in English, start competing in clarity."
- "Polish serves the speaker, not the audience." Simple words + strong logic.
- Triage: "Does my performance depend on communicating carefully here?" No → System 1, imperfect and fluent.
- Comprehensible beats concise; scripts at Flesch–Kincaid grade 5–8.

**Structures (conclusion first)**
- Scissor: "The main thing is..." → cause → "The reason I'm telling you is..."
- SCQA: Situation → Complication → Question → Answer.
- So-what sandwich + an explicit ask.
- AIRBUS when blank: Acknowledge, Identify, Reconfirm, Broadcast, Unpack, Summarize.
- Presentations: GPS - Guide (one sentence), Path (three), Support (one idea per slide, BLUF titles).
- Writing: your own English first; AI fixes grammar only; out-loud test.

**Calibration**
- Recommendation: state it → "Based on X and Y" → payoff → pause → risks only when asked.
- "My understanding is..." - 30% sure, 100% confident.
- Report → Advise → Commit: "If it were my call, I'd ___ because ___."
- Never claim to know people / the truth / what happened: "When X, you appear Y" / "Here's what I see differently" / "Walk me through your thinking."
- Contrasting: the don't, then the do. Mutual purpose: go one level higher.

**Words**
- sorry → thanks; just → delete; try → commit + update clause.
- hard worker → results/impact driven; team player → lead with influence; fast learner → resourceful; responsible → take ownership. Always + one example with a number.
- Say "help." Be specific. Check timing.

**Voice**
- Three dials: tone (use the body), speed (decide the feeling; fast = emotion, slow = thought, pause = sink in), volume (fit the room; go louder than comfort).
- End statements down, not up.
- Pause instead of "um." "Can I take a moment to collect my thoughts?"

**Meetings**
- Level 1 repeat the question; Level 2 "The main thing is... For example..."; Level 3 name the worry/decision/understanding and confirm aloud.
- Can it wait? → message recap. Can't? → "I want to revisit what [name] said about X... Would you mind recapping it so we're fully aligned?"
- Ask for clarity: "Can you expand on that?" / "Could you rephrase that?"
- Disagree: "I see your perspective. I'd like to offer another viewpoint for us to consider."
- Push back: acknowledge → constraint tied to shared goal → path forward.

**Small talk**
- "How are you?" = positive state + one specific hook; use their name; smile.
- Ask for the best, not the logistics; volunteer your own best.
- Party: observe → reflect → validate → open question → strength ID; exit, then circle back.
- Be interested, not interesting.

**Do / Don't**
- Do raise your hand first and figure out what to say second. Don't wait for the best point.
- Do read silence and pushback as information. Don't read them as rejection.
- Do write badly in your own words. Don't send AI-composed English.
- Do turn the volume up. Don't apologize for taking up space.
