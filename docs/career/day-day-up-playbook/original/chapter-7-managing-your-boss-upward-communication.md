# Chapter 7 — Managing Your Boss & Upward Communication

> This chapter is about the one relationship that decides most of your career outcomes: the one with your manager. The coach has managed twenty-plus direct reports, run performance reviews, put someone on a PIP, and been put on one himself. His central observation is that Chinese and other Asian professionals were raised in a system where feedback flows only downhill, where "no" to an authority figure had real consequences, and where staying quiet felt like maturity. In a North American workplace those instincts read as fear, over-agreeableness, and invisibility. The remedy is not to become louder or more political. It is to treat the relationship as something you actively shape: align in 1:1s instead of reporting status, remove your manager's uncertainty instead of adding to it, build a "bank balance" so you can afford to say no, give feedback upward without ambush, walk into reviews with evidence, read your manager's operating style, and — when it goes badly — know whether the PIP in front of you is in good faith. As he puts it, "Every day, you train your manager how to treat you. So train them well."

**What you'll be able to do**

- Run a 1:1 that aligns on expectations, priorities, resources and development instead of reciting a task list, and use the "menu approach" to ask for resources.
- Send updates that lower your manager's anxiety — including when you are off track — and escalate problems with two paths forward instead of a burden.
- Build trust with senior leaders through small touch points, closed loops and curiosity rather than compliance or flattery.
- Say no, push back on a decision, or announce a leave using scripts anchored to what your manager cares about most.
- Give a manager negative feedback safely (permission → observation → shared fix), and recover from a public dressing-down within 24 hours.
- Walk into a performance review with a brag book, respond to negative feedback with questions and a situational reframe, and negotiate future expectations.
- Place your manager on the assertiveness × structure matrix, choose the matching tactics, and manage an irrational boss "as an operating system."
- Diagnose whether a PIP is good faith or bad faith and negotiate accordingly.

## 7.1 The alignment model: what a 1:1 is actually for

### The concept

Most people use their 1:1 to report: "I finished X, I'm working on Y, next week Z." The coach, who led a team of twenty, calls status updates "the lowest value of our time" and "the feeling of this could have been an email, personified." A status-only 1:1 signals that you are "obsessively focused on tasks, not outcomes." The result is that the manager quietly doubts whether you understand what matters, you leave feeling unappreciated, and "trust is going to erode quietly."

The alternative is to use the meeting to align on four things: **expectations, priorities, resources, development**. "Stop looking to deliver work in your 1:1s. Start delivering trust."

### Why it works — the manager's view

Managers pick the person who does the 20% of work that drives 80% of results; busy is not productive. When someone arrives with a list of ten things and expects praise, the manager is thinking "half of these shouldn't even be on your list." The coach is blunt: "I would actually choose the lazy employee who prioritizes strategically over the hardworking one who executes blindly every single time."

And "your manager's priorities are absolutely your priorities" — they are the measuring stick your performance is judged against. Not understanding (or not caring about) them is one of the commonest managing-up failures he sees.

### The framework: four alignments

**1. Expectations.** Give a quick update, then ask whether you are solving the right problem and how success is measured. "Doing what you were asked and meeting expectations is not always the same thing." Crucially, if the expectation is unrealistic or you disagree, surface it *now* — pretending to be aligned while acting differently is what breaks trust.

> "Am I solving the right problem? Is this meeting your expectations? How do you define and measure success?"

**2. Priorities.** Ask what is top of mind for your manager and why. Write the answer at the top of your own task list. Before the meeting, run the self-check: What is top of mind for me and why? For my manager and why? What changed in team goals this month? What feedback does my manager have on where I spend my time?

> "What is top of mind for you this week? How can I help with this priority? I want to make sure I'm focused on what's most important to you and the team."

The kind of pushback managers love to hear in this slot: "I know we planned to do X, but based on what I'm seeing, based on the priorities, I think Y would actually be better." / "I know that we plan to launch feature X, but based on customer feedback, I actually think feature Y would have more impact."

**3. Resources — the menu approach.** Asking for resources is not complaining; it helps the manager deliver what they promised upward. But "I can't do that, I need more help" is a stop sign. Replace the yes/no ask with an A/B choice — "a menu instead of a stop sign" — which positions you as a strategic partner.

```
I've taken some time to evaluate the resourcing, and I've come up with these two options.
If you want A by next week, I'll need support from Y.
If we don't have support from Y, I can still deliver B by next week.
```


**4. Development.** Don't wait for the annual review; once a quarter (monthly if you can) block an extended 1:1 for growth. Ask for one strength to double down on and one weakness to fix, and scope stretch projects together. Managers are often themselves evaluated on developing talent, so owning your growth "makes your manager look good too." But the ownership is yours: "Nobody cares about your career except you," and it is a misconception that developing you is the manager's job.

> "What's one strength that I should double down on, or one weakness that I need to address?"

### Worked example: the cost of fake alignment

The coach's own story, told in three videos. His boss instructed the hiring managers to say "whatever it took" to land candidates — oversell the role. He disagreed silently. Then a dream candidate asked about hours and he told her honestly that he couldn't promise 9-to-5. She checked with the boss, who "exploded." The lesson he draws is precise: the boss was angry "not because I disagreed … because I had acted like we were aligned when we weren't. I had surprised him." "That was the day that I stopped being his top performer." The fault was the silence, not the disagreement.

### Bring problems *and* solutions

"Bring solutions, not problems" is sound but incomplete. Sometimes you should bring a problem, because your manager's role is to level up your performance using experience you don't have yet. The skill is knowing when to resolve proactively and when to collaborate — and when you collaborate, arrive with a proposal and a genuine request for input:

```
This is the problem we're facing. I've identified solutions A and B.
My initial proposal is to go with B because of XYZ.
However, I'd love to get your input on this approach to make sure I'm considering all angles.
Do you think solution B aligns with our priorities, or is there another perspective I might be missing?
```


🗣 **Coach's words:** "Stop looking to deliver work in your 1:1s. Start delivering trust."

### Common mistakes

- Reciting a task list and expecting credit for volume.
- Nodding along to an expectation you privately think is unrealistic, then doing your own thing.
- Framing a resource need as "I can't" instead of as options.
- Treating development as an annual event owned by the manager.

## 7.2 Reducing your manager's anxiety: updates, escalation and blame

### The concept

Employees assume success means doing the job well. Managers define success as reduced uncertainty. They sit under shifting director priorities, KPI pressure and resource competition, and are "anxious people" — possibly more anxious about your work than you are. "Your job is to remove uncertainty, not add to it."

🗣 **Coach's words:** "The best thing you can bring to your manager isn't just talent, it's predictability."

### Why it works — two customer stories

*The sliding door.* When the coach bought a backyard sliding door, the vendor let him hammer-test the glass, showed a finished install and walked through a 20-year warranty — "he answered the anxieties that he knew that I had" before being asked. When the installers found the frame half an inch too high, they offered two options (cut drywall or cut hardwood) and let the customer decide.

*The Amazon toboggan.* Amazon doesn't say "arriving soon" and go silent; it says three days, left warehouse, in transit, out for delivery, photo proof. It delivers peace of mind, not just a toboggan. "No matter what work you do, you actually work in the service industry because you work in the service of your boss."

"Silence doesn't feel neutral to them. It feels like something might be wrong." People fill information gaps with worst-case assumptions.

### The method

**Step 1 — Pre-answer unspoken worries.** Before each project, write down: "What is my manager worried about that they haven't said out loud?" Answer each before work starts.

**Step 2 — Weekly one-liner.** "Hey, the project is currently on track. Here's a recap and next steps." You are "updating them on their anxiety." A junior-level variant: "Hey, quick update. The draft is 80% done, still on track for tomorrow."

**Step 3 — Updates as tracking notifications, especially when off track.** Structure: status → cause → what's aligned/fixed → new ETA. The manager then trusts you won't hide or surprise them, and can redirect ("the VP doesn't care about that data point anymore, just ship it").

```
Hey, quick update. I expect to deliver the report three days later than we discussed.
I ran into this issue with data latency, but I've aligned on the issue with the data team,
And they're gonna ship the fix tonight. Just letting you know.
```


**Step 4 — The problem-plus-two-paths rule.** Bringing just a problem transfers the burden. Before escalating ask: "Have I thought through at least two ways forward?" If not, you're not ready.

```
Hey, quick update. The report's gonna be three days late. I ran into a data issue,
But I've got two paths forward. Want me to walk you through them?
```
This tells the manager "I'm on top of it, I'm not hiding, and I won't make you solve this alone."

### Making the update decision-ready

Two structures from the communication chapter apply directly here. The **scissor method** clamps both ends of the message: open with "The main thing is…" (conclusion first, blocking the Chinese "pu dian" context-first habit) and close with "The reason I'm telling you is…" (forces the ask). Client Bing's rewrite: "The main thing is we're two weeks behind and the launch date is at risk. The cause was the vendor delay in week two. The reason I'm telling you this is that I'd like your input on whether we hold the date and cut the reporting feature or move the date." **SCQA** (Situation → Complication → Question → Answer) does the same for a decision: "We're off track for Q4 delivery. The issue is that several new features were added mid cycle. So we need to decide whether to reduce scope or add more resources. My recommendation is…" "Stop trying to impress. Start helping them decide."

### Ownership without blame

Client Kim, an AI engineer: "The harder I work, the more blame I get." Chinese professionals work weekends for the applause of ownership, but when things go wrong "suddenly it's all on you." Three moves:

1. **Responsibility, not liability.** Responsibility is owning your piece; liability is taking the blame for the whole thing. Don't assume an outcome that was never yours.
   - Don't: "Workstream B is behind schedule, so I'll make sure it all gets done."
   - Do: "I've made sure workstream A is done and on track. I want to flag that workstream B is behind schedule. I think we should align on how we can support it to get back on track."
2. **Document before you dive.** "Undocumented help is invisible help." Clear it with the teammate, then tell your manager where your responsibility starts and ends. "Visibility is as simple as raising your hand before you solve the problem."
   - To teammate: "Part A is done and I have some time. I notice Part B is tough. Want me to take a pass? Your call."
   - To manager: "Quick update. Part A is done. Teammate okayed my help on Part B, so I'm stepping in to support. Let me know if you have concerns."
3. **Acknowledge, don't apologize.** "Every sorry is a quiet confession." Apologizing accepts blame; acknowledging accepts the situation. Remind the boss you're on the same team and pivot to what's next.
   - Don't: "I'm so sorry. Unfortunately, the client terminated our deal. I should have flagged this earlier."
   - Do: "The client went another way, and I'm just as frustrated as you are. In our sync last week, we discussed how Part B was behind, and despite our efforts to step in and support, it wasn't enough. Here's what I think we can do going forward."

The reflexive "sorry" is a broader habit — his own manager once told him, "You don't need permission to take my time." Replace sorry with thanks: "Thanks for pointing that out. I'll make that change."

### Common mistakes

- Going quiet because there's "nothing new" — silence reads as risk.
- Escalating a problem with no options attached.
- Volunteering to "make sure it all gets done" on a workstream you don't own.
- Leading with context and burying the ask.

## 7.3 Building trust: connection, not compliance

### The concept

The tension many Asian professionals feel around senior leaders "is not respect, it's fear in a suit." Authoritative parenting trained a belief that you are below authority and must quietly comply — never question the teacher, never talk back. Compliance is self-protective and self-centric (your image, your safety, your fear); connection requires focusing on the other person. Trust with Western leaders comes from connection.

The coach's illustrations: a Seoul business lunch where twelve people ate in silence, bowed and filed out behind the boss; his white Canadian friends who called parents by first name and negotiated their allowance.

### Why it works — the leader's view

"Leaders need to poop too." Leadership is lonely: when the coach became a director, people stopped joking with him and stopped giving him feedback; he missed positive feedback most. Leaders remember the people who make them feel human and connected. Being comfortable around leaders also signals leadership potential — "I can operate at your level."

The underlying equation: trust equals "competence times care. Not added, multiplied" — being right with zero care yields zero trust. Chinese professionals were taught that logic matters most and that care is "拍马屁 in a different costume." It isn't; "care is a skill."

### The method: four moves

**1. Small seasonal touch points.**

```
Thanksgiving:
With Thanksgiving upon us, I want to share my gratitude and genuine thanks for your support
With [project]. Our team is lucky to have your care and guidance.

Year-end:
With the end of year approaching, I wanted to drop you a line to say thanks for your leadership
And coaching on [project]. I appreciate you. Hope you have a wonderful holiday break and I'm
Looking forward to another great year ahead.
```

**2. Close the loop when they help you.** Report what you tried and the result.

```
I'm just checking in to close the loop on your helpful advice earlier. I tried what you suggested
And it made a big difference by [specific impact]. Thank you again for your guidance.
```

**3. Curiosity instead of flattery.** "Wow boss, you were so smart, you're the best" is a lazy shortcut — "textbook 拍马屁." Curiosity produces the same feel-good outcome through genuine interest. Adopt the mindset of a journalist writing an article about their life. (He cites Dale Carnegie: more friends in two months by being interested in others than in two years trying to interest them.)

> "Hey, I noticed how you advocated for more resources for our team in that meeting. How do you communicate persuasively like that?" / "How are you spending your time outside of work these days?"

**4. Treat leaders as humans.** Joke with them, give them feedback, remember they carry pressure you can't see.

### The first 30 days: listen before you act

If the manager is new to you, the trust-building starts with a listening tour. The urge to "prove myself fast" is a trap — you say yes to everything and volunteer for every project; the commonest new-hire pitfall is acting before observing. "Do you think your manager is an idiot? No? Then why are you doubting them for hiring you?" Your manager and HR are nervous too: they invested time, budget and credibility.

The tour: book 1:1s with manager, teammates, cross-functional partners and skip-levels; ask about the tool stack (what and why), team rituals, who really influences decisions, and what success looks like; and profile your manager deeply — how they communicate, decide and lead, and their current priorities. "Play the new girl card" and ask the dumb questions that make the team question useless process steps. At Shopify, the impressive new hires on his bizops team were the ones who asked deep questions, not those who pushed big changes in week one.

🗣 **Coach's words:** "You have nothing to prove because you were chosen."

### Common mistakes

- Mistaking silence for respect.
- Flattering instead of asking.
- Taking advice and never reporting back.
- Over-proving in week one instead of observing.

## 7.4 Saying no, pushing back, and asking for something big

### The concept: the relationship bank account

The coach's own final-round director interview went well — until HR relayed the feedback: "the team found you to be a bit too agreeable." He was offered senior manager instead. He sees the same pattern in many coached Chinese professionals. The fear of "no" comes from upbringing, where refusing parents or teachers had real consequences. In the Western workplace, "saying yes was how you survived, but now saying no is how you're gonna show leadership."

The relationship is a bank account. Deposits are real value delivered — solving problems they couldn't solve without you, delivering what they actually need. A "no" is a withdrawal. "Why else do you even have a balance if not to use it?" Build the balance by asking two questions in every 1:1 and delivering on them: "What is top of mind for you right now?" / "Here's how I can help with that — what do you think?"

### The sandwich method

Decline because you are protecting what they care about most, not because you are busy — you appear as a thought partner, not a complainer.

1. **Acknowledge their pressure.** "I understand that there's pressure to get the reporting project out this week."
2. **Decline anchored to their top priority.** "I know the product launch is your top priority right now. I want to make sure it gets every attention it needs. If I take on this reporting project this week, then I won't be able to give the launch my full attention. And I don't want to split focus when something this important is on the line for us."
3. **Offer a path forward** — delegate, deprioritize or postpone.
   - Delegate: "I know that Eric is an expert on this subject. Can he take the first draft while I stay focused on the launch?"
   - Deprioritize: "If you want the reporting project done by the end of this week, then we have to deprioritize the development of Y feature."
   - Postpone: "Could we revisit this project in two weeks when priority Y will be wrapped up?"


When you genuinely can't say no: choose the yeses that build a skill you need or visibility with people who matter — then commit fully. Resentment "just makes the work heavier." And the limit case: if a manager never acknowledges what you bring and treats every no as an attack, "that is not a communication problem. That is the wrong manager. You cannot outwork someone who has already decided not to see you."

The same three-beat shape — acknowledge / constraint tied to a shared goal / path forward — runs through the coach's general pushback scripts, e.g. on a pulled-in deadline: "I totally get the urgency. I want to get this done faster too. But with the current scope, delivering tomorrow means cutting quality. And I know we both want to deliver something that we're proud of. So I'm happy to discuss how we can do that." And on "Why is this late? Unacceptable": "I hear you, and I want to reassure you, staying on track is my top priority. Transparently, the additional scope added over the past week has extended the timeline… I'd like to take this opportunity to align on future expectations."

### Changing a decision: ask, make them feel the cost, accept

Client Jen (5+ years in product) learned a reorg would make "Lady," a peer she couldn't stand, her manager. Her draft message was "I can take anybody as my manager, but I cannot, will not, work with her." The coach agreed the ask was valid and necessary, but it must not sound like complaining — leading with "I don't want to work with her" is self-centered and hands the manager a problem they'd rather ignore.

**Step 1 — Ask before you tell.** "When you want to influence someone with more authority than you, you don't walk in with demands. You align with them first." Jen's first conversation was only questions: "Why did you structure the reorg this way? What are you hoping it achieves? If this goes well, what's your ideal vision?"

**Step 2 — Make them feel the cost.** "To move someone, you have to make them feel something." Vague wording avoids painful specifics, "but that's precisely the messy space where she can actually influence." Arguments must be concrete, visual and tied to what the manager already cares about.

Weak version (avoid):
> "I want to be transparent. Lady and I don't align well, and I don't think I'd develop effectively reporting to her. My suggestion would be for me to take a separate vertical and report to a different manager."

Strong version:
```
I understand you want Lady in this role. Her strategic experience makes sense, but I have to be
Transparent. Lady and I see things very differently, and I don't believe I'll grow under her.
The last time we worked together, we disagreed on nearly every call, and a project that should
Have taken a week took a month. Putting me under her now doesn't give you two strong contributors.
It gives you half a functional resource, once you subtract the energy we'll spend on friction.
I'm raising this because I want your plan to work for the team. I have one idea which might help.
Let Lady own this vertical, and let me take a separate one. That way, we're both at full speed,
Pulling the same direction instead of against each other.
```


**Step 3 — Accept what comes.** You can do everything right and still not get it; others are allowed to disagree with or dislike you. Ruminating lets them "live rent-free in your head… They're not paying the cost of that conflict. You are." The worst outcome is "taking them home with you every single night."

A related mindset tool: swap "should" for "how can I." "My manager should support me" becomes "How can I get more support?" — because "every time you say how things should be, you're robbing yourself of the power you needed to make a change."

### Announcing a leave: Acknowledge, Assure, Affirm, Align

Managers want to celebrate news like a pregnancy but must immediately think about workload, stakeholders and teammates; a badly handled announcement can shape the relationship after you return. Avoid the hesitant version ("so I guess I need to take maternity leave soon") and the cold notification ("I'm informing you that I will be taking maternity leave effective two weeks from now"). Come with a drafted high-level transition plan and propose touch points.

```
I wanted to share some exciting personal news. I'm expecting.                       [Acknowledge]
I know this may mean adjustments for the team, and I'd like to work with you to make the
Transition as smooth as possible. As a first step, I've already drafted a high level transition
Plan which outlines how I envision handoffs to ensure continuity during my leave.    [Assure]
I've really appreciated your support so far, and it means a lot to know that I can count on your
Guidance during this transition.                                                     [Affirm]
I'd love to set up regular touch points so we can stay aligned on the transition plan. That way,
When I return, I can step right back in and keep building on our momentum.           [Align]
```


### Common mistakes

- Declining because "I'm busy" instead of because you're protecting their priority.
- Saying yes and then doing the work with resentment.
- Walking in with the demand before asking what the decision-maker wants.
- Flat, abstract asks that spare you the painful specifics.

## 7.5 Giving feedback upward — and surviving public criticism

### The concept

In Chinese families feedback flows only downhill, so telling a manager something negative "can feel like a violation of nature's law." That is survival instinct, not a character flaw. But "feedback is a gift" cuts both ways: deliver it "wrapped," the way you'd want to receive it. Client Coco wanted to tell her "toxic" manager the damage she was causing; the coach offered what has and hasn't worked, not a single right answer.

### The three-step method

**Step 1 — Connect before you correct.** Ask permission so it's a conversation, not an ambush: unrequested feedback feels like an ambush; welcomed feedback feels like a conversation. And assume positive intent — she is probably under pressure you can't see and coping badly. "If you walk in believing she's a villain, she will feel it, and she will act it."

```
I've been reflecting on how our team works together, and I have some observations from the ground.
Would you be open to hearing them sometime this week? I care about keeping our team close,
And I want to help where I can.
```

**Step 2 — Create shared reality.** Observation, not correction. "Your micromanagement is hurting us" is an accusation and triggers defensiveness. Compliment sandwiches fail because the underlying message is still "there is something wrong with you." An observation is a fact both of you can look at, anchored to team results. Then the key sentence: "What are you seeing from your side?"

```
I've noticed that in our last few team meetings, people have gone quiet. Ideas that used to come up
In the room now only get shared in private chats afterward. I worry we're losing input that could
Help us hit our goals. What are you seeing from your side?
```

**Step 3 — Improve reality together.** No to-do list handed over, no vague "let's see." Ask what to do differently and how you can do your part. "It's hard to feel attacked by someone who just asked how they can help."

> "What do you think we should do differently? And how can I do my part?"

**Caveat:** this works with leaders who are "difficult but reachable." Where there is no mutual respect, it doesn't — see 7.7 for that case.

### Calibrated language

Never claim more than you know — about people, the truth, or what happened. "You're not detail-oriented" → "When X happens, you appear Y." "This is wrong" → "I see where you're coming from. Here's what I see differently." "Why did you do that?" → "Can you walk me through your thinking?" This is not softness: "Accuracy is not softness."

### After a public dressing-down: the 24-hour rule

"Wendy," promoted four times in ten years at a global luxury brand by a Devil-Wears-Prada boss, was called "immature" in front of colleagues and senior leaders. She swallowed her words and tears "like most of us Chinese do." The coach's reframe: "When was the last time you lost your temper?" (She had snapped at her boyfriend.) "Do you think you're a cruel, unreasonable person?" So why would one snap make her manager one?

The backstory mattered: days earlier Wendy had made an insensitive joke at an event; the manager had pulled her aside ("Why are you so awkward all the time?"); Wendy stayed quiet, thinking that was mature. The manager didn't move on, and it resurfaced publicly. Public criticism "is not good management," but "when a manager loses it, it is almost never just about that moment… you owe it to yourself to find out what that is."

The method:
1. In the moment, don't fight back — withdraw, breathe, cool down.
2. Within 24 hours request a private 1:1. "Never go to bed angry"; don't let it "sit and rot."
3. Define two outcomes: get their perspective (what and why) and get room to share yours.
4. Open with the three-part script:

```
I believe your intention was [something positive].            ← signals no attack
And when you [specific behavior], it makes me feel [honest feeling].   ← factual, no blame
I'd love to explore and understand your perspective better, and I also wanted to share mine.  ← opens the door
```


No Cinderella ending is promised; the value is learning "something real about the person that she works with every day."

### Common mistakes

- Ambushing with unrequested feedback.
- Accusations and compliment sandwiches.
- Handing the manager a list of fixes, or ending with nothing.
- Staying silent after a private warning and assuming the issue is closed.

## 7.6 Performance reviews: the brag book and the negotiation

### The concept

As a manager of twenty, the coach saw only "bits and pieces" of each person's work; "most of what I knew came from what they told me." The best-reviewed reports "actively chose and presented the facts," and if they brought evidence to back their self-evaluation, agreeing was easy. "You can't get rewarded for what your manager doesn't know."

🗣 **Coach's words:** "The Western workplace is not the humble Olympics. You don't get a gold medal for being the humblest employee. You just get forgotten."

His own parents urged him to be 谦虚 — modest — despite his growing up in Canada. Being modest in this system just keeps you invisible.

### Part A — The brag book (year-round)

A mentor showed the coach the brag book, which "helped me to rise in my own career." It is a running document of achievements for review, promotion, or job search.

Build it in four steps:
1. **One STAR story per project**, factual and objective — to maintain trust.
2. **Connect actions to company values and your development goals** (cultural fit + readiness for the next level).
3. **Show business impact.** Ask "How does my company make money? How does my work contribute to that?" (The same question the coach's savvy 5pm-leaving coworker asked to separate real outputs from busywork: "Effort is just an input, and inputs don't matter.")
4. **Receipts** — screenshots of Slack messages, emails, reports, code.

Template sections per entry: summary of why the project is a great experience; areas of development showing readiness for the target role's next level; values demonstrated; proof.

If your wins don't feel like wins, brain-dump every project from the last 3–5 years, score each on challenge/validation/impact, and rewrite the top one from "I updated a landing page and my manager was happy" to root cause → action → business number ("…drove five million dollars incremental revenue in the first year"). Feed the book weekly with a Friday email of three impact bullets — "raw material" for your manager to advocate for you in rooms you aren't in; "she's good" doesn't survive a conference room.

### Part B — Presenting growth areas

Managers "trust balanced self-awareness." Be honest, don't minimize the weakness, but pair every problem with a solution already in motion — "maximize your effort to work on it." Formula: name it + concrete action taken.

> "Stakeholder communication is one area that I'm developing, and I've already started daily updates in Teams to keep alignment."

### Part C — The review is a negotiation

Most people "walk into reviews hopeful, nervous, completely passive." A review is a negotiation over two things: negative feedback and future expectations.

**Negative feedback — questions first.** Defensiveness "only brings you further apart," and "if you want to influence your manager's opinion, you have to understand it first." Three questions:
- "Could you share a specific example of this?"
- "Is this more about the way that I do things or my communication?"
- "What would a stronger version of this look like to you?"

Then the "neat trick": reframe the feedback as situational — "less an issue about your personality and more about a situation that you can work on together."

```
I totally see what you mean about missing deadlines and I understand why that's important for the
Team. A lot of that came from new project handoffs without clear owners. Would it make sense if
I proposed a standard kickoff checklist to prevent that going forward?
```


If the feedback feels unfair, reset instead of reacting: "I'd like to take some time to reflect on this. Can we schedule a follow-up next week to review next steps?"

(Why managers respect the direct route: from the other side of the table, the coach found that soft feedback — "you did great, but…" — produced nodding and no change, and that the report felt shut out until he was direct and immediately followed with support. Managers who give you clear feedback are doing the harder, more respectful thing.)

**Future expectations — negotiate the input.** Salary and promotion can't be negotiated in the review; expectations can, and they are "the input that creates" salary and promotion. "Turn 'I hope they see my value' into 'we already agreed on what great looks like.'" Three questions:
- "What would exceeding expectations look like over the next 6 to 12 months?"
- "What's the next big challenge you'd like to see me take on?"
- "Which skills, if I level them up, would make me promotable?"

**Raising promotion.** "By leading three cross-functional projects, I've developed and showcased the skills needed for a formal senior analyst role. Could we discuss what steps are needed to make that transition?" If the answer is "not yet": "I understand. Then could we document measurable goals instead so I know exactly what to achieve before revisiting this next month?" — "That is how you turn no into not now."

Remember that the review is not the only checkpoint: regular check-ins turn "a single annual review into 12 checkpoints." And your manager may not even be the decision-maker on promotion; ask directly, "Who actually makes the call on this promotion?"

### Worked example: the roadmap that didn't exist

The coach's wife, a senior data scientist with two years at the top rating, was told she must lead cross-org initiatives to reach staff level. Her previous manager — "when you're performing at the highest level, you don't need to do even more" — spoke to the current one, who apologized and produced a realistic roadmap. She was promoted six months later. The point is not the intervention; it is that the expectation had never been made concrete until someone forced the question.

### Common mistakes

- Waiting for the manager to notice; competing in "the humble Olympics."
- Self-evaluations without receipts or a money line.
- Minimizing a weakness, or naming it with no action attached.
- Arguing with feedback before understanding it.
- Trying to negotiate the salary number instead of the expectations that produce it.

## 7.7 Manager types: train them, read them, and manage the irrational ones

### The concept: you train your manager

Replying instantly to 10pm emails trains "always on call." Saying "no problem" to last-minute dumps trains "poor planning has no consequences." Smiling through unfair criticism trains "disrespect is acceptable."

The coach's own case: a boss who nitpicked, messaged at night and swore at him on Slack, and who berated him for telling a candidate the team works overtime. He apologized out of fear — "Hey, I'm sorry about that. It won't happen again." His wife asked: "Why did you apologize for telling the truth?" He then spent a year "untraining" the boss: no weekend replies, pushback on last-minute scope changes, defending his work, standing up to unfair criticism. The first time caused a big argument; after that, "never another disrespectful comment… He respected me more."

"Boundaries are not selfish. They're your manual for how others should treat you" — but the manual must be written in the manager's language. That is what the matrix is for.

### The Manager Matrix

Two dimensions (adapted from the Blake–Mouton grid): **assertiveness** (need for control) and **structure** (reliance on systems/order). Four styles:

| | **Low structure** | **High structure** |
|---|---|---|
| **High assertiveness** | **Visionary** — motive: recognition ("a need to achieve in order to earn love") | **Micromanager** — motive: control |
| **Low assertiveness** | **Hands-off pleaser** — motive: belonging | **Team builder** — motive: harmony |

**Micromanager.** Constant check-ins, rewrites your work, responds poorly to surprises. Do: update proactively before asked; be reliable; ask for feedback early; align early on what good looks like. Don't: deliver your way without aligning on objectives; hide mistakes; disappear for long stretches.

**Visionary.** Bold ideas, unclear execution, sudden direction changes, frustrated by detail or hesitation. Do: bring structure (summarize the vision into next steps); bring solutions not problems; show genuine appreciation and excitement. Don't: drown them in details; threaten their vision; wait for step-by-step guidance.

**Team builder.** Invites feedback before deciding, regular check-ins, follows clear processes. Do: communicate regularly; use their templates and routines; build the team up. Don't: dismiss processes as bureaucracy; surprise them; go silent.

**Hands-off pleaser.** Avoids tough conversations, praises often with little actionable input, lets priorities drift. Do: clarify expectations yourself ("What does success look like for you here?"); be gentle but direct; offer structure. Don't: mistake kindness for leadership; fail to deliver (they won't tell you until too late); push them into conflict.

Caveats: no type is best — micromanagers help in a crisis, visionaries in innovation, pleasers rebuild psychological safety; most employees thrive under team builders; people shift under pressure. Use it "as a map… not as a label." Understanding what drives your boss lets you stop taking it personally. The coach admits he began as a hands-off pleaser himself.

### The power game: what each type takes, and how to take it back

Three of the four types steal a form of power from you — and "you don't get your power back by changing your boss. You get it back by changing how you respond to them."

**Micromanager → steals decision-making power (autonomy).** Resisting — slower replies, sharing less — makes it worse, because "micromanagers are just anxious people who are desperate for certainty"; less visibility means more control. Redirect the anxiety with regular proactive check-ins structured as **Status / Next move / Exceptions**. Client Gina, near quitting, started a daily 4pm update before her boss could ask:

```
Hey, quick update. X is delayed by a week due to data issues. I plan to deprioritize Y to get back
On track. I wanted to align with you on that plan first before moving forward.
```

**Hands-off pleaser → steals clarity.** They avoid feedback, delay decisions, reassure instead of committing. Pushing for a straight answer is exactly the pressure they flee. Instead "make clarity less risky to give" with a small, concrete, visible commitment — "action creates momentum." Not "I really need you to support me on this project," but: "Can I add you to the draft thread and tag you for feedback?"

**Visionary → steals planning power.** Priorities change weekly; defending the plan makes you "a negative Nancy." Visionaries "need someone who can turn their ideas into priorities and trade-offs and next steps." Show genuine excitement first, then run every idea through three checkpoints: (1) What's the outcome we're trying to create? (2) Where does this sit against existing priorities X and Y? (3) Should we move A or B forward as a next step? "You're not saying no… you're just turning their ideas into decisions."

> "I love the decision. Let's make sure that we can actually make it happen. Here's three things I'd like to discuss with you…"

### When the boss is irrational: manage the operating system

Standard managing-up advice assumes a rational boss with clear priorities. Many senior managers are "messy individuals whose overdeveloped strengths turn into volatility and control issues." The move is to stop managing them as a person and manage them as an operating system: "Operating systems don't have feelings… They just need the right inputs to produce the right outputs." Your job is to learn how the system runs, not to fix it.

1. **Map the pattern.** Keep a log of triggers and reactions. Patterns emerge: panics at surprises; rewards speed over perfection; needs control. "Their behavior is perfectly rational to them. You just don't know the rules."
2. **Design your inputs.** Speed-oriented boss: send rough drafts early — "Hey, can I get your early feedback?" Surprise-averse boss: send heads-ups — "Hey, heads up. This situation came up. I'm handling it my way unless you see a problem."
3. **Build power outside their orbit.** Relationships with another team's manager, the VP, mentors, potential future teams. Stress test: "If my boss leaves or gets fired or turns on me tomorrow, am I still valuable to the organization?"


**Worked example.** A Chinese professional's boss was both a micromanager (daily updates demanded; public chewing-out for missing one) and a serial boundary-eroder — "we mostly hired you to look pretty for our clients," gifts, offers to pay for her nails, talk of trips and massages. She needed his sponsorship, salary and growth, so quitting wasn't the answer. Mapping the pattern showed he read her personal sharing as something other than friendliness. She stopped sharing personal details and firmly redirected him to work topics; the emotional dumping "melted away." She booked coffee chats and led a project with the VP. Six months later she was promoted, with wider scope, out of the manager's shadow. The SVP later told a pointed story on a plane about a CEO fired over an affair — "as long as there's a power imbalance, the manager is always at fault" — a warning to her boss and a vote of confidence in her.

### Common mistakes

- Apologizing for telling the truth.
- Resisting a micromanager by going quiet.
- Demanding a straight answer from a pleaser.
- Defending the plan against a visionary instead of converting the idea into a decision.
- Trying to understand or fix an irrational boss's feelings instead of their inputs.
- Having no relationships that survive your boss leaving.

## 7.8 The PIP: agreement, not order

### The concept

A performance improvement plan comes in two kinds. **Good faith:** the manager wants you to improve and stay. **Bad faith:** the decision to let you go is already made and the PIP is labor-law compliance. Most clients assume they know which one they have; most are wrong.

🗣 **Coach's words:** "A PIP is not an order. It's an agreement, and agreements are negotiable."

### Step 1 — Diagnose with the commitment question

Book a 1:1 right after notification and ask for a commitment. "If they say anything other than a confident yes, it is a bad faith PIP."

```
I take your feedback seriously, and I want to succeed here. I'd like to clarify one thing with you,
Which can help with that. If I meet every expectation in this PIP, are you willing to commit today
That I can stay on this team long term?
```

### Step 2 — Good faith: make it SMART and get support in writing

Convert vague goals into specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound outcomes, and ask for concrete support. Managers must show their HRBP and their own manager that they supported you — so they have reason to agree. Get it in writing.

```
For me to succeed, I'd like to align with you on what "improved communication with stakeholders"
Actually means. Can we write down two or three specific measurable outcomes? And to hit these goals,
I'll need your help with giving me concrete feedback shortly after meetings. Can I count on that?
```

### Step 3 — Bad faith: negotiate the exit

You have leverage, because a PIP is painful for the manager: justify the hire, document, draft a plan, deliver it, update upward, then backfill and raise the hiring bar. The coach has done this to a direct report and knows the workload. Offer the easy way out at a price: voluntary resignation in exchange for severance and an agreed reference. The silver lining: "you're gonna be the one calling the shots."

```
It sounds like we may both be better served by a clean transition. I'd like to work with you on that.
If I voluntarily resign, I'd appreciate a gesture in return. I'd request X weeks of severance and
A neutral to positive reference we can agree on in writing.
```

### Before the PIP: is the role even needed?

If the relationship is sliding, one diagnostic question cuts through: "Hey, if I leave this role, would you even backfill it?" The coach asked it after a year in a role he knew was wrong in week one, got no straight answer, and paid with devolved skills and two more years to recover. Friends' advice to stay is "talking to themselves"; the right question is "What is staying doing to me?" Or, more bluntly: "Always either be learning or earning. If you aren't doing either, you should be leaving."

### After the PIP: data, not identity

Two years into Uber, the coach's manager put him on a PIP for communication and presentation skills. He drafted a furious email ("Respectfully, this is BS, and you just want to reduce headcount without severance") and left it in drafts. He couldn't tell his parents or friends; the shame traced to a culture where "my value lives in the eyes of others," and it followed him to the next job — thirty minutes of sweat-soaked prep for every thirty-minute 1:1. Shame "will cost you a raise that you never asked for or a promotion that you never pursued." It ended the day he posted the PIP publicly on LinkedIn. You don't need a public post, but you need honesty with yourself: "A PIP or any setback is data. A failure is data." "When you know who you are, you get to choose what the data means to you."

### Common mistakes

- Signing the PIP as presented, as if it were an order.
- Assuming you know which kind it is without asking the commitment question.
- Accepting vague goals ("improve communication") with no support clause.
- Carrying the shame into the next job.

## Practice set

### Drills

**Drill 1 — Zero-status 1:1 agenda (7.1).** Rewrite your next 1:1 agenda with no status items. Under each of the four headings — Expectations, Priorities, Resources, Development — write one question from the corpus. Add one resource ask in menu form ("If you want A by next week, I'll need Y. Without Y, I can deliver B."). Bring it and ask the "top of mind" question verbatim; write the answer at the top of your task list.

**Drill 2 — Unspoken-worries list (7.2).** For your current project, list three worries your manager likely has but hasn't said. Today, send one one-line status update that answers one of them.

**Drill 3 — Tracking notification rewrite (7.2).** Take your last long status message to your manager. Rewrite it in three sentences: "The main thing is [status]." → one line of cause → "The reason I'm telling you is [decision needed]." Then rewrite it once more in the four-line status/cause/fix/ETA shape. Compare.

**Drill 4 — Sorry audit (7.2).** Find the last message where you wrote "sorry" to your boss about a team outcome. Rewrite it as an acknowledgement ("I know this is frustrating") plus a forward plan. For one week, log every "sorry" and convert it to a "thanks."

**Drill 5 — Close the loop (7.3).** Identify one piece of advice a leader gave you in the last month. Act on it. Send the three-sentence loop-closing message naming the specific result.

**Drill 6 — Flattery → curiosity rewrite (7.3).** Write down the last compliment you paid a senior leader. Rewrite it as a curiosity question about *how* they did the thing.

**Drill 7 — The sandwich (7.4).** Take a real request you wish you had declined. Write the three parts: acknowledge their pressure; decline anchored to your manager's *actual* current top priority; then all three path-forward options (delegate / deprioritize / postpone). Read it aloud until the tone is warm.

**Drill 8 — Flat → felt (7.4).** For a change you want from your manager, write the three curiosity questions you'd ask first. Then draft the flat version of your ask, and rewrite it with one concrete past example, one cost statement, and one "here's how it helps your plan" sentence.

**Drill 9 — Accusation → observation (7.5).** Take one criticism you have of your manager. Convert it into a two-sentence observation (what you saw, when, the team outcome you worry about) ending with "What are you seeing from your side?" Then script the permission ask.

**Drill 10 — Role-play: the private conversation after public criticism (7.5).** Recall a recent public criticism. Fill in the three-part opener. A partner plays the manager with these lines: *"Honestly I don't think it was a big deal."* → you restate your feeling without blame. *"Well, since you ask — this isn't the first time."* → you ask for the specific earlier incident and listen. Time-box to five minutes.

**Drill 11 — First brag-book entry (7.6).** For your top project this quarter, write one STAR entry, one sentence linking it to how the company makes money, one value demonstrated, and attach one receipt.

**Drill 12 — Role-play: negative feedback in the review (7.6).** Partner plays manager: *"One concern is that you've missed a few deadlines this quarter."* You respond with the three questions, then the situational reframe with a proposed fix. Partner then says: *"I'm not sure you're ready for the next level."* You reply with the "no → not now" line asking for documented measurable goals.

**Drill 13 — Place your manager (7.7).** Using three observed behaviors, place your manager on the assertiveness × structure grid. Pick one "do" and one "don't" from their quadrant for this week. Name which power they take (autonomy, clarity, planning) and script the matching move: a status/next/exceptions update, a small visible ask, or the three-checkpoint response to their latest idea. Separately, name one behavior you've been "training" (e.g., late-night replies) that you will stop this week.

**Drill 14 — Operating-system log (7.7).** Keep a two-column log for two weeks: trigger → boss's reaction. At the end, write one "input" change per pattern and name one person above or beside your boss to book time with. Answer the stress test in writing: "If my boss left tomorrow, am I still valuable here?"

**Drill 15 — SMART-ify vague feedback (7.8).** Take any vague performance feedback you've received ("improve communication"). Rewrite it as two or three specific, measurable, time-bound outcomes plus the specific manager support you'd request.

## Cheat sheet

**1:1 = alignment, not status.** Expectations ("Am I solving the right problem? How do we define success?") · Priorities ("What's top of mind for you and why?") · Resources (menu: "If you want A, I need Y; without Y, I deliver B") · Development (quarterly: "One strength to double down on, one weakness to fix?"). Disagree out loud; never fake alignment.

**Remove uncertainty.** Pre-answer unspoken worries → weekly one-liner ("On track. Recap + next steps.") → off-track = tracking notification (status / cause / fix / new ETA) → never a problem without two paths. Scissor: "The main thing is… / The reason I'm telling you is…" SCQA for decisions.

**Blame-proof ownership.** Responsibility ≠ liability · Document before you dive (teammate → manager) · Acknowledge, don't apologize · Replace "sorry" with "thanks."

**Trust = competence × care.** Seasonal thanks · Close the loop with the result · Curiosity, not flattery · "Leaders need to poop too." New job: 30-day listening tour before acting.

**Say no from the bank balance.** Deposits = solving what they couldn't solve without you. Sandwich: acknowledge pressure → decline anchored to *their* top priority → delegate / deprioritize / postpone. Can't say no? Pick yeses that build skill or visibility; then commit fully.

**Change a decision.** Ask first (why / what for / ideal vision) → make them feel the cost (concrete, visual, tied to their goal, with a proposal) → accept the outcome; don't take them home.

**Leave announcement.** Acknowledge → Assure (transition plan) → Affirm → Align (touch points).

**Feedback upward.** Connect (permission + positive intent) → shared reality (observation + "What are you seeing from your side?") → improve together ("What should we do differently? How can I do my part?"). Only for difficult-but-reachable leaders.

**After public criticism.** Withdraw, cool down, request a private 1:1 within 24h. "I believe your intention was ___. When you ___, I felt ___. I'd love to understand your perspective and share mine."

**Reviews.** Brag book: STAR per project · values + development · business impact ("How does my company make money?") · receipts. Growth area = name it + action in motion. Negative feedback → 3 questions → situational reframe → propose fix. Negotiate expectations, not salary: "What does exceeding look like in 6–12 months? Next big challenge? Which skills make me promotable?" "No" → "not now" with documented goals. 12 checkpoints, not one review.

**Manager matrix (assertiveness × structure).** Micromanager (control): update before asked, never hide mistakes → status / next / exceptions. Visionary (recognition): excitement first, then 3 checkpoints (outcome? vs. priorities X/Y? move A or B?). Team builder (harmony): use their process, never surprise. Hands-off pleaser (belonging): small visible asks, clarify success yourself. Map, not label.

**Irrational boss = operating system.** Map triggers → design inputs (early drafts / heads-ups) → build power outside their orbit. Test: "If my boss leaves tomorrow, am I still valuable?"

**PIP.** Ask: "If I meet every expectation, will you commit today I can stay long term?" Anything but a confident yes = bad faith. Good faith → SMART outcomes + support, in writing. Bad faith → voluntary resignation for severance + agreed reference. Setback = data, not identity.

**Do:** surface disagreement early · update before asked · bring options · ask before telling · anchor to their priority · document help · keep receipts.
**Don't:** fake alignment · go silent · say "I can't" · apologize for the truth · ambush with feedback · compete in the humble Olympics · sign a PIP unread.
