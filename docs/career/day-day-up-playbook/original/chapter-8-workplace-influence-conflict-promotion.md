# Chapter 8 — Workplace Influence, Conflict & Promotion

> This chapter covers the part of the job that nobody in your engineering degree, your MBA, or your parents' dinner-table lessons prepared you for: getting your work *seen*, moving people who don't report to you, disagreeing without damage, absorbing responsibility without absorbing blame, and turning "you're doing great" into an actual promotion. The coach's core diagnosis runs through every video here: Chinese and other non-native professionals in North America are trained to do 10/10 and communicate 5/10, while their Western co-workers do 5/10 and say 11/10. The gap is not English and it is not talent. It is a set of learnable skills — visibility, influence, calibrated disagreement, ownership hygiene, political literacy, and promotion mechanics — and this chapter teaches them as habits with scripts.

**What you'll be able to do**

- Explain why "invisible impact is indistinguishable from no impact," and run a daily / weekly / quarterly visibility routine that does not feel like bragging.
- Take credit by *celebrating* (team, insight, collaborator) and maintain a brag book with receipts that makes your manager's job of advocating for you easy.
- Influence without authority by finding the one pressure point, learning what that person wants, and helping them get it; and structure a persuasive ask with ACT.
- Disagree using contrasting, mutual purpose, and the three "don't claim more than you know" swaps, so the relationship survives the argument.
- Choose a conflict style you will respect yourself for, deliver hard truth with support, and grow your "collaboration capacity" with people you dislike.
- Keep the upside of ownership without the blame: responsibility not liability, document before you dive, acknowledge instead of apologizing.
- Identify who actually decides your promotion, learn how they decide, and do the work that decision requires — including building sponsors, informants and mentees.

## 8.1 Visibility is a skill, not a personality

### The concept

The coach's starting claim is blunt: the number-one factor in who gets promoted is not ability but visibility. He cites an HBR study of more than 240 leaders in which visibility ranked first — and he insists visibility is a skill, not a personality trait and not loudness. The same idea is stated as an equation: "Influence is the product of what you do multiplied by what people know". Multiply by zero and you get zero.

He sketches a two-axis model — communication versus competence. *Smooth talkers* are high talk, low doing (fake smile, big words, no delivery). *Quiet doers* — where most Chinese professionals sit — are high doing, low talk. The target is the top-right corner, the *smart talker*: competent and communicative. The dichotomy between quiet talent and smooth talk is false; the most successful people do both.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Results whisper, communication amplifies."

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Invisible impact is indistinguishable from no impact."

### Why it works — the manager's perspective

The coach managed 20 people and admits he saw only "bits and pieces" of each person's work; most of what he knew came from what they told him. When someone brought facts to back a self-evaluation, agreeing was easy. Your manager needs *raw material* to advocate for you in rooms you are not in — "she's good" does not survive a conference room. And leaders are competing for attention: the workplace "is loud and crowded and full of people competing for the attention of leaders who have more priorities than they have hours".

His own cautionary tale: he built the case for and then built a 100-person data engineering org from zero, and got "meets expectations" because he never communicated the story. Leadership's summary was "John supported a data cleanup".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "The Western workplace is not the humble Olympics. You don't get a gold medal for being the humblest employee. You just get forgotten."

### The method: daily / weekly / quarterly

The coach packages visibility into three rhythms:

1. **Daily — ask one question per meeting.** Choose from three types:
   - Clarity: "What does success look like here?"
   - Priority: "If we could only focus on one of these things, which one matters most right now?"
   - Impact: "Who else does this affect?"
2. **Weekly — the Friday summary email.** Send your manager three impact bullets. Paste your week's Slack messages and notes into an AI tool; it takes about ten minutes. This is the raw material they use to advocate for you.
3. **Quarterly — a skip-level relationship built on usefulness.** (1) Assess your recent work for measurable impact; (2) identify which senior leader's team faces the same challenge; (3) reach out and offer the solution.

The story behind step 3: at Shopify's 500-person data org he fixed project management for his team, offered the fix to the engineering VP, was called out at all-hands, and was pulled into VP-level opportunities — without ever asking for advocacy. In the networking video the same episode is told as an AI project-management tool shared with engineering; a VP Slacked him that evening, they co-built for three days, and the VP later expanded his scope. "I just made my work impossible for him to ignore".

The third leadership micro-habit, "do things and tell people," is the same principle as a daily question: "How can I make this work more visible?" — public Slack/Teams updates, early drafts to your manager, emails celebrating wins, "here's what I learned" notes.

### Worked example — the Friday email

```
Subject: Week of [date] — three things

1. [Result + number]: Shipped the churn dashboard; ops now sees weekly churn
   without a manual pull (saves ~4 hrs/week for the team).
2. [Decision unblocked]: Flagged the web-user data gap; we agreed to validate
   before the QBR so the churn number isn't overstated.
3. [Cross-team]: Shared the PM template with Eng; VP asked for a walkthrough.

Next week: [one line].
```
(Structure per; the bullets are illustrative fill-ins, not the coach's.)

### Common mistakes

- Treating visibility as "loud." It is a routine, not a volume setting.
- Waiting to be noticed. Being modest (谦虚) "just keeps you invisible".
- Asking your skip-level for advocacy instead of *offering* them something useful.
- Describing "hard worker / team player" as your brand — everyone in the West is a team player; managers value influence and results. Translate: "I lead with influence" and "I'm results driven," each backed by one example.

## 8.2 Taking credit: celebrate, don't brag — and keep the receipts

### The concept

The coach grew up on his father's tortoise-and-hare lesson: "never celebrate too soon, or you will fall behind." A manager later made "increase team visibility" one of his success metrics — speak up, share wins with other teams to earn credibility. After what he thought was an average presentation, the GM shook his hand and called it the best he'd seen about the team's work.

The distinction that unlocks this for people who hate self-promotion: **bragging** is "look what I did" — everyone hates it. **Celebrating** puts the spotlight on others while owning your result and your role. Celebration signals to leadership that "the engine is working" and they should trust the team more. And the hare lost not because he celebrated but "because he forgot that there was still somewhere to go".

### The method: three celebrations

| Trigger | Template |
|---|---|
| **The Team** — after a team win | "I'm so proud of how our team navigated X challenge. By moving fast, we managed to get Y results. And I wanted leadership to know that the engine is working." |
| **The Insight** — when the win is an idea others can reuse | "Hey everyone, big win today. We cracked X problem. The key insight was Y. I'm excited about the potential to expand our impact into Z space by applying this insight." |
| **The Collaborator** — sharing credit with a core contributor | "Huge shout-out to [colleague] for the help on this project. We just went live. You are a champion of cross-functional collaboration, and you played a pivotal role in securing five million dollars in benefits." |

Note what every template does: a real number, a named person or team, and your role stated without apology.

### The brag book

Celebration is the public channel; the brag book is the private ledger you bring to reviews and promotion talks. A mentor showed the coach the format and it "helped me to rise in my own career". Build it year-round, because "you can't get rewarded for what your manager doesn't know".

Contents:

1. **One STAR story per project**, factual and objective (to protect trust).
2. **Connection to company values and your development goals** (cultural fit; readiness for the next level).
3. **Business impact** — answer "How does my company make money? How does my work contribute to that?"
4. **Receipts** — screenshots of Slack messages, emails, reports, code.
5. **Areas of development** — be honest, don't minimize, and pair each with a concrete action already in motion: "Stakeholder communication is one area that I'm developing. I've started daily updates in Teams to keep alignment."

If your wins "just felt like work," recover them with the three-step "Career CPR": brain-dump every project from the last 3–5 years; score each High/Medium/Low on challenge, validation, impact; then think like a boss and translate to business value. The reframe example: from "I updated a company website's landing page, and my manager was happy" to "Our product launch was underperforming. I discovered that poor landing page conversion was the root cause. So I rebuilt the page from the ground up… It lifted conversion by one percent, helped us reach our launch target, and drove five million dollars incremental revenue in the first year".

### Common mistakes

- Celebrating only your own name. Spotlight others; own your role.
- Bringing feelings, not facts, to a review — bring receipts.
- Minimizing your development area. Name it and show the action you've already taken.
- Expecting your manager to remember. Regular check-ins turn "a single annual review into 12 checkpoints".

## 8.3 Influence without authority: pressure points, ACT, and care

### The concept

Influence without authority is hard because "you're trading nothing for influence" — you control neither salary nor promotion; you control only where you apply pressure. The coach's story: his bizops team was tasked by the CPO to revamp a slow technical-hiring process. The recruiting team felt threatened; a recruiting manager dodged meetings and got defensive; explaining frameworks and data ("be more modern, more data-informed") made them lean further away. When he stopped chasing, he realized the pressure was spread across the wrong people. The real pressure point was the VP of R&D, who set the vision for engineering and data science. He opened with a question, not a pitch: "What does good hiring mean to you? Not what it means to our CPO, just what you care about?" Her answer was diversity and inclusion and being seen as a modern, people-first leader — none of the funnel or time-to-offer language. So they ran a referral campaign for underrepresented candidates and DM'd LinkedIn profiles late at night. Once she felt supported, she championed them into leadership meetings and the recruiting team's resistance faded.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Influence is not about convincing others to do what you think is right. It's about recognizing that right means something different to each person."

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Maybe influencing without authority is just the act of manipulating others. With kindness."

### Method A: the pressure-point framework

1. **Identify the pressure point** — the one person who actually sets the vision others follow.
2. **Understand what they want** — ask an open question about *their* definition of success.
3. **Help them get it** — then let them champion you.

### Method B: ACT for the idea nobody hears

Early in his career the coach rehearsed ten times in the mirror, spoke in a VP meeting with his hands shaking on his notes — and got "silence… people just move on as if I'd never spoken." His diagnosis: being ignored is a failure of influence, not likability. "The more you try to be liked at work, the more they ignore you".

**ACT**
- **A — Assert credibility with nuance.** Sound confident but honestly acknowledge limits; "when you lead with nuance, people lower their defenses." *"Given the small sample size, I'd caution that results aren't statistically definitive. That said, we're seeing a strong directional signal that aligns with last quarter's trends."*
- **C — Contextualize with social proof.** Nobody wants to be first into uncertainty. Instead of "I promise you this is a great idea": *"Product and ops have been early adopters and feedback's been positive."*
- **T — Trigger a low-commitment action.** Newton's first law — objects in motion stay in motion. *"Can I add you to the draft thread so you can weigh in if you'd like?"*

The same low-commitment move is prescribed for a hands-off manager who avoids decisions: "Can I add you to the draft thread and tag you for feedback?" beats "I really need you to support me on this project".

### Method C: trust = competence × care

Client Sam, a commodity trader "paid to be right," got his first poor review in ten years after the COO complained. The feedback that hides in reviews as "poor communication skills" is really "you're right and nobody wants to work with you" — and the first thought, "is it my English?", is wrong: "It never is". The coach cites Susan Fiske's research that people judge warmth before competence — "friend or foe comes first" — replicated across roughly 50 countries including China. Chinese professionals were taught that logic and being right matter most; care felt like "拍马屁 in a different costume." It isn't. "Care is a skill."

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Trust equals competence times care. Not added, multiplied."

Three steps:

1. **Be curious** — before responding, ask why they said that. Sam asked, *"Why is cost the most important thing right now?"* and learned the COO was protecting margins and preventing layoffs in volatile markets — Sam had been "arguing with a man who was quietly trying to save everyone's jobs."
2. **Show care before you convince** — say aloud what they are carrying: *"Wow, it sounds like you are under intense pressure right now. I want to make sure whatever we decide actually helps you with that."* "The COO didn't need Sam to agree with him. He needed Sam to see him."
3. **Decide: win the argument or win the relationship.** Sam accepted centralization for now and plans to pilot a distributed model when the company stabilizes. The COO's report: "something about me has changed."

### Method D: close the loop after someone helps you

The moment after receiving advice or an intro is "where a real connection is missed when it could have been born." The helper wonders "did she try what I suggested? Was that even useful?" Citing the Benjamin Franklin effect — someone who helps you concludes they must like you — the coach says a short follow-up reinforces the helper's self-story and "makes them want to help you again". Script: *"Last time you told me to try XYZ. I actually did, and it helped so much. Here's how it made things better."* The leader-facing version: *"I'm just checking in to close the loop on your helpful advice earlier. I tried what you suggested and it made a big difference by [specific impact]. Thank you again for your guidance"*. With leaders, replace flattery ("wow boss, you're the best" — "textbook 拍马屁") with curiosity: *"I noticed how you advocated for more resources for our team in that meeting. How do you communicate persuasively like that?"*.

### Common mistakes

- Pushing harder with logic on a defensive team. More evidence produced more defensiveness.
- Pitching before asking. The first meeting with the VP was a question, not a deck.
- Trying to be liked instead of credible, contextualized and actionable.
- Being right at zero care — the product is zero.
- Going quiet after help — the connection dies in silence.

## 8.4 Disagreeing without damage

### The concept

The coach was taught "indirect praise, direct criticism." His teachers wrote "Jonathan has opportunities to demonstrate more initiative"; his father skipped the straight A's and said "You're too quiet" — direct, but not objectively accurate. Western culture runs the other way: direct, generous praise; indirect criticism. Strong opinions are fine. What triggers defensiveness is "deterministic judgments about other people."

The fastest way to lose respect at work is not a big mistake — it's being perceived as disrespectful, at which point people defend their self-worth instead of hearing the feedback. So the two conditions for a safe disagreement are **mutual respect** and **mutual purpose**.

### Method A: contrasting (for mutual respect)

Late at night on a pitch deck, Tom from sales wanted to cut half of the coach's financial model. The coach snapped. Tom's reply made "all of my defensiveness just melt away": *"Hey John, I'm not saying your work doesn't matter. It's brilliant and I rely on it. I just think if we overload them, they'll stop listening before they even see how good it is."* Resolution: "you bring the facts, I'll bring the story".

**Contrasting** = (1) the *don't* — say what you don't mean; (2) the *do* — say what you do mean.

> "I don't mean to say your idea isn't good. Your ideas are really valuable here. I just want to make sure that we present it in a way that lands as clearly as it deserves."

### Method B: mutual purpose (go one level higher)

Disagreements are "almost never about the actual goals and almost always about how to achieve them". (1) Find the purpose behind their idea — "why is this important to you?"; (2) invent a mutual purpose one level higher.

> "I'm hearing from you that you really want the VP to be confident in our numbers. Is that right? Okay. Well, we both want the VP to align on our recommendation. Let's find a way to make both the numbers and the story work together."

The interview-facing version of the same idea is the "abstraction ladder": "If we can't agree on a timeline, can we agree on the broader project goal? If we can't agree on that, can we agree on our team's overall goal?" — up to "help the company make money." "There's always a shared goal if you're willing to look high enough". And the underlying stance: "When two capable people disagree, I don't assume one of us is wrong" — disagreement is information about differing priorities, information or assumptions; ask what concern their approach addresses, lay out two options with trade-offs, then "disagree and commit".

### Method C: don't claim more than you know (three swaps)

Both cuts of the same lesson:

| You claim to know… | Sounds like | Swap to |
|---|---|---|
| **People** — "You're not detail-oriented." | "You've already decided who they are" — "one of the fastest ways to destroy trust." It may be three things seen on one deliverable on one day. | "When you miss the daily update to the client, it can come across as a lack of attention to detail." (When X happens, you appear Y.) |
| **The truth** — "This is wrong," "Actually," "This won't work." | Perception spoken as truth. The thermostat fight at 22°C: "it's cold" is "a report on you." | "I see where you're coming from. Here's what I see differently." |
| **What happened** — "Why did you do that?", "You shouldn't." | Interrogation; "you've made a mistake." | "Can you walk me through your thinking?" |

A fourth claim is the reflexive apology: a client blurts "I'm sorry" whenever her manager sounds unhappy — claiming responsibility she hasn't established. "Accuracy is not softness… The most skilled communicators… sound more calibrated".

### Method D: the acknowledge-then-hold pattern

For pushback situations the coach demonstrates one reusable three-beat shape: acknowledge warmly → state the constraint or feeling tied to a shared goal → offer a path. When told to "stop overreacting, relax": *"We respect each other, right? So I'm sure you didn't mean it that way. See, when you attacked me instead of the problem, it felt personal. And it made me uncomfortable. But I'd like to reset because I value working through disagreements with you, not against you."* In a meeting, swap "I'm sorry, but I disagree, and here's why" for "I see your perspective. I'd like to offer another viewpoint for us to consider".

### Common mistakes

- Opening with the verdict ("this is wrong") instead of the contrast.
- Arguing the method when you haven't agreed the goal.
- Diagnosing a person from one data point.
- Confusing calibration with softness; calibrated language is *more* precise, not weaker.

## 8.5 Conflict: choose the style you'll respect yourself for

### The concept

"Workplace conflict is the cost of real decisions." Every response has a cost: **fight** (name the issue clearly, risk discomfort), **flight** (say something but not the real thing, protect feelings, leave room for misunderstanding), **freeze** (avoid, delay, hope it goes away — the coach's own default). Fighting too hard leaves scars; flying too far means never coming back; freezing too long means never changing. "Assertive communication" training rarely felt authentic to him. So the only decision rule he offers: **"Which path will I respect myself for later?"**. The Bilibili cut on personal conflict frames the same test — "The measure of winning a conflict isn't about what you say. It's about whether you feel good about yourself when it's over" — with a third option, *ignore*: acknowledge what they said and do what you want anyway ("Hey, I hear you. Good day to you, too").

### Worked example: hard truth followed by support

Managing a team of ten, the coach had a superstar report whose filler words were losing executives' patience — executives have little patience for hesitation, and weak language undermines strong skills. His soft feedback ("you did great, but your speaking could improve") produced nodding and no change; the vagueness preserved his comfort at the cost of hers and eroded trust. She finally challenged him: *"John, I noticed that you are not bringing me into these presentations. Is there a reason that you don't want me there?"* / *"…you said you wanted to support me, but I feel like you're shutting me out."* He chose fight:

> "I want you to succeed. And I can see the way that I've been communicating with you hasn't been getting through. So I'm going to be real clear with you right now. When you say uh every few seconds, you sound incompetent."
>
> "I know this sounds rough, but I want to help you to show up as who you really are, which is a kick-ass leader, and I have an idea on how we can get there."

The plan was five minutes of practice at the end of every 1:1; trust was rebuilt and she improved fast. "I did not leave her there with the harsh truth".

**Direct-feedback structure:** clear statement → acknowledge it's harsh → offer help and a recurring plan.

### Working with the colleague you can't stand

Ann, a data analyst, had "Karen" — a non-manager stakeholder who assigned her data-entry work and criticized her publicly. Ann cried at lunch and wanted to quit. The coach's reframe: the constraint on your career isn't Karen, it's your **collaboration capacity** — the size of your cup. "Karen always shows up again." You don't control who pours into your cup; you control how big the cup is. Everyone can work with people they like; the few who can work with people they dislike built bigger cups. Collaboration "is not about liking people. It's about getting things done, even when there's friction".

Three options: **A suffer, B quit, C navigate.** Option-C tactics:
- Escalate out-of-scope demands to your manager and "borrow his authority."
- Reframe the other person's behavior as *their* need for control; laugh off public challenges outwardly and inwardly.
- Stop letting them "live rent-free in your head" — you, not they, pay the cost. Every "this is so unfair" thought is you pouring into your own cup.
- Acceptance mantra: "I don't like you. And I commit to working with you because my cup is growing." (Amazon's "disagree and commit"; others are allowed to disagree with and even dislike you.)

The same acceptance closes the Jen-and-"Lady" case: after doing everything right — asking the manager's goals first, then making a concrete, visual ask ("a project that should have taken a week took a month… half of a functional resource once you subtract the energy that we will spend on friction") — you may still not get it. Ruminating means "taking them home with you every single night".

### When your manager snaps at you in public

"Wendy," promoted four times in ten years by a Devil-Wears-Prada boss, was called "immature" in front of senior leaders and swallowed it "like most of us Chinese do." Days earlier she'd made an awkward joke, been pulled aside, and stayed quiet thinking that was mature — but the manager didn't move on. "When a manager loses it, it is almost never just about that moment." Method: don't fight back in the moment; withdraw and cool down; within 24 hours request a private 1:1 with two goals — their perspective and yours. Opener: *"I believe your intention was [something positive]. And when you [specific behavior], it makes me feel [honest feeling]. I'd love to explore and understand your perspective better, and I also wanted to share mine"*.

### Common mistakes

- Softened feedback that changes nothing and quietly costs trust.
- Delivering the hard truth and leaving — no support, no plan.
- Treating suffer/quit as the only options; Option C exists.
- Letting a public blow-up "sit and rot" — "never go to bed angry".
- Leading a request with "I don't want to work with her" — sounds self-centered and hands your manager a problem they'd rather ignore.

## 8.6 Ownership without blame

### The concept

Client Kim, a client-facing AI engineer: "The harder I work, the more blame I get." Ownership keeps her up at 2 a.m.; Chinese professionals work weekends for ownership's applause, and when things go wrong "suddenly it's all on you".

### The method — three moves

**1. Assume responsibility, not liability.** Responsibility = owning your piece and doing it well. Liability = taking the blame for the whole. Don't assume outcomes that were never yours (his childhood group projects in Canada taught him this).

> ❌ "Workstream B is behind schedule, so I'll make sure that it all gets done."
> ✅ "I've made sure workstream A is done and on track. I want to flag that workstream B is behind schedule. I think we should align on how we can support it to get back on track."

**2. Document before you dive.** "Undocumented help is invisible help" — if the project fails, all anyone sees is your fingerprints. Two messages, in order:

> To the teammate: "Part A is done and I have some time. I noticed that part B is tough. Do you want me to take a pass? Your call."
> To your manager: "Quick update for you. Part A is done. Teammate okayed my help on part B, so I'm stepping in to support. Let me know if you have any concerns."

"Visibility is as simple as raising your hand before you solve the problem." If it works, you get the credit; if it doesn't, the boundary is on record.

**3. Acknowledge, don't apologize.** "Every sorry is a quiet confession." Apologizing accepts blame; acknowledging accepts the situation. Remind your boss you're on the same team, then pivot to next steps.

> ❌ "I'm so sorry. Unfortunately, the client terminated the deal. I should have flagged this earlier."
> ✅ "The client went another way, and I'm just as frustrated as you are. In our sync last week, we discussed how Part B was behind. And despite my efforts to step in and support, it just wasn't enough. Here's what I think we can do going forward."

### The language of blame-absorption: sorry / just / try

The reflexive vocabulary that invites blame is the same vocabulary that signals low power. "The most polite person in the room has the least power." Three deletions:
- **sorry → thanks**: "Thanks for pointing that out. I'll make that change."
- **just → delete**: "Hey, checking in. How are things going?"
- **try → commit to the outcome + update clause**: "I expect to have this done by tomorrow. I'll update you by end of day if anything changes."

"Uncertainty kills careers faster than being occasionally wrong."

Publishing your prioritization decision is a related protection. When someone asks "why didn't we do X?": *"I shared this with you in the working group last week, along with my rationale. I'm sure this isn't the first time that you're hearing about this, is it?"*.

### Common mistakes

- Volunteering for the whole outcome to look heroic.
- Helping quietly, then being surprised your fingerprints are the only ones visible.
- Apologizing in high-stakes moments — it reads as confession.
- Apologizing for telling the truth. The coach once did, out of fear; his wife asked "why did you apologize for telling the truth?" He then "untrained" the boss — no weekend replies, pushback on scope changes, standing up to unfair criticism. One big argument, then "He respected me more".

## 8.7 Politics, sponsors and access

### The concept

After running performance reviews, the coach names three truths:

1. **"Performance has nothing to do with how hard you work. Effort is just an input, and inputs don't matter."** Only outputs that move the business count. The co-worker who leaves at 5 asks "how does this business make money? how does my work connect?" — and drops low-value busywork onto people "who don't understand the game."
2. **"Soft skills are a multiplier for hard skills."** Senior leaders haven't touched their core hard skill in years. Engineer A (brilliant, can't explain, hard to work with) vs Engineer B (weaker technically, clear, collaborative): a director whose job is 90% people picks B.
3. **"You must be political to succeed."** Politics = "the unwritten rules of how influence, relationships, and communication work." He built side projects nights and weekends expecting celebration; his boss said, "John, it's time to drop what you're doing because we actually have priorities to achieve here." The problem: no sponsor, and ideas not connected to business goals.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Hard work got you here, but it won't get you there."

### Access, not networking

"Chinese professionals don't have a networking problem, we have an access problem." While we collect coffee chats, "people who have access are getting promoted and pulled into key projects that we've never even heard about." The question for any coffee chat: **"What access do I want from this relationship?"** Three relationships:

| Relationship | What it gives | How to build it | Script |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sponsor** | Opportunity — uses their reputation to open doors. Citing Sylvia Ann Hewlett: sponsorship predicts promotion and salary growth about as strongly as hours worked; only 1 in 5 men and 1 in 8 women have one. "Sponsors don't find talented people. They find visible people." | Assess recent work → ask how the target's team could benefit → reach out (or via their EA). | "Hey, my team just solved X. I think your team might be dealing with something similar. Would you be open to a quick chat so I can share what worked?" |
| **Informant** | Information. The day before Shopify's 20% layoff a director peer asked whether the SVP had gone quiet; the coach had been pulled into a session; the peer was laid off next day. "Luck is not a strategy. Information is." Trust must run both ways. | Ask who has visibility you lack and who's been generous → book time with a specific question plus something to offer. | "Hey, I'm navigating this situation right now and I know that you've been through something similar. I would love twenty minutes of your time. I've also got some things that I'd like to share with you that I think you'd find useful." |
| **Mentee** | Reputation. Immigrants "rarely get the leadership title until we've already proven we can do the job." Mentoring builds leadership reputation before the title and a base of allies; "I'd done the job long before I had the job." | Volunteer as onboarding buddy, peer reviewer, or sounding board. | — |

### Build power outside your boss's orbit

When a boss is irrational, "stop managing them as a person and manage them as an operating system": map the pattern (trigger → reaction), design your inputs (rough drafts early for speed-lovers: "Hey, can I get your early feedback?"; heads-ups for the surprise-averse: "Hey, heads up. This situation came up. I'm handling it my way unless you see a problem"), and **build power outside their orbit**. Stress test: *"If my boss leaves or gets fired or turns on me tomorrow, am I still valuable to the organization?"* A coachee whose senior boss eroded boundaries (comments about being hired "to look pretty," gifts, offers to pay for nails) needed his sponsorship and couldn't simply quit; she stopped sharing personal details, redirected him to work, built a relationship with the VP through coffee chats and leading a project, and was promoted within six months.

Reputation rules from the same corpus: never write what you wouldn't want on the front page of the Wall Street Journal; never say about someone what you wouldn't say to their face; gossipers gossip about you too; "HR is not your friend. They work for the company".

### Common mistakes

- Measuring yourself by input (hours) when the scoreboard counts business output.
- Absorbing busywork dropped by people who understand the game.
- Collecting coffee chats with no access goal.
- Building an entire career inside one manager's orbit.
- Treating "political" as dirty. It's the unwritten rulebook; ignoring it has a cost.

## 8.8 Leadership micro-habits: head-up, not head-down

### The concept

"Leading is a verb… not a title that you're given". At Shopify's data leadership team, Chinese teammates did the hard research and debugging ("call Xiao Tong," "Xiao Li and Xiaoyang will stay late") while decisions on what to work on were made almost exclusively by white colleagues — attributed not to discrimination but to how Chinese professionals see themselves and small daily reinforcing habits. "Drowning in tasks but starving for recognition" comes from not understanding what impact means to the person who signs your paycheck. "Your head down work got you here. Your head up leadership will take you further."

### Method A: three micro-habits

1. **Daily top three.** Before closing the laptop, write tomorrow's top three priorities; next day circle #1 and do it first. "If you don't decide what matters to your success, others will decide for you." Five minutes; people see focus and calm and treat you like a leader. His story: as a new manager on a 12-person, $30M compliance remediation project, he pulled all-nighters configuring DocuSign and asked why everyone else went home at 5. His *real* top three were lead the team, see around corners (the regulatory/tax landscape), and align leadership. Result: delegation, expertise that cut remediation time in half, more headcount and budget, and a full week of vacation with the team running perfectly.
2. **Weekly OODA loop** (John Boyd). Ask: What did we observe? How does it change our orientation/strategy? What do we decide? What action? Treat each decision as a hypothesis; Uber "didn't just move fast and break things. We were learning faster than everyone else" — launch a city, fight regulators, tune surge pricing on data, playbook the next city. Ask "what's the smallest loop I can run this week?" People began treating him as someone who "sees around corners."
3. **Do things and tell people.** See 8.1.

### Method B: align, apply, speak with certainty

1. **Align with leadership's vision (15 minutes).** Feed all-hands transcripts, QBR docs and strategy documents into a company-approved AI; ask for near/long-term goals and why, explicit/implicit trade-offs, and greatest risks — your "core business review at one thousand feet."
2. **Apply it at every kickoff.** How does this contribute to business success? Is it worth pursuing? What priority would I give it?
3. **Speak with certainty.** Hedging ("I recommend X, but of course there are trade-offs…") loses credibility the moment "but" appears. Formula: recommendation + reasons + metric, then *pause*. *"My recommendation is to prioritize feature A. Because of X and Y, this gives us the fastest path to improve metric Z."* The FP&A-manager version: "My recommendation is a five percent reduction in employee expenses. Based on X and Y, this gives us the fastest path to margin improvement" — risks go on a separate page shown only when asked. If challenged, "good. They're doing their job". "You don't need to be 100% sure to be 100% confident" — an executive he worked with needed only 30% certainty.

### Method C: report → advise → commit

Three levels of workplace communication. **Reporting** (facts, insights) is where most Chinese professionals live; anyone from intern to manager can build a report — and in 2026 AI writes the queries, synthesizes and visualizes "faster than your coffee." **Advising** lays out your opinion, but someone else still decides and executes, "and that is where all the risk is… one of the only differences between your manager and you." **Committing** is making the call and standing behind it. Promotions "aren't rewards for being right all the time"; they go to those who make the hard call with no clear answer.

> Habit line: "I'm seeing [insight]. And if it were my call, I'd [decision] because [reason]." … "I stake my name to this decision. I commit."

### Method D: quiet power for introverts

You don't have to play the extrovert's game. "I don't need to be loud to lead. Introverts bring depth, not noise." Recharge (block 30 minutes after back-to-back meetings); push toward async — *"Is there a TLDR version that we can discuss async to protect our calendars?"*; at events, be seen, talk to who you planned, then take an unapologetic "French exit"; in meetings, observe what others miss, ask deep questions — "when you speak quietly, the whole room becomes quiet"; and name it out loud: *"Hey, I'm an introvert. So if I disappear early, it's not you, it's me"*. Related: when fast, polished colleagues run the meeting, don't out-talk them; add clarity with a recap request — *"I want to revisit what [name] said about X, because I think it's important. Would you mind recapping it so that we're fully aligned?"* — which "shows quiet leadership… without needing to contribute anything smart yourself".

### Common mistakes

- Hero mode: being the doer everyone calls while strategy tables stay elsewhere.
- Reporting when you could commit.
- The "but" that undoes your own recommendation — "your opponent is yourself".
- Trying to "fix" introversion instead of announcing it.

## 8.9 Getting promoted: the KPI ladder and the black box

### Part A — your KPI changed

"If you're feeling stuck, it does not mean that you failed. It means that your KPI has changed". The strengths that got you promoted start working against you at the next level.

| Level | KPI | Three demonstrable behaviors |
|---|---|---|
| **Junior IC** | Reliability — "if your boss feels safer giving you work than giving your co-workers work, you grow." | Never miss deadlines (due Friday → send Thursday). Update before they ask: "Hey, quick update. The draft is 80% done, still on track for tomorrow." Be on call within an hour for time-sensitive messages during work hours — "The best ability is availability." (Startup "Excel monkey" promoted as "my go-to closer behind the scenes.") |
| **Senior IC** | Ownership — absorb complexity from your boss. "Everyone's reliable. Managers are looking for leverage." | Clarify success in one sentence up front. Ask "if I only had five hours this week, what would I do?" — "managers promote people who move big rocks, not the ones that carry small ones perfectly." Say no strategically without damaging the relationship. |
| **People manager** | Team performance — "what your team delivers without you." | Solve repeat pain at the system level (SOP, workflow redesign, training) instead of hero mode. Delegate outcomes, not tasks: "I trust you to own X. Success looks like Y. It's due by Z. So let's plan it out together and I will let you run with how we actually get it done." Make yourself intentionally unnecessary — "if you can't take a week off without chaos…" |
| **Director** | Organizational performance — developing organizations, not individuals. | — |

The manager-level story: with ten directs he stayed in IC mode, spent day one of a vacation hunched over a laptop in a hotel, and realized "I was the bottleneck." A strong report who'd been asking for authority "crushed it" once given space. The trap at senior IC: "you're really good at this specific thing" sounds like a compliment but means you're stuck; doubling down on domain skill won't advance you. And the aphorism: "If you're irreplaceable, then you're unpromotable".

Saying no is itself a leadership signal. A final-round director interview ended with "the team found you to be a bit too agreeable." Build a relationship bank balance with your manager (real value delivered = deposits), then spend it with the sandwich: acknowledge their pressure → decline anchored to *their* top priority → offer a path (delegate / deprioritize / postpone): *"I know the product launch is your top priority right now… If I take on this reporting project this week, then I won't be able to give the launch my full attention… Could we revisit this project in two weeks when priority Y will be wrapped up?"*.

### Part B — three confessions from a manager

Promotion is a black box even to your manager:

1. "So much of this is out of my hands" — five companies, five processes, some none.
2. Telling someone they aren't ready risks the relationship, so "many managers pick avoidance."
3. "Your career is not the thing I think about most" — leading 20 people on top of his own work. A client's line: "I'm the biggest advocate for my own career because promoting me is simply too much work for my manager."

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "You cannot wait for your manager to promote you. You must support your manager to promote you."

**The method**
1. **Find who decides.** Usually your manager, a committee, or a senior leader (your +1). A manager who keeps saying "it's coming" is often not the decider and won't chase the person who is. Ask straight: *"Who actually makes the call on this promotion?"* If they don't know, ask around and observe. "Headcount capped" means the decider is a senior leader.
2. **Learn how they decide.** Structured (rubric) → track the boxes. Opinion-based → read the person: what do they want, what do they fear, who have they promoted before and what did those people share (ask coworkers who grew). Then show that promoting you gives more of what they want and less of what they fear — e.g., spinning your work into "head of X" keeps the org lean and avoids a wave of requests.
3. **Do the work the promotion requires and get in front of the decider.** Bella's decider was an old-school, gut-driven senior director who valued selling up and visibility. She booked executive-communication coaching, delivered a high-profile outcome, and asked: *"John, the one thing that I would deeply appreciate is if you could start inviting me to present my work with the senior director. I know I'm ready."* After the director praised her, the manager said, *"You're right. Bella is a star. I'm thinking we should be proactive in our retention. Give her a reason to commit here."* Approved on the spot.

His closing confession: the ambitious ones who asked directly "were always going to be fine"; the quiet hardest workers waiting to be noticed kept him up at night.

### Part C — turning "no" into "not now," and setting your own pace

In the review, salary and promotion can't be negotiated, but expectations can. Raise it: *"By leading three cross-functional projects, I've developed and showcased the skills needed for a formal senior analyst role. Could we discuss what steps are needed to make that transition?"* If "not yet": *"I understand. Then could we document measurable goals instead so I know exactly what to achieve before revisiting this next month?"* — "That is how you turn no into not now." Ask: "What would exceeding expectations look like over the next 6 to 12 months?" / "Which skills, if I level them up, would make me promotable?".

When the answer is still "give it another couple years" — as the coach heard after saving the company from a $30M loss while leading 20 people — swap every "should" for "how can I": "I should get the promotion" → "How can I get a promotion?" "Should" puts the weight on someone else and "robs yourself of the power that you need to make a change." Then ask: what do I want to feel, what produces it, where can I actually get that — and build a two-path plan (internal transfer and external applications).

And the hardest truth in the corpus: companies promote you when they *need* you, not when you deserve it. The coach interviewed externally every two years, got five promotions in ten years and director by 30 — "not because I was a genius… but because I never let one company decide my pace." Managers only offered the promotion when he had an outside offer: "John, is there anything we can do to make you stay?" — "Maybe getting me that promotion that I've been asking for". You can also interview for a manager title without direct reports, by surfacing mentorship and leading-without-authority proof points — your "invisible promotions".

### Common mistakes

- Proving the old KPI harder (reliability at senior IC; IC output as a manager).
- Waiting for the manager to bring it up; assuming your manager is the decider.
- Asking "when?" instead of "who decides and what do they need to see?".
- Accepting "not yet" without documented goals.
- Letting one company set your pace.

## Practice set

### Drills

1. **(8.1) The Friday email, this Friday.** Paste this week's Slack messages and notes into an AI tool, generate three impact bullets, edit them so each has a number or a decision, and send them to your manager. Save the email as your first brag-book receipt.
2. **(8.1) One question per meeting for five days.** Before each meeting, pick clarity / priority / impact and write the question on a sticky note. Log which one you asked and what it surfaced.
3. **(8.2) Celebration rewrite.** Take a win from the last month. Write it three ways — Team, Insight, Collaborator — each with a real number and a named contributor. Post the best one in a team channel.
4. **(8.2) Career CPR.** Brain-dump every project from the last three years; score each H/M/L on challenge, validation, impact; rewrite the top scorer in root-cause → action → business-number form.
5. **(8.3) Stakeholder pressure map.** For one stalled initiative, list every stakeholder, mark who actually sets the vision the others follow, and write the one open question you'd ask them first (model: "What does good hiring mean to you?").
6. **(8.3) ACT in three sentences.** For an idea you want to raise this week, write one sentence per letter — nuanced credibility, social proof, low-commitment ask — and read them aloud before the meeting.
7. **(8.3) Care before convincing.** Before your next disagreement with a senior person, write one curiosity question ("Why is ___ the most important thing right now?") and one "I see what you're carrying" sentence; decide in advance whether this is an argument or a relationship moment.
8. **(8.4) Contrast + climb.** Take your last disagreement. Rewrite your opening line in don't/do form; then write "why is this important to you?" and the one-level-higher goal you both share.
9. **(8.4) Calibration log.** For one day, log every "you're…", "this is wrong", "why did you…", and "sorry". Rewrite each as "When X, you appear Y" / "Here's what I see differently" / "Walk me through your thinking".
10. **(8.5) The conversation you're freezing on.** Write the fight version in three parts — clear truth, acknowledgment that it's rough, support plan with a recurring routine. Then answer: would I respect myself for delivering it?
11. **(8.5) Name your Karen.** List the two things they do that hurt most; for each, write an Option-C move (escalate/borrow authority, reframe as their need for control, or detach).
12. **(8.6) Document-before-dive rehearsal.** Next time you help on someone else's workstream, send the two messages (teammate → manager) before starting, and file them. Separately, find your last "sorry" message to your boss about a team outcome and rewrite it as acknowledgement + forward plan.
13. **(8.7) Access audit.** Name one candidate each for sponsor, informant, mentee. Send the sponsor script about one piece of recent work this week. Run the stress test: "If my boss leaves tomorrow, am I still valuable here?"
14. **(8.8) Tonight / Friday / this week.** Tonight write tomorrow's top three; Friday run a one-page OODA on the week; post one public update about something you shipped. In one update, append the "if it were my call" sentence.
15. **(8.9) KPI diagnosis.** Write your current level's KPI and the next level's in one sentence each. List three things you do that belong to the previous level. Pick one next-level behavior to do this week (a pre-emptive status update, or handing off one recurring task with documentation).
16. **(8.9) Decision-maker intel.** Write who you believe decides your promotion and how confident you are. Fill in three intel items — wants, fears, past promotees. If uncertain, schedule the direct question with your manager this week.

### Role-play prompts (say your line out loud)

- *Stakeholder, after you present data:* "Honestly, I don't think this framework applies to us." → Respond with contrasting, then a why-question.
- *Manager, in front of the team:* "Why is this late? This is unacceptable." → Acknowledge, transparent constraint, align on future scope.
- *Manager, in 1:1:* "You're doing great, let's talk about promotion next cycle." → Ask "Who actually makes the call on this promotion?"
- *Manager, after a lost deal:* "What happened?" → Acknowledge, cite the documented sync, pivot to next steps. No "sorry."
- *Manager, new request on Wednesday:* "Can you take the reporting project this week?" → Sandwich: pressure → decline anchored to their top priority → delegate/deprioritize/postpone.

## Cheat sheet

**Frameworks**
- Influence = what you do × what people know
- Visibility: daily question (clarity/priority/impact) → weekly Friday email → quarterly skip-level offer
- Three celebrations: Team / Insight / Collaborator
- Brag book: STAR + values/development + business impact + receipts + development area with action
- Pressure point: identify → understand what they want → help them get it
- ACT: Assert credibility (with nuance) → Contextualize (social proof) → Trigger (low-commitment ask)
- Trust = competence × care: be curious → show care → choose argument or relationship
- Disagree: contrasting (don't/do) + mutual purpose (one level higher)
- Don't claim to know: people → "When X, you appear Y"; truth → "Here's what I see differently"; events → "Walk me through your thinking"
- Conflict: fight / flight / freeze → "Which path will I respect myself for later?" Hard truth → acknowledge → support plan
- Difficult colleague: grow the cup; A suffer / B quit / C navigate (borrow authority, reframe, detach)
- Blame: responsibility ≠ liability → document before you dive → acknowledge, don't apologize
- Delete: sorry → thanks; just → (nothing); try → commit + update clause
- Access: sponsor (opportunity) / informant (information) / mentee (reputation)
- Micro-habits: daily top three → weekly OODA → do things and tell people
- Head-up: align (15-min AI business review) → apply (three kickoff questions) → speak with certainty (no "but")
- Report → Advise → Commit
- KPI ladder: reliability → ownership → team performance → organizational performance
- Promotion: find who decides → learn how (rubric or wants/fears/precedents) → do the work and get in front of them

**Phrases**
- "What does success look like here?" / "Who else does this affect?"
- "What does good [X] mean to you? Not what it means to [exec], just what you care about?"
- "I don't mean to say ___. I just want to make sure ___."
- "Why is ___ the most important thing right now?" / "It sounds like you're under intense pressure."
- "I'm seeing ___. If it were my call, I'd ___ because ___."
- "Part A is done. Teammate okayed my help on B, so I'm stepping in. Let me know if you have concerns."
- "Who actually makes the call on this promotion?"
- "Could we document measurable goals instead, before revisiting this next month?"

**Do / Don't**
- Do send the Friday email; don't wait to be noticed.
- Do spotlight others while owning your role; don't say "look what I did."
- Do ask the decision maker's definition of success first; don't pitch to the defensive team.
- Do state the contrast before the disagreement; don't open with "this is wrong."
- Do follow hard truth with a plan; don't soften it into nothing.
- Do raise your hand before you help; don't absorb a whole outcome that was never yours.
- Do acknowledge; don't apologize in high-stakes moments.
- Do show the next level's KPI; don't prove the old one harder.
- Do interview externally every two years; don't let one company set your pace.
