# Chapter 9 — Career Strategy & Mindset

> Every earlier chapter taught a skill: a resume bullet, an interview story, a message to your boss. This chapter is about the operating system underneath those skills. The coach's argument, repeated across dozens of videos, is that Chinese and other immigrant professionals in North America are rarely limited by ability — "you'll be limited by one of two things, your skills or your beliefs. Never let it be your beliefs". The beliefs in question are specific and learnable to unlearn: that hard work is rewarded automatically, that confidence must come before action, that a setback says something about your worth, that a goal handed to you by your parents is your own, that the safe move is to wait. Most of the videos here are story-driven; this chapter extracts the reasoning and the transferable method from each rather than retelling them. It closes with the coach's view of how AI changes the game, which he argues is less about learning AI and more about "discovering yourself".

**What you'll be able to do**

- Decide whether a career goal is genuinely yours by asking what you are willing to endure for it, and convert every "should" complaint into an actionable "how can I".
- Build confidence as evidence rather than emotion: act first, rebuild your action → habit → character chain, and design for failure so risk stops feeling like life or death.
- Retell your worst work story in the third person so it credits your contribution truthfully, and treat setbacks as data rather than identity.
- Sort risks into smart and stupid by their upside/downside asymmetry and pick the ones worth taking.
- Audit your career as a garden — seed, soil, climate — and identify which layer to adjust.
- Recognise which career KPI you are being measured on now (reliability → ownership → team performance → organizational performance) and set your own promotion pace by returning to the market.
- Diagnose a bad role with "what is staying doing to me?" and, in the AI era, position yourself around what a knowledge base cannot replace.

## 9.1 Whose goal is it? Ownership, endurance, and the "should" trap

### The concept

Much career misery, the coach argues, comes not from a hard job but from chasing a goal you never chose. A viewer once commented that "no one should need so much energy aligning with managers, this is draining." The coach's read: the complaint isn't really about energy; it's about feeling trapped doing something inauthentic. Many Chinese professionals were handed a vision by parents and teachers, were loved when they achieved it, and became very good at knowing what other people want — then called that skill "ambition".

The coach's own version: his father, a restaurant owner in Toronto, once pointed at the tallest building downtown and told him "one day, you're gonna buy that building." Two decades later, at Uber, working until 2 a.m. and 30 pounds heavier, he launched Vancouver and felt nothing. On a plane he ran a five-whys on himself: why am I killing myself → for the promotion → to prove my value → to feel worthy → because it was Dad's dream. He later quit at director level to build his own thing — "not the top floor of someone else's building. My own building".

### The coach's reasoning

The wrong question is "what do I want?" because "wanting is cheap". Everyone wants the promotion, the dream offer, the big-company resume — few want the 1,000 applications and 999 rejections that come with it. The real question is:

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "The real question is, what are you willing to endure?"

Once you answer honestly, both paths are legitimate. If you genuinely want the promotion — "genuinely yes, not 'I guess I have to'" — then accept the politics, the self-selling, the smiling and nodding at people you don't like, and stop being angry about it: "You chose this. Own it." If you don't, then clock in, do good work, go home, and choose that peace consciously, "without bitterness watching someone else get promoted". Resentment comes from wanting the reward while refusing the cost.

A related reframe comes from the coach's therapy. Imagine running a 10k with someone holding a gun to your head; for two years at Uber he ran like that — 2 a.m. Slack, +30 pounds, a relationship nearly ended, "a zombie blacking out on the subway." The belief underneath was that work is something done to you rather than chosen by you, "that's why people have to pay you for it" — a belief reinforced by anti-work, FIRE and 躺平 content. The gun is handed down with love: parents ("do you want a good life? Then you must study"), teachers, then employers. "Nobody tells you when the gun moves from their hand into your own. That handoff, I would call adulthood". The coach is explicit that bad managers and toxic cultures are real structural problems; the question is still "is the gun in their hand or in mine?" He contrasts a client competing for a GM role in the Middle East, working 7 a.m. to 11 p.m. and feeling fine, because the work was chosen.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "The moment that you realize that no one's holding a gun to your head, the finish line stops looking like survival. It starts looking like freedom."

### The method: replace "should" with "how can I"

The linguistic version of the same ownership shift is the coach's most tactical tool in this area. Client complaints — "my manager should support me more," "they should give me that offer," "I should get that promotion" — all carry a feeling of being wronged. "Should" feels good because it puts the weight on someone else, but it "robs yourself of the power that you need to make a change," and it tricks the brain into believing you've already done everything you could. He concedes each complaint may be true — yes, a good manager should support you; yes, you were the perfect fit — and then: "The workplace is never the way it should be". His own example: after saving his company from a $30M loss and leading 20 people, he asked "I think I'm ready for promotion. When can we make it happen?" and was told "not there yet, give it another couple of years".

1. **Swap the verb.** "My manager should support me" → "How can I get more support?" "They should give me the offer" → "How can I get the offer?" "I should get the promotion" → "How can I get a promotion?" The coach's claim is that "the way that you think shapes the way that you speak, yes, but the way that you speak also shapes the way that you think".
2. **If "how can I" draws a blank, shrink it to three questions:** What do you want to *feel*? What can make you feel that way? Where can you actually get that? People often don't know what they want, but they know how they want to feel.
3. **Build a game plan with more than one path.** A client felt unappreciated by a kind-but-harsh manager. She discovered she wanted to feel *competent*; a higher title could prove that; so they planned an internal transfer path and an external application path in parallel.

The same ownership principle appears in the coach's rapid-fire list of "truths": nobody cares about your career as much as you; if you don't decide what you want, your manager decides for you; your manager's job is to manage you, not to grow you; nobody gives you work-life balance, you create it. In the leadership-habits video, the line is "If you don't decide what matters to your success, others will decide for you".

### Common mistakes

- Wanting the reward while resenting the cost — the source of "bitterness watching someone else get promoted".
- Stating "should" sentences as if they were action plans; the feeling of being right substitutes for the work of getting what you want.
- Treating "I have to" as a chosen "yes." The coach's test is *genuine* willingness, not resignation.
- Hiding in busyness. When afraid, the Chinese reflex is to learn more — "we learn so that we can avoid doing"; staying busy feels safer than risking rejection, but it is hiding.

## 9.2 Confidence is evidence: act first, then feel

### The concept

The coach's position is unambiguous: "Confidence is not where you begin, it's where you end." He led big teams, built a seven-figure business, and spoke on national TV — "all of it, terrified out of my mind". Affirmations and "fake it till you make it" fade; "affirmations and emotions don't last. Identity does." His motto is "evidence over emotions". In the 2026 lessons video this is lesson two, "You're confident because you act," with the observation that many treat their careers like a dress rehearsal for a moment that never comes.

### The coach's reasoning

Three mechanisms recur across the videos.

**1. Fear is default; make inaction more painful than action.** Humans are wired for fear. You silence it not by feeling brave but by making "the pain of inaction greater than the pain of action." The coach's example is physical: overweight in his twenties, eating McDonald's at 2 a.m. on overtime, he broke his back carrying his niece, which shattered his self-image as "athletic." He started "doing the healthy stuff that healthy people do" until not going to the gym hurt more than the workout. "You lack confidence because your expectations are too high" — success "is an inevitability when you do the small things right".

**2. Outcomes follow identity — rebuild the chain.** At 220 lb at 27, crash diets lost 10 lb in two weeks and regained it in two months because "every healthy choice felt like this impossible test of willpower." The quote that changed him: "Watch your actions, they become your habits. Watch your habits, they become your character. Watch your character, it becomes your destiny." His old chain: a 2 a.m. McDonald's "just once" → comfort meal whenever stressed → "a person who disrespected my body" → obese. At 33, 40 lb lighter, the identity is "I am a healthy person, and healthy people take care of their bodies". The coach's central claim: "We chase the big outcome without becoming the person who could achieve it".

The workplace version he draws is exact. Broken chain: speak only when you have something "worthwhile" (which, as an ESL speaker, is almost never) → used to staying quiet → "someone who doesn't have much to say" → "invisible in the room." Rebuilt chain: Action — "I speak when I have an idea, even if it's just average" → Habit — used to speaking up → Character — "someone who adds value to discussions" → Destiny — speaks up well everywhere at work.

**3. Design for failure, not for success.** "Work so that you may quit. Interview so that you may fail." Hesitation happens because "you've made success your goal," so every risk "feels life or death" and failure "threatens your identity" — which is why the comment you rehearsed in the team meeting never gets said. People who live freely "chase process." Reframe every action's purpose: a presentation is to collect criticism; volunteering for a project is to observe how you react to new challenges; an interview is to "earn a rejection that tells you exactly where you can do better." "When you embrace the worst, the worst can't happen anymore," and each failure eliminates one path toward the true destination.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "What if your goal wasn't to succeed? What would you do if you wanted to fail badly and learn faster than anyone else in the room?"

### The method

1. **Decide who you want to be** (the identity, not the outcome).
2. **Choose the smallest action that person would take** — "I speak when I have an average idea" — and do it regardless of feeling: "F it, I'm gonna do it scared anyway".
3. **Set the goal of each attempt as feedback** — write down the criticism you hope to collect before the presentation or interview.
4. **Log the evidence.** Confidence accumulates from the record, not from the mood.
5. **Audit your accounts.** "Your accounts never lie": the coach told himself he was a creative entrepreneur while his calendar showed 2 a.m. Excel reports at Uber and his bank account showed margin-chasing losses. "You become who your actions quietly prove you are". Look at your calendar and your bank statement; they show who you actually are.

A worked example from coaching: an accountant who became a controller panicked weeks into the role. The coach's advice was simply "just do more and you'll feel better." Two years later she is a top performer running a side business. Another: the coach avoided making content for years ("I'm not a social media person") and stayed unknown despite excellent client results; one video later, 100,000+ followers in two years. Lesson three: "Skills are easy, beliefs are hard" — Chinese professionals chase degrees and certificates but rarely question beliefs. His test question: "Who says that I need more?" Apply and let the manager say no; say the words and let them respond.

### On waiting

The coach built his coaching practice on nights and weekends for five years while waiting for "the right moment" to leave a job he loved. Two family medical emergencies in 2023 taught him that "life does not wait for you." The inner voice demanding "more evidence, more validation, more certainty... wasn't trying to help me. It was death itself" — death "isn't loud, it's quiet, it looks like comfort, like certainty". In the 2026 lessons video: "At some point waiting stops being discipline and starts becoming fear". The reflective question he offers is "How would you feel if nothing changed?" — never try the idea, never move to the city, never tell the person.

### Imposter syndrome and the mirror

The coach treats imposter feelings as a signal, not a verdict: "Imposter syndrome is proof you've leveled up. If you're the smartest in the room, wrong room". For new hires: "Do you think your manager is an idiot? No? Then why are you doubting them for hiring you?" and "don't try to squish it. Just give it a big warm hug and thank it for showing up". For immigrant professionals specifically, the pep-talk version: you don't need another degree or more fluency, "you need a mirror." An accent "means that you carry a story in your chest"; starting over means "now you know how to build from zero to one." Lines to hold: "I've been through more than this, and I'm still standing." / "Let me show you what I've built".

### Common mistakes

- Waiting to feel ready. "Careers are treated like a dress rehearsal for a moment that never comes".
- Attempting the big outcome with willpower instead of changing the small action; the crash-diet pattern.
- Speaking only when you have something "worthwhile" — the ESL trap that ends in invisibility.
- Relying on affirmations. "Affirmations and emotions don't last. Identity does".

## 9.3 Rewrite the story: setbacks as data, wins as evidence

### The concept

"We internalize our failures and externalize our successes." Western coworkers, the coach observes, often do the reverse — success was "their vision, their call"; failure was "the timing, the process, the missing data". Neither is lying, because:

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Every story is just a collection of facts. Select the failures and give away your wins, and you have a self-deprecating story. Do the opposite, and you have a self-appreciating one. Both are true. So tell yourself the better one."

### The coach's reasoning

Counting wins is not arrogance; it is "collecting evidence," and evidence is what enables confidence, bravery and risk-taking. "The self-critical story doesn't make you better. It makes you scared". The third-person technique works, the coach says citing psychologist Ethan Kross, because using your own name instead of "I" stops emotional flooding and lets you judge yourself "the way we judge a friend, fairly".

The cultural root is spelled out in the PIP story. Two years into Uber, at the start of COVID, his manager put him on a performance improvement plan for communication and presentation skills. He drafted a furious email ("Respectfully, this is BS, and you just want to reduce headcount without severance") and left it in drafts. He could not tell his parents (whose narrative was: smart, but maybe lazy) or his high-performer friends. He traces the shame to a culture where "my value lives in the eyes of others" — face, 面子, parents who "graded me at the dinner table". The shame followed him: at the next job he spent 30 minutes preparing for every 30-minute 1:1, and a kind manager's "you don't have anything to prove" felt empty. His warning: shame "will cost you a raise that you never asked for or a promotion that you never pursued". When he updated LinkedIn to start his business, every profile read like "a highlight reel of toxic positivity"; he posted the PIP publicly, and "that day was the day that my shame finally went away." You don't need a public post, but you need honesty with yourself: "when you know who you are, you get to choose what the data means to you".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "A PIP or any setback is data. A failure is data."

### The method: the five-minute third-person rewrite

1. Choose the work story you are most ashamed of.
2. Write it in the third person, as if it happened to someone else.
3. Select the facts that most appreciate that person's contribution.
4. Retell it enough times that it becomes your official telling.

**Worked example — Eve's dashboard.** Eve, a senior financial analyst with no engineering background, dreaded interview questions about her "failed" real-time sales dashboard: the CEO asked her to build it, the data came out wrong, and she concluded she couldn't deliver. The coach's reframe, using identical facts:

> "Nobody in the company could build what your CEO wanted. So among all his employees he trusted you, a financial analyst with zero engineering background. You taught yourself Power BI and built the company's first dashboard from zero to one. The data was wrong because the company had no data foundations — and your dashboard proved what was possible and justified hiring a full-time data engineer. Eve, you didn't fail to build the dashboard. You are the reason that the dashboard exists."

**The biography version.** The coach narrates his own 33 years twice. Loser side: grew up poor in a restaurant, bullied, fat and nerdy ("Bubbles"), never first, imposter at work, failed every business, married the first girl who said yes. Hero side, same facts: parents gave everything; bullied *after* being designated gifted; youngest-ever Toronto Spelling Bee winner and two-time national presentation champion; felt like an imposter *as a Shopify director*; failed businesses until Mr. Jonathan Office; married the first and only girl he ever asked, together ten years. "Growing up as a Chinese meant that we chose to write the loser side for our story, hoping that it would motivate us" — "what if you've been the hero all along?".

### Setbacks that set your price

Two videos apply the same logic to money. First, the scarcity trap: a candidate "M" had failed 20+ interviews and opened every self-introduction with "most recently, I was laid off." They rebuilt her opener to lead with strengths ("the whole point of interviews is to focus on your strengths"), she got the offer — and signed instantly, selling herself short. "If you got the offer, the company believes that you are the top candidate, and top candidates deserve top compensation." Rejection means "you haven't found the right match yet"; "scarcity will shrink your vision." Principle: "Never let scarcity set your price". Second, the exploitation trap: a candidate with an Ivy League master's was paid minimum wage at a small company and belittled by her boss. The coach's reframe: "Your labor is as cheap as you believe it is"; companies that exploit "will always stay small… if you pay the minimum, you get the minimum"; and "your price does reflect your value. Make that the motivation to ask for what you deserve." She got a verbal offer at more than double. For underrepresented candidates, the coach adds that North American companies set voluntary diversity targets and actively source candidates — "job hunt with confidence because you're worth it and they know it" — but it is "not a free pass": you still must be the most qualified.

### Common mistakes

- Leading with the loss. "Hi, I'm M. I have five years of experience. And most recently, I was laid off".
- Letting one evaluation define you rather than inform you.
- Confusing self-criticism with self-improvement — it "makes you scared," not better.
- Being modest to stay likeable: "You're not competing in the humble Olympics" — modesty (谦虚) just keeps you invisible.

## 9.4 Take the right risks: Stretch × Stay, smart vs. stupid

![Bounded career experiment versus unbounded risk: limit downside, learn, and preserve options](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-9/02-comparison-bounded-career-risks.png)

### The concept

"Work harder, earn more" is a lie: construction workers, nurses and taxi drivers grind longer hours than anyone and don't earn more. Earnings are "how far you can stretch multiplied by how long you can stay". The same idea underlies the performance-review truths: "Performance has nothing to do with how hard you work. Effort is just an input, and inputs don't matter", and "Hard work alone isn't the answer; if you're irreplaceable, you're unpromotable".

### The coach's reasoning

- **Stretch** = educated risks: the risky high-visibility project, fighting for the role you're not ready for, pushing into an industry nobody believes in yet.
- **Stay** = surviving "the fog of uncertainty when nothing is guaranteed"; most people turn back right before the breakthrough.

His illustration: Meta reportedly paying an Apple engineer $200M/year to join its AI team, versus Tim Cook's $75M in 2024. The engineer isn't twice as smart; they "stretched early, betting their entire career on AI before it was hot" and stayed building skills "until the world caught up".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Your next salary jump won't come from another late night. It'll come from the risk that you're most afraid to take."

**Which risks?** In turbulent times — anti-immigrant sentiment, visa rules changing overnight, China–West tension, a weak economy, AI — the question isn't "should I take risks?" but "which risks are worth taking". The coach's two-part frame:

1. **Control your perspective.** We were taught control makes you strong — plan everything, minimise mistakes, work twice as hard — but "you don't survive tough times, you flow through them." You can't control the economy, but you can control your relationship with money ("Do I really need another Labubu?"); you can't control visa policy, but you can control your preparation and self-worth, "not defined by any stamp on any passport." His model is the poet Su Shi, exiled and ridiculed, who complained, then kept writing and painting — observe, accept, continue.
2. **Control your reactions: avoid stupid risks, seek smart ones.** A stupid risk has unlimited downside and limited upside — buying crypto on margin in a downturn, taking a loan to put into Bitcoin to make losses back fast. A smart risk has bounded downside and unbounded upside — building "portable value," a globally in-demand skill; investing in yourself; a course; a small business. Worst case you lose a few hours a week or a small investment; best case you are "unstoppable everywhere". In uncertain times it's natural to avoid all risk, but "most risks offer opportunity".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "We've crossed oceans, learned new languages, rebuilt from zero. Flow is in our blood… I don't wish you survival. I wish you flow."

### Risk at the level of a sentence: report → advise → commit

The coach calls willingness to take risk a "high-level workplace skill" and locates it in how you communicate. Reporting (facts, insights) is where most Chinese professionals live; advising lays out an opinion, but someone else still decides and executes — "and that is where all the risk is... one of the only differences between your manager and you." Committing means making the call and standing behind it. Promotions "aren't rewards for being right all the time"; they go to those who make the hard call when there's no clear answer. The habit line:

```
I'm seeing [insight]. And if it were my call, I'd [decision] because [reason].
```


### Risk in a relationship: confrontation and conflict style

Two framings from the corpus. A guest essay by Shirley, an ex-staff data scientist now studying psychotherapy, redefines confrontation as "the front of us meeting the front of others." She used to have two modes — mask on to keep the peace, or dump ugly feelings — and avoided confrontation because she equated it with the second. Her reframe: "every confrontation is co-created" — you choose which front to bring, and thereby invite a different front from the other person. Confrontation becomes "the risk of letting a little more of yourself come forward, and seeing whether the other person is willing to meet you there." Her example is the personal question she never asked a product lead about his home decor during a COVID call: "I love how you styled your couch. Are you interested in home decor, too?".

The coach's own conflict framing rejects "clap back harder" advice because it doesn't take responsibility for what happens after. Know which of three responders you are — fight, flight (leave or avoid), or ignore (acknowledge what they said, do what you want anyway) — and choose the one you'll feel good about afterward. His wife is a fighter and walks away satisfied; he ignores and feels fine; both are valid. The English-language version adds "freeze" (delay, hope it goes away — his own default) and the decision question "Which path will I respect myself for later?"; direct feedback must be followed immediately with support and a plan.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "The measure of winning a conflict isn't about what you say. It's about whether you feel good about yourself when it's over."

### Common mistakes

- Reaching for one more late night instead of the risk you're afraid of.
- Taking gambling-style risks to recover losses fast (unlimited downside).
- Avoiding all risk during uncertainty, when "most risks offer opportunity".
- Turning back in the fog "right before the breakthrough".
- Staying at the reporting level because deciding feels exposed.

## 9.5 The garden model: seed, soil, climate

### The concept

"Your career is a garden, not a ladder." Growth depends on three layers — the **seed** (your skills and nature), the **soil** (company, manager, coworkers), and the **climate** (industry, geography, timing) — and you have to tend each. The coach calls this the "three key factors" of career leaps for ordinary people, and every layer is illustrated with a mistake he made.

![Career garden model showing seed (skills and interests), soil (manager, company, team), and climate (industry, geography, timing)](/assets/career/day-day-up-playbook/chapter-9/01-infographic-career-garden-model.png)

### Seed — grow yours, not someone else's

The seed is fully in your control and grows fastest when you "nurture the plant that you were meant to become, not the one that someone else wanted you to be." His father wanted a doctor; he loved Chaucer, spoke on TV as a kid, won a national business case competition. His 9-to-5 taught him application; his 5-to-9 — years of coaching, cringe videos, clumsy newsletters — grew the skills that became his business. He quotes Naval: "do what feels like play to you but work to others". Losing your seed has a price: he ignored a request for a content-marketing sample from the CEO of Wealthsimple when it was a 12-person startup, because he wanted to do "strategy"; he joined five years later after it became a unicorn (now $10B). The audit question: *"Am I growing the seed that belongs to me or the one someone else planted?"*

### Soil — you cannot outwork bad soil

Two soil mistakes. First, letting others choose it: being headhunted by a big-brand company "felt so good," but he joined against his gut, found dry, slow, political soil with no growth, and stayed too long because leaving felt like failure. Second, not recognising good soil: in 2016 he passed on a Shopify enterprise account manager role at $3/share ("already public, can't grow much") and joined later at $200. "You can't spot great soil if you have poor vision" — so borrow the vision of a mentor or coach who has walked ahead, not parents or friends who "don't know what they're talking about". The audit question: *"Is the problem me, or is it the soil around me that's starving my growth?"*

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Great soil can grow even the most average seeds, but bad soil kills great ones. You cannot outwork bad soil."

### Climate — the most underrated layer

A booming climate is rocket fuel (tech in the 2010s; the AI boom now); a shrinking one makes you feel like you're running uphill. Signs of momentum: rising job openings, more companies investing, skills growing in demand. "A rising climate can make you look lucky, but a declining one can make you look lazy." He joined a Series C startup at ~$50k in tech's golden age, which led to Google, Uber, Shopify and a 5x salary from his 20s to his 30s — "to be honest, I got lucky. I was in the right climate". The audit question: *"Is my industry moving up or falling behind?"*

### The method: the garden audit

1. Write one paragraph answering each of the three questions (seed / soil / climate).
2. Anyone who tells you what your seed, soil or climate *should* be — "run in the opposite direction".
3. Adjust **one layer at a time**.
4. For soil, borrow vision: ask someone five-plus years ahead to evaluate your current company as soil and compare their read to yours.

### Common mistakes

- Choosing soil for the brand or the flattery of being headhunted.
- Staying in bad soil because leaving feels like failure.
- Judging a company's future with your own poor vision ("already public, can't grow") instead of a mentor's.
- Ignoring climate entirely, then blaming yourself for feeling slow in a shrinking industry.

## 9.6 Set your own pace: the KPI ladder, the two-year rule, and the myth of deserving

### The concept

"Feeling stuck doesn't mean you failed. It means your KPI has changed". The strengths that got you promoted start working against you because each level is measured on something different, and nobody tells you.

### The KPI ladder

| Level | KPI | What it looks like | Tactics the coach names |
|---|---|---|---|
| Junior IC | **Reliability** | Your boss "feels safer giving work to you" than to coworkers | Due Friday → send Thursday; update before asked ("the draft is 80% done, still on track for tomorrow"); reply within an hour to time-sensitive requests during work hours — "the best ability is availability" |
| Senior IC | **Ownership** | Identify and scope problems, plan, execute with minimal guidance; "absorb complexity from your boss" | Define success in one sentence before starting; "if I only had five hours this week, what would I do?" — "managers promote people who move big rocks, not the ones that carry small ones perfectly"; say no strategically |
| People manager | **Team performance** | "What your team delivers without you" | Solve repeat pain at the system level (SOPs, workflow redesign, training) instead of hero mode; delegate outcomes not tasks; make yourself intentionally unnecessary — "if you can't take a week off without chaos, you haven't shifted" |
| Director | **Organizational performance** | Developing teams and organizations, not individuals | — |


The trap for Chinese professionals is the senior-IC stage. "You're really good at this specific thing" sounds like a compliment but means you're stuck, and the common response is to double down on domain skill and reliability. But "everyone's reliable. Managers are looking for leverage". This is why less-skilled people become managers: they are "not [less skilled] in the dimension that matters right now". The coach's own lesson: as a first-time manager of 10+ he stayed in IC mode, worked through a vacation, and realised "I was the bottleneck"; a strong report who'd been asking for authority "crushed it" once given space. To show manager-readiness before the title: think at the team level, treat problems as systems, fix root causes — build the month-end SOP so the team stops scrambling, or train the team. Mentoring and leading without authority are your "invisible promotions"; the coach mentored aggressively when every manager was 5+ years older, and felt ready for his first direct report because "I'd done the job long before I had the job".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "Decide whether you want to keep proving who you were or start becoming who you need to be."

### The two-year rule: companies promote when they need you, not when you deserve it

The belief that hard work plus a boss who likes you yields promotion is "a lie": coworkers slaved for 3–10 years and watched outside hires take the role. The fast growers sought promotions outside. Externally you control the narrative — it doesn't matter if your boss thinks you're a poor performer or the company says wait three years; if you can convince an interviewer you have the skills, the promotion is yours. Result: five promotions in ten years, director at 30, "not because I was a genius... but because I never let one company decide my pace." Managers offered the promotion only when he had an outside offer: "another company has to discover your value first before your own company will suddenly recognize it".

> Manager: "John, is there anything we can do to make you stay?"
> Coach: "Maybe getting me that promotion that I've been asking for."

Method: every two years, interview externally for the next-level title and make your employer compete with the market. "Never wait for permission to grow". Waiting for your boss to hand you a team "can mean waiting forever; go interview for the title".

### Three supporting rules

- **Learning, earning, or leaving.** "Always either be learning or earning. If you aren't doing either, you should be leaving." Job success is 9-to-5; career success is 5-to-9.
- **Title is what can't be taken away.** Between comparable companies the coach admits he "mostly chased title," possibly short-sightedly. Work-life balance can vanish when a company struggles; a beloved manager and team can be torn away by restructuring — "that has happened to me before." "When it's on your resume, it's on your resume," and the meaning and interesting work "come with a title".
- **Politics and outputs, not effort.** From running performance reviews: only outputs that affect the business count; the coworker who leaves at 5 asks "how does this business make money? how does my work connect?" Soft skills multiply hard skills — a director whose job is 90% people picks the clear, collaborative engineer over the brilliant, unexplainable one. And "you must be political to succeed," where politics means "the unwritten rules of how influence, relationships, and communication work." The coach's own side projects were shut down because he had no sponsor and hadn't connected them to business goals. The access you need comes through three relationships — a sponsor (opportunity), an informant (information), a mentee (reputation) — and "sponsors don't find talented people. They find visible people". Hence "do things and tell people": "Invisible impact is indistinguishable from no impact".

### Common mistakes

- Doubling down on the previous level's KPI when stuck.
- Letting one company set your pace; waiting for internal recognition that only comes with an external offer.
- Confusing effort with performance — "hard work got you here, but it won't get you there".
- Building side projects without a sponsor or a link to business goals.
- Reputation shortcuts: "20 years to build, five minutes to ruin." Never write what you wouldn't want on the front page of the Wall Street Journal; never say about someone what you wouldn't say to their face.

## 9.7 When to leave, and where to go

### The concept: "What is staying doing to me?"

Client Angela received brutal feedback from her manager — "You aren't even aware of how poor your communication is. It's hopeless to explain" — and asked ten friends whether to quit. Eight said stay: bad market, resume gap, "people would kill for your job." "Every one of them was right... but they're also terribly wrong".

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "When others give you advice, they aren't talking to you. They're talking to themselves."

Friends see their own job, mortgage and manager. The coach's own cost of listening to that logic: he stayed a year in a role he knew was wrong in week one ("a year is the minimum time... to not raise eyebrows"). After a meeting where he felt invisible he asked his manager, "Hey, if I leave this role, would you even backfill it?" — and got no straight answer. The company didn't need the role. His skills devolved, his confidence drained, and it took two more years to recover; at the next job, under a kind manager, he over-prepared for every 1:1 out of lingering fear.

The method is a reframe plus a diagnostic:
1. Replace "What would you do?" with **"What is staying doing to me?"** — answered honestly across skills, confidence and health, before asking anyone's opinion.
2. Ask your manager the backfill question. A non-answer is an answer.
3. The coach does not claim to know Angela's decision; the win is that she is asking the right question.

### Burnout: what "one more promotion" costs

The coach's earliest story on this: he identified with his company and title, worked 80-hour weeks, and "told myself that I loved it." He couldn't be present at meals with his girlfriend because he was worrying about "a stupid deck"; she "slowly became a shadow of her happy self." He gained ~30 lb in two years and slept past his stop on the commute. His warning to overachievers and "yes people": "one more promotion" thinking ignores that one hospitalization, one breakdown, or one person leaving can derail everything. Resolution: he quit for a job where "I worked less, and I said yes less, and I earned more". Combined with 9.1's "gun" question, the lesson is not "work less" but audit what the pace is costing and whether the pace is chosen. For introverts specifically, protecting energy is a legitimate strategy: block 30 minutes after back-to-back meetings, push toward async ("Is there a TLDR version that we can discuss async to protect our calendars?"), and announce your introversion rather than "fix" it.

### Finding direction: skills → passion → fit

"The worst career mistake is wasting your best years growing someone else's career." The coach spent two decades in business strategy before returning to writing, a childhood dream his parents vetoed ("writers don't make any money"). His three-lens method:

1. **Skills — evidence, not guesses.** Look at five real projects and write only the skills that produced actual results.
2. **Personality — Big Five, not MBTI.** For each skill ask "does my personality provide assistance or resistance?" (high conscientiousness assists project management; low agreeableness resists people management; high openness assists data analysis). "Skills tell you what you do well already. Big Five tells you what you can keep improving on until you become unstoppable."
3. **Passion — the Good Time Journal** (from *Designing Your Life*): for three weeks log activities with engagement and energy; for energizing moments tag AEIOU — Activities, Environments, Interactions, Objects, Users. "The things that you should enjoy often drain you, and the things that you secretly love give you energy every time."
4. **Fit** — prompt an AI with your skills and passions to output five job titles with descriptions, salary, progression and fit rationale.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "We have two lives, and the second begins when we realize that we only have one."

### Making the move: change one variable

To break into any role or industry: (1) change only the job title *or* the industry, never both, because interviewers need clear relevance — the coach kept "strategy and operations" titles and switched from corporate to tech; (2) learn who fits via coffee chats and LinkedIn profiles at target companies; (3) look like the ideal candidate through stories — resume bullets as Problem → Skills → Achievement and "three superstar stories" layered with details proving the target skills. A senior data scientist in banking moved laterally into a senior AI product manager role this way. And when the market, not a course, is the teacher: "Stop filling up your head with more learning. Fill up your resume instead" — even a failed application round reveals what the market wants and which gaps are real.

### Common mistakes

- Asking "what would you do?" and averaging the answers.
- Staying the "minimum year" in a role the company doesn't need.
- Telling yourself you love the 80-hour week.
- Guessing your skills instead of reading them off real projects; using MBTI.
- Changing title and industry at once.
- Enrolling in another course instead of applying.

## 9.8 AI and your career: the race toward discovering yourself

### The concept

The coach sees two fear-driven groups, both stuck: "panic learners" stacking AI bootcamps and certificates for unrelated roles, and haters demanding AI be banned — "one in anxiety, the other in denial." AI's real damage is to "your sense of value as a human being". AI-proofing a career is not about learning more AI; it's about using AI as a tool to lean harder into your distinctly human value.

### The coach's reasoning — his own failed AI product

He launched an AI coaching product trained on his frameworks and sold at a fraction of his fee. It failed. Clients kept asking, "how much time will I get with you personally?" His conclusion: "AI has commoditized common knowledge"; what clients were paying for was lived experience and the ability to read "what is unsaid in that room." The Industrial Revolution analogy: those who thrived weren't only the engineers but the managers and salespeople who understood "what the factory made possible" and doubled down on what they were already good at.

> 🗣 **Coach's words:** "The race was never towards discovering AI. It was always toward discovering yourself."

This is the same "build your spikes" logic from the COVID-era story: newly married with a mortgage at Uber in 2020, he took a Python course he hated until he realised he didn't want to be a data scientist — he wanted a secure job. Applying revealed he would never out-code his wife (a staff data scientist), but nobody could tell stories like him: a 100% success rate on case interviews. "Stop trying to close every gap that you have. Build your spikes." He pivoted to strategy & operations leadership and resigned the same day Uber announced mass layoffs.

### What AI changes in the ladder

- **Reporting is commoditized.** In 2026 AI writes queries, synthesizes and visualizes "faster than your coffee"; what it can't do is make good decisions — and that gets truer the higher you climb. Hence the premium on committing (9.4).
- **Task-level AI use is table stakes.** "In 2026, nobody should be even talking about task problems anymore" — agents chain tasks in seconds, and "your role is going to be reduced to a button pusher." Move to process problems (scale your impact so more people solve more problems — as a manager he'd ask "That's a great job. Now, how do you plan to scale your impact?") and business problems (using AI to make better decisions that create opportunities). Leaders aren't smarter; they've "accumulated more context," and AI helps you catch up on context faster. "Stop listing your AI toolkit. Start showing your business impact".
- **Judgment is the differentiator.** The coach's decision rule for AI: "if this goes wrong, how bad is it?" Low stakes (meeting summaries, market research, slide outlines) → autopilot, delegate and monitor; "if you're still doing these things manually, you are already falling behind." High stakes (a difficult stakeholder conversation, a presentation asking leadership to trust your call, a job interview) → copilot, "never outsource your thinking".
- **Fit still rules.** "How do you use AI?" is a fit question: "never" looks rigid, "for everything" looks brainless. Search "[company] stance on AI" and match your register, then give a domain example.

### AI and authenticity

AI-written English is obvious to everyone but you — the coach couldn't see the AI slop in a Chinese travel agent's message that made his wife cringe, and the reverse happens to Chinese professionals writing English with AI. Using AI to compose is "performing competence instead of building it," and "the skill you lack is the same skill needed to evaluate the output." "The performers stay perfectly polished and perfectly invisible... The builders sound ESL for a few months, then they sound like leaders". The rule: decide the message yourself ("if you can't distill your main message into a single sentence, you don't have a main message"), write it in your own English, mistakes and all — "I think we should delay the launch. The data's not ready. Can we discuss Thursday?" — and let AI fix only grammar. The same holds for interview prep: everyone using GPT sounds the same; use AI to break down the JD and surface your competencies, then use your judgment to choose — "be half machine, half human, and one hundred percent unforgettable".

### The method: the fulcrum audit

1. Acknowledge AI; stop panic-learning for roles you don't want.
2. List three things clients or colleagues value in you that a knowledge base couldn't replace (lived experience, reading the room, storytelling, judgment).
3. For each, write one way AI could free time to do more of it.
4. Re-express your AI use at the process or business level, not the task level.
5. Build "portable value" — a globally in-demand skill — as the smart risk of the era.
6. If you need brand-name AI experience on the resume now, complete a company-designed simulation (Forage: BCG's Gen AI financial chatbot, Tata's Gen AI data analytics) — "you can't always be employed, but you can always be hustling".

### Common mistakes

- Stacking AI certificates for a role you don't want (anxiety) or refusing to engage (denial).
- Listing tools in interviews instead of problems solved.
- Letting AI write your messages and your intro — "Made with AI" is visible to everyone but you.
- Trying to close every gap instead of building the spike.

## Practice set

### Drills

**Drill 1 — The endurance ledger (9.1).** Write your next career goal. Beneath it, list its three real costs (e.g., politics, self-promotion, hours). Next to each write "willing" or "not willing" — *genuinely*, not "I guess I have to." If any is "not willing," write the alternative path you will consciously own instead, and one sentence choosing it "without bitterness".

**Drill 2 — Should → How can I (9.1).** Write your three most frequent "should" sentences. Convert each to "How can I…?" For the one that matters most, answer: What do I want to feel? What can make me feel that way? Where can I actually get that? Then draft two paths (internal, external).

**Drill 3 — Rebuild the chain (9.2).** For one work behaviour (e.g., silence in meetings), write your current action → habit → character → destiny chain. Then write the rebuilt chain, starting with the smallest action ("I speak when I have an average idea"). Commit to it for two weeks and log each instance as evidence.

**Drill 4 — Design for failure + accounts audit (9.2).** Before your next presentation or interview, write the specific criticism you hope to collect; afterward, record what you learned and which path it eliminated. Same week: review last week's calendar and last month's bank statement, write the identity they "prove," and pick one action that closes the gap with the identity you claim.

**Drill 5 — Third-person rewrite, timed (9.3).** Five minutes. Choose the work story you're most ashamed of. Write it in the third person using your own name. Select the facts that most appreciate that person's contribution. Read it aloud three times and keep it as your interview version. Extension: write your career bio twice with identical facts — loser side, hero side — and circle the one you currently use.

**Drill 6 — Smart-risk sort (9.4).** List the three career moves you are considering. For each, write worst-case loss and best-case upside. Keep only those with small, bounded downside. Then write the one risk you are most afraid of and the first concrete step you could take this month.

**Drill 7 — Commit sentence rehearsal (9.4).** Take one piece of analysis you would normally just report. Append: "I'm seeing ___. And if it were my call, I'd ___ because ___." Say it aloud until it sounds like yours; use it in your next update.

**Drill 8 — Garden audit (9.5).** One paragraph each: Am I growing the seed that belongs to me or one someone else planted? Is the problem me, or is the soil starving my growth? Is my industry moving up or falling behind? Name the single layer you will adjust this quarter, and ask one person 5+ years ahead to rate your soil.

**Drill 9 — KPI self-placement (9.6).** Identify your level and write its KPI in one sentence. List three things you do that belong to the *previous* level's KPI. Pick one next-level action (a Thursday delivery, a one-sentence success definition, a documented handoff of a recurring task) and do it this week.

**Drill 10 — Market check (9.6).** Write the date of your last external interview. If it's over two years, book three exploratory conversations this month for the next-level title. Score your current role on learning (1–5) and earning (1–5); if both are below 3, write the first step of an exit plan.

**Drill 11 — "What is staying doing to me?" (9.7).** One page, before asking anyone else: what staying in your current role is doing to your skills, your confidence, and your health. Then role-play the backfill question with a partner playing your manager: *You:* "If I leave this role, would you even backfill it?" *Manager (partner improvises a vague non-answer):* "Well, we'd have to see how things shake out…" Notice how a non-answer feels.

**Drill 12 — Fulcrum audit (9.8).** Write three things colleagues value in you that a knowledge base couldn't replace, one way AI could free time for each, and one AI use rewritten at task, process and business level — keep only the highest level you can truthfully support.

## Cheat sheet

**Frameworks**
- Endurance test: not "what do I want?" but "what am I willing to endure?" → own the choice, either way
- Gun question: "Is the gun in their hand or in mine?"
- Should → "How can I…?" → feel / what produces it / where to get it → two paths
- Evidence over emotions: decide identity → smallest action, scared → collect evidence → inaction hurts more than action
- Action → Habit → Character → Destiny
- Design for failure: presentation = collect criticism; interview = earn a precise rejection
- Your accounts never lie: calendar + bank statement = who you are
- Third-person rewrite: shameful story → third person → select appreciative facts → retell
- Setback = data, not identity
- Wealth = Stretch × Stay
- Control perspective + control reactions; smart risk = bounded downside, unbounded upside
- Report → Advise → Commit
- Conflict: fight / flight / ignore (or freeze) — "will I respect myself after?"
- Garden: Seed / Soil / Climate — adjust one layer at a time
- KPI ladder: Reliability → Ownership → Team performance → Org performance
- Two-year rule: go back to the market; make your employer compete
- Learning / Earning / Leaving
- "What is staying doing to me?" + backfill question
- Direction: Skills (from real projects) → Big Five fit → Good Time Journal (AEIOU) → AI-matched titles
- Pivot: change one variable; learn who fits; three superstar stories
- AI: stakes test (autopilot / copilot); task → process → business; own-English, AI fixes grammar only
