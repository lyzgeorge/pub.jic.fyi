# Chapter 1 — What Employers Are Really Asking

This chapter shows you how to work out what an employer is actually testing, and how to find that out from people and job descriptions before you write a single answer.

![An interview question leads to the employer’s underlying concern, then to a metric and a concrete work example.](/assets/career/jeff-su-playbook/chapter-1/01-question-to-evidence.webp)

*A strong answer starts with the concern behind the question and ends with evidence the employer can assess.*

## 1.1 Where this advice comes from

This book's advice comes from one practitioner's career, not hiring research. Jeff Su interned at KPMG, spent two years as an EY consultant (New York, then Shanghai), then worked at Google as a key account manager for Chinese exporters and in product marketing for Greater China. His examples come mostly from US tech and consulting, and many rules of thumb, such as time splits and follow-up limits, come from "my experience" rather than data. The videos span 2020 to 2024, so where the advice changes, this book gives the dates.

## 1.2 Decode the hidden question

Almost every interview question has a literal form and an underlying concern, and Jeff opens nearly every video by naming what the interviewer is "really" asking.

**Why it matters.** Answering the literal wording can spring its trap. "Where do you see yourself in five years?" invites a job title, but any title is wrong: an aggressive one sounds arrogant, a conservative one questions your confidence. So write down the concern behind each question and check that every part of your answer speaks to it. Several videos split one question into two or three implicit ones and give each a share of the answer.

| Question as asked | What the interviewer is really assessing |
|---|---|
| Tell me about yourself | In order: who you are now, how you got there, what value you bring to this role. |
| Why are you a good fit? | What you can bring (about 50%), what you want from it (about 30%), what makes you unique (about 20%), a split from Jeff's experience. |
| Why do you want to work here? | Growth mindset, research on the *role*, and enthusiasm. |
| Where do you see yourself in five years? | "Are you gonna be a dependable employee who's gonna stay here for the long haul?" |
| Greatest strengths? | Humility balanced with confidence, and a match with the team's high performers. |
| Biggest weakness? | Self-awareness, and the basic competencies to work independently. |
| A time you failed | Self-awareness, determination, willingness to improve, and that you won't repeat it. |
| A conflict | Whether you accept other views and put business needs first. The outcome is "simply a by-product." |
| Why are you leaving? | Whether you stay dignified, and whether your reason will limit you in the new role. |
| Why did you leave? (layoff) | Whether you can do the job well quickly, and whether this is calculated rather than desperate. |
| Can you explain this gap? | Reliability, capability, and commitment. |
| An unpreparable question | Your thought process more than the "right" answer; Jeff passed a round with an answer the interviewer didn't want. |
| Hybrid-work questions | Whether you will succeed in and fit their setup. |
| Any questions for me? | Preparation, critical thinking, enthusiasm, and your chance to screen them. |

Decoded, the five-year question is about dependability, so the answer shifts to the journey: what you will learn and contribute in the first years, which signals commitment without promising to stay.

**Qualification.** A decoded concern tells you what to prove, not how to structure the answer (Chapters 4 to 7). Look-alike questions can need different emphasis: Jeff wants an "honest, selfish element" in "why are you a good fit" but a "mainly selfless" answer to "why do you want to work here" (see Chapter 4).

## 1.3 Research the role through people, not pages

The best research source is someone who does the role. Mission statements and news are "too high level" for "why do you want to work here," and can seem "too idealistic and not down to earth." Job descriptions, in Jeff's "unpopular opinion," are "often just too vague" for choosing a strength. Interviewers know their own high performers' qualities, and when yours match, they "start to unconsciously think about how you'd perform on the job."

**Who to ask.** Someone in the exact role, found through your alumni database or LinkedIn, or else someone with the same title at a comparable company. Jeff's EY five-year sample answer opens with "I had the opportunity to chat with a few alumni who currently work at EY." You can also end a recruiter message by asking to meet someone in the role, which gives you an "unfair advantage" and tells you whether the day-to-day suits you (Chapter 3). With professionals this interconnected, there is "no excuse not to network."

**What to ask.**

| Question | What it gives you |
|---|---|
| "What are you measured on when it comes to performance review time?" | The exact metric you'd be judged on, day-to-day examples, and a follow-up on how they hit it. |
| "Are you happy in the role? Why?" | A specific answer to what you want from the job. |
| "Think back to a recent hire you enjoyed working with. What made them stand out?" | The qualities the team values, concretely (see 1.4). |

**Where the videos disagree: the job description as a source.** An August 2020 video puts the JD in a word cloud: it "doesn't usually paint a complete picture of the role" but is "a great starting point." A November 2020 video calls JDs "often just too vague" and says to ask an insider. The 2023–2024 ChatGPT videos prompt on the JD, for example for its "three most important responsibilities," to "understand what the hiring manager is really looking for," and the LinkedIn videos mine many JDs for keywords (1.5). Since both views coexist in 2020, this is not a change of mind. Editorial guidance: use the JD for stated responsibilities and vocabulary, and insiders for what distinguishes high performers and the real day-to-day.

## 1.4 The "recent hire" question: one tool, three uses

Ask someone to think of a recent hire who impressed them and describe what made that person stand out. It forces a specific colleague to mind, so you get concrete examples instead of the generic advice "What advice do you have for someone like me?" produces.

| When | How it's used |
|---|---|
| Before | Message an insider: "Hi, sorry to bother you, but if you could think back to a recent hire on your team who you enjoyed working with, what made them stand out?" Use the answer to choose a strength. |
| During | Ask the interviewer the same, then prepare matching experiences for the next round, because "they've basically just told you the qualities they look for." |
| After | If there was no small talk, the answer gives you a specific person and example for your thank-you email (Chapter 7), and shows initiative. |

## 1.5 Extract keywords from job descriptions

Keyword extraction treats job descriptions as data on how employers describe the role. Jeff uses it mainly for LinkedIn, which works like a search engine: your profile's keywords decide how often you appear in recruiter searches. It surfaces terms you wouldn't guess; for his product marketing role it found "measurement" and "channel," and earlier "customer-centric," "growth" and "sellers," which he reads as a sign the role is cross-functional with sales.

| When | JDs | Tool | Keywords kept | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 1, the target role | Free word cloud | Most frequent | Interview examples; Experience |
| 2021 | 20 | Word cloud (e.g., MonkeyLearn) | Top 5 | About |
| 2022 | 10+ | Word cloud by frequency | Not fixed; five skills | About, Skills |
| 2024 | 5 | ChatGPT or Gemini frequency prompt | Top 3, each 9–10 times | Whole profile |

It works only for a targeted search, such as marketing roles in consumer hardware electronics; JDs from ten unrelated industries scatter. No video explains the changing count, though the move to 5 coincides with the switch to an AI prompt. Editorial guidance: whatever the count, keep the JDs to one target role. Chapter 2 covers placement; Chapter 8 gives the prompts.

## 1.6 The measurement mindset

Jeff treats knowing how success is measured as a signal in itself. Metrics show you know your goals and trust your capabilities ("Why else would you bring it up?"), and "nothing gets a hiring manager more excited than new hires demonstrating they are data driven and results oriented." The idea runs through every stage: ask role-holders what they're measured on; in "tell me about yourself," replace "delivered exceptional results" with "15 times return on investment"; in a creativity story, quantify how the solution beat the alternative; close by asking "How will my success be measured for the first one or two quarters?" and how that ties to partner teams' metrics; and on the resume and profile, avoid the most common mistake, "describing what they do instead of quantifying their impact."

Even an intern can count coffees prepared or records input, because "the reader cares more about you knowing the importance of measurement than the actual numbers themselves."

**No end result is not an excuse.** An agency professional who rolls off projects early can report pitches supported per quarter against peers, or better, the dollar value of successful pitches: "There are always meaningful data points." A student can write "my presentation scored 24% higher than the class average" instead of "I led a team presentation." Chapter 8 shows how to ask an AI tool for candidate metrics.

## 1.7 Transferable skills as the bridge

When your experience doesn't match the role, transferable skills connect what you've done to what it needs, and Jeff reaches for them with career changers, fresh graduates and hiring-manager reservations. A transferable skill "grows with you"; a tool skill may not, since the role may move away from the tool, the company may use another (you cite Monday.com, they use Notion or Asana), and insiders may know it better, which makes the claim look "presumptuous."

- **Choosing:** "If you have relevant work experience, double down on it. If you don't, mention skills you have developed." For an entry-level account manager, honesty and respect matter with clients now and a team later.
- **Ramping up:** a graduate or industry changer should show solving a hard problem with minimal guidance and how they'd repeat those steps.
- **Answering a reservation:** for a gap you can't close, such as a missing hard skill or years of experience, say you're working on it and highlight transferable skills. "The way you handle their objection matters a lot more than the objection itself." A thank-you email can add a sentence on it.
- **Transitions:** in a layoff answer, show your skills are "immediately transferable" (Chapter 6).

For a hypothetical Tesla operations role, Jeff admits his supply-chain experience "is limited to tracking my package on Amazon," so he would lead with team leadership and process optimization from B2B product marketing.

**Qualification.** The framing needs honest facts. When ChatGPT turned Jeff's Senior Management Consultant title into Product Manager, he noted you can highlight transferable skills, but you "can't change my title."

**Chapter standard:** Before you draft any answer, you can name the concern behind the question, one insight you got from someone in the role, the top keywords from a targeted set of job descriptions, and one metric and one transferable skill that prove your fit. Editorial guidance: if any of these five is missing, go back to research before you write.
