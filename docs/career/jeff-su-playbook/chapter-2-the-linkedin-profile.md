# Chapter 2 — The LinkedIn Profile

This chapter shows you how to build a LinkedIn profile that recruiters can find through search, that supports your resume without repeating it, and that signals your availability at the level of openness your situation allows.

![A searchable professional profile connects a headline, About section, achievements, and skills.](/assets/career/jeff-su-playbook/chapter-2/01-profile-architecture.webp)

*The profile works as a connected search result and proof of your professional story.*

## 2.1 What is a LinkedIn profile for?

A LinkedIn profile is a search-indexed page whose job is to bring the right people to you. Jeff borrows career coach Austin Belsak's definition: the goal is to "drive relevant traffic to you even when you're sleeping," which for a job seeker means messages from recruiters and hiring managers.

**It is found through search**: LinkedIn works like Google, ranking profiles that match recruiters' keywords and filters. **It is read as a check on you**: recruiters open it after your resume to see whether it confirms their picture. **Its goal depends on who you are**: job seekers keep changes quiet, while entrepreneurs write long summaries for prospective clients.

Jeff says "All-Star" profiles are "40 times more likely to be contacted by recruiters" and "18 times more likely to show up in hiring managers' search results," and that nine items reach that level (unsourced; the criteria may have changed since 2022).

## 2.2 Profile versus resume

The resume is tailored to one role; the profile is a general overview of you as a professional. They should tell the same story but do different jobs.

| Element | Resume | LinkedIn profile |
|---|---|---|
| Targeting | Hyper-targeted to each role | High-level, so About can be more general |
| Summary | Short one-liner | About complements the resume, with few adjectives or buzzwords |
| Achievements | Bullets cut to three or four lines | Experience adds background and context |
| Voice | Third person is common | First person ("I am…"); Jeff says 80% of respondents in one study prefer it |

Because the profile is read right after your photo and banner, it must fit the story your resume already told. A 2020 video cites 575 million users and a "recent poll" in which 73% would keep their LinkedIn profile over their resume (unsourced).

## 2.3 Photo, banner and URL

The photo and banner are the first things a visitor sees, and free tools are enough for both. Tools and menus below date from 2020–2022.

**The photo.** Use a recent photo, face clearly visible, looking at the camera and smiling, upper body in frame, and clearly separated from the background; Jeff's own earlier photo failed all three. Cut yourself out with remove.bg (the free account gives one high-resolution download, so choose carefully). In Canva, make a 1600 × 1600 px design, which stays sharp when zoomed even though LinkedIn shows about 400 × 400, and put either a free two-color gradient or a darkened, blurred, vignetted image behind you, warming the top layer slightly so you stand out. Download as PNG and avoid Canva Pro elements.

**The banner.** Start from a free Canva "LinkedIn banner" template (1500 pixels was "the most updated size" in 2022). Keep text and images to the right, since your photo covers the left. A short value tagline can work; avoid charts chosen only to "look smart," as Jeff's consulting-era banner did.

**Custom URL and website.** Replace the default URL with a clean one and use it on your resume, cover letter and email signature, especially for cold emails. Jeff cites a "71% better chance" of hearing back when a resume includes a LinkedIn link (unsourced). The intro gained a website field in 2022; if you have no site, a free link page or a Medium or Substack blog can serve.

## 2.4 Placing keywords

Keyword placement decides how often you appear in recruiter searches. Chapter 1 covers how to *extract* keywords; this section covers where to put them. The most specific rule, from the 2024 data-backed video and attributed to Sarah Johnson's study, is that each keyword should appear 9–10 times across the profile.

| Section | Mentions per keyword |
|---|---|
| Headline | 1 |
| About | 2–3 |
| Experience | 3–5 |
| Skills | 1 |
| **Total** | **9–10** (27–30 for three keywords) |

This works only when your target roles are consistent; keywords from ten unrelated industries scatter and help nothing.

The videos disagree on About versus Experience; see the table in 2.11. Editorial guidance: the 2024 allocation is the most recent and compatible with the 2020 point: niche terms concentrate in Experience, and About uses your core keywords without becoming a keyword list.

## 2.5 The headline

The headline is the line under your name, and LinkedIn says it is a major factor in search. The 2024 video cites Sarah Johnson's study: 46% of respondents see it as the most important section, yet only 20% of users update it (no link given).

**Three mistakes to avoid:** uncommon abbreviations; your job title alone, which Jeff says 99% of users use and which makes you identical to peers; and "seeking opportunities" or "open to work," which recruiters don't search for.

**How the advice changed.**

| | 2022 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | Title + a one-sentence value line (credited to Belsak) | Belsak's two parts: keyword overview, then a value line with results |
| Example | "Product Marketing at Google" + "I bring boring ad products to life." | Keywords separated by bars (e.g., product marketing, digital advertising), then a line on improving small businesses' return on ad spend (paraphrased; metric left as a placeholder) |

Both keep the value line; 2024 replaces the title with keywords.

**Writing it.** Condense your job into one sentence, as Jeff turned "explain complex Google Ads features so small-business owners understand them" into his value line. For models, look at optimized profiles of people at your target companies (search method in Chapter 3).

## 2.6 The About section

The About section should make your audience reach out to you, with proof, not adjectives. The opening line matters most: the 2024 video cites Sarah Johnson's study that 84% of people read the first sentence, 32% of those read all of it, and 65% of users, many of them recruiters and hiring managers, have contacted a stranger because of something on their profile (no link given).

**Belsak's job-seeker templates.**

| Part | Three-part version (2021, 2022) | Four-part version (2024) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Intro: your field + years of experience + specific value you've brought | Strong intro with keywords and metrics |
| 2 | Three case-study bullets, like resume bullets, matched to the target role | Three strongest achievements |
| 3 | A close naming the work that excites you, without saying you're looking | "I get excited about opportunities where I get to do X." |
| 4 | none | Contact information |

**Length.** Belsak's examples run 880–1,120 characters, and there is no perfect length. The limit was 2,000 characters as of 2021. Draft first, then check the count.

**Example script** (Jeff notes the figures are "clearly hypothetical"):

> Intro: "Innovative product marketing manager with 7+ years of experience in B2B marketing. I help companies identify high-potential leads and I'm responsible for US$121 million in acquisition revenue, representing 78% growth year on year."
>
> Close: "I love applying my B2B lead-gen experience to help tech companies reach high-growth SMBs to further scale their businesses."

**Refinements.** Students should use internships and extracurriculars with metrics (see Chapter 1). Keep buzzwords minimal; "innovative" is acceptable only because the bullets prove it. Add one or two safe interests (food, travel, family, sports, hobbies) with one specific detail, such as the team you follow, for an instant point of connection. Use Unicode bold or emojis sparingly: "if everything is highlighted, nothing is highlighted." Write in first person and proofread with a tool such as the Hemingway app.

## 2.7 Experience

The Experience section expands resume bullets into fuller evidence under standard titles recruiters search for.

- **Expand, don't paste.** Copying the resume isn't harmful but wastes the space; add the background and context resume bullets leave out.
- **Always quantify, even without end results** (see 1.6): "There are always meaningful data points."
- **Reuse your performance review.** A good self-review has the same ingredients: what you did, the result, and the data. Jeff pasted his latest review in after removing internal-only information; update LinkedIn at each self-review deadline.
- **Use your highest title**, and if you took on an extra role, keep the one title you want to be known for and list the added duties in the description. Several titles confuse headhunters filling one role.
- **Use LinkedIn-standard titles.** Jeff's internal "torso account strategist" became "key account manager," the term recruiters search. Career Explorer (2023) shows standard titles.

## 2.8 Featured

The Featured section shows your work instead of describing it, and the advice broadens over time from "a portfolio" to "everyone has something to feature."

| Who | What to feature |
|---|---|
| Students | High-scoring classwork, research projects, case-competition decks |
| Professionals with public work | Website, portfolio, externally published projects |
| Professionals without public work | A post where someone thanked and tagged you, or your own decks and spreadsheets with confidential material removed |
| Job seekers | A shared Google Drive link titled "My Resume," description blank so it is one click away instead of two |
| After a milestone | Your post about a finished project, tagging teammates (see Chapter 3) |

## 2.9 Skills, endorsements and assessments

The Skills section is a search filter, and only endorsed skills count.

- **Recruiters filter on skills.** The 2024 video cites Sarah Johnson's study that 45% of recruiters actively reference Skills, a figure Jeff doubted until he found the Skills filter in LinkedIn Recruiter. LinkedIn says profiles with five or more skills get 17 times more views (unsourced beyond "LinkedIn").
- **Only endorsed skills count.** As Jeff reports LinkedIn, a skill needs at least one endorsement, so adding 50 skills doesn't help. Pick five or more from your keyword research (Chapter 1) and ask friends, family and colleagues to endorse them; per Belsak, endorsers who list the same skill weigh more.
- **Skill assessments** (dated: as of 2023, and LinkedIn may since have retired them). Each had 15 multiple-choice questions, and a top-30% score earned a badge and a higher ranking in recruiter searches. Jeff's ranking: badge plus endorsements > many endorsements > few. He suggested Office tools early in a career and industry tools (Adobe, AWS, Google Ads) later.

## 2.10 Signalling that you are open to work

How openly you signal depends on whether you are employed: covertly if you are, overtly but framed if you are between jobs.

### Covert signals (employed)

- **Hide profile edits before you start** by turning off "Share profile updates with your network" in visibility settings; otherwise colleagues are notified of every change.
- **Close the About section with the work that excites you.** Belsak's image: you and your boss are at a bar next to a recruiter from your dream company, and you want to tip off the recruiter without alarming your manager.
- **Keep "open to work" out of the headline**, where everyone sees it.
- **Click "I'm interested" on company pages** (2023) to join that company's recruiter view. It expires after a year, not every company enables it, and Jeff said not to expect many messages at first.

### Overt signals (between jobs)

| Option | How | Jeff's view |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn's Career Break (2023) | An Experience entry that shows recruiters the reason for a gap | Useful for explaining the gap |
| Target-role entry | An Experience entry titled with the roles you want, "LinkedIn" as the company (for the logo), describing what you've been doing, with metrics | His pick; "career break" or "position eliminated" makes a weak first impression |

"The choice is yours; both options are available." For explaining a gap in an interview, see Chapter 6.

## 2.11 Where the videos disagree

| Question | Earlier position | Later position | How to read it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keywords in About or Experience? | About holistic; niche keywords are "more relevant in the Experiences section anyways" (2020, for a profile complementing a tailored resume) | Keywords in About (2021–2022); spread everywhere, 2–3 in About (2024) | Most weight in Experience, some in About (2.4) |
| Headline base | Title + value (2022) | Keywords + value with results (2024) | Both keep the value line; 2024 adds search terms |
| About template | Three parts (2021–2022) | Four parts, adding contact information (2024) | Same core; later version eases contact |
| Open-to-work signal | Covert close in About (2021, 2024) | Overt target-role entry between jobs (2023) | Different conditions: employed vs. not |

**Chapter standard:** A finished profile has a clear photo and a right-weighted banner, a keyword-plus-value headline, a first-person About of roughly 900–1,100 characters that proves its claims and closes on the work that excites you, and Experience entries with standard titles and metrics. It also has at least five endorsed skills, something real in Featured, and an open-to-work signal that fits your employment situation, with edit notifications turned off before you begin.
