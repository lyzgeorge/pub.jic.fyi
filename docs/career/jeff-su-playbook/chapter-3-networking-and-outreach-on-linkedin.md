# Chapter 3 — Networking and Outreach on LinkedIn

This chapter shows you how to find the people most likely to help you into a target role, reach them with requests they will accept, and stay visible to them over time.

## 3.1 Why do weak ties matter?

Acquaintances are more likely than close friends to help you find your next job. Close friends "swim in the same pool of information" as you, so opportunities come from "friendly outsiders." Jeff attributes this "strength of weak ties" to Mark Granovetter and says 84% of respondents in "one of the most influential research papers on sociology" got their job through a weak relationship (no paper given). His own Google job came through a friend who was then "acquaintances at best."

**Get your profile ready first (see Chapter 2).** People check it before they accept or reply.

## 3.2 Finding the right people

Purposeful outreach beats blind outreach: filtering by role or company is what makes a connection relevant. Target people in the exact role you want; recruiters, #Hiring posters and recent joiners at target companies; and first- and second-degree connections, who are likeliest to agree to a coffee chat. The menus below are as recorded and may have moved.

| Method | How | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| College alumni filter | College page → Alumni → type a title, or "company, function" (e.g., "Nike, marketing"); "Where they work" shows headcount per company | Learning from someone ahead of you in a field or at one company |
| Company People tab | Company page → People → filter location, department, 1st/2nd-degree | The warmest contacts inside one company; also works on a former employer's page |
| Group messaging | Group → members → message anyone | Messaging without connecting or paying for Premium, even third-degree contacts |
| Boolean search | e.g., `Facebook AND ("Account Manager" OR "Marketing Manager")` → People → 2nd-degree | Precise lists by role and company, using Google-style operators |
| Post search by author company | Empty search → Posts → All filters → Author company | Active users at target companies, who are likely to answer comments |

**Using groups well.** You must be admitted first. Students should join school, alumni and high-school groups; once working, your company's group is "most likely to help you down the line." To branch into a field, search it, filter to Groups, and join one where you genuinely share a background, but never pose as another company's employee. The tools combine: paste an alumnus you found at a target company into your alumni *group* search, where there is a "very good chance" you can message them. A specific request posted to a group also works: an Emory senior who asked which classes suit business continuity consulting got several alumni replies within 24 hours.

## 3.3 Connection requests

Every request should be personalized. The videos give two overlapping but distinct frameworks.

**Framework A: Jeff's three message types (2020).** Something *relatable*, a specific *compliment*, or *added value*. These are alternatives, so use one or combine two. Two real requests he received:

> Relatable + compliment: "Hi Jeff, came across your Q&A video on YouTube. I've really enjoyed learning about your career trajectory, especially the EdTech-from-management-consulting part. I'm an education student interested in exploring opportunities in consulting. Would love to stay connected."

> Added value: "...I think you could also try content on LinkedIn and job searching. Tried and true topic, see if your audience likes it."

The second, from a high-school friend he'd lost touch with, read as a genuine wish to help, and his LinkedIn content exists because of it.

**Framework B: Austin Belsak's three steps, as modified by Jeff (2021).** An ordered sequence. First, *lead with positivity*, ideally about them ("Congrats on starting a new blog"). Second, *show them you know them*, "where the magic happens," choosing one angle from easiest to hardest: their career path, something they posted, or an offer of value drawn from their other social media (a friend of Jeff's saw a VP's Instagram post about renovating and sent paint recommendations). Third, *ask a genuine question*, which builds them up and shows serious thought.

> "Hi Jeff, hope you're doing well... I've been following your YouTube channel for a while... I have one question for you: how do you think one should react or prepare when he is the one approached by the company for a position?"

| | Framework A (2020) | Framework B (2021) |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Three alternative message types | Three ordered steps |
| Core move | Relate, compliment, or add value | Show you know them: path, then content, then value |
| Ending | None required ("would love to connect") | A genuine question |
| "Add value" | One of three equal options | The hardest angle within step 2 |
| Used for | General requests; recruiter notes | Follow-up requests after commenting on posts |

Later videos refer back to "a simple three-step process" and to connection templates without restating either one. Editorial guidance: A tells you what content earns attention, and B gives the message a shape and a reason to reply. A request built with B can use A's relatable or compliment material in step 2.

**Mechanics for both.** On mobile, never use the default Connect button, which sends a generic request; use More → Personalize invite. Stay considered even with first-degree connections you haven't spoken to in a while, track new contacts so follow-ups happen, and pay it forward.

## 3.4 Commenting to stay visible

Thoughtful comments put your name in front of recruiters and hiring managers before you ask for anything, and because people at one company are usually connected, one comment can reach all their radars. Jeff says only 1.2% of users actively engage (unsourced).

**What a good comment looks like.** By Jeff's own estimate, about 95% of comments on "I just started a new job" posts are plain congratulations. Instead, politely ask for one tip that helped them succeed. On general posts, skip "thanks for sharing" and spend five minutes adding something, such as a related experience. The person may reply, tagged recruiters may check your profile, and you gain a natural reason for a follow-up request (3.3).

**Cadence by audience.**

| Audience | Cadence | Then |
|---|---|---|
| Active job seekers | Meaningful comments on **five posts a day for two weeks**, targeting recruiters, #Hiring posters and new joiners | Send requests using Belsak's steps; Jeff "guarantees" meaningful progress toward a role at a target company |
| Employed professionals not looking | **One thoughtful comment every Friday afternoon for a month** | Likes build confidence to comment more, then to post a key learning from work |

The two serve different goals, weeks-long results versus a habit, so they do not contradict each other.

**Expect a funnel.** Jeff jokes with an illustrative dating-app funnel (100 swipes, 10 matches, 5 replies, 2 dates): 100 comments still yield replies, a wider network and coffee chats, which beats "blindly applying online." As Belsak puts it, "You can't do the same thing as everybody else and expect different results."

## 3.5 Reaching recruiters: the three-step sequence

Recruiters are flooded with applicants. You stand out by asking them to connect you with someone in the role, after earning that ask. It shows you are proactive and a problem-solver (the quickest way for an outsider to judge fit is to talk to someone doing the job), and it is a more considerate favor than "can I pick your brain for 30 minutes?" The conversation also gives you an "unfair advantage" in the interview and shows whether you want the day-to-day work.

In Jeff's hypothetical example ("in case my manager comes across this video"), he is a Google product marketer in Shanghai approaching Apple recruiter "Annie" about a Product Marketing Manager posting.

1. **Connect without mentioning the job.** Use Framework A; the only goal is acceptance.

   > "Hi Annie, I see that we're both working for MNC tech companies and based out of Shanghai. I also noticed you pursued your bachelor's degree at UNNC, where our company recently ran a college outreach program. Would love to connect if possible."

2. **After acceptance, ask for an introduction.** With rapport established, a longer message won't seem too direct.

   > "...I saw the Product Marketing Manager job posting from two weeks ago, and I'm currently doing a bit of research on the role to see whether I'd be a good fit. Would you happen to know anyone on the team I could have a casual chat with to better understand the position? As a fellow product marketer working in the tech space myself, I'd love to learn more about the day-to-day and team culture before I bother you with specific questions on the application process."

3. **Close the loop after the chat.** Cite something specific you learned, confirm your interest, propose a next step, and name a skill the recruiter likely wants.

   > "...my conversation with Jane went very well... I had no idea the iOS App Store was creating so many opportunities for Greater China app developers while adhering to Apple's strict privacy policies... I would love to send over my resume for your review if convenient. I sincerely believe the stakeholder management skills I've developed as a product marketer at Google would transfer over well to this role at Apple."

**A no still helps.** Recruiters who decline often offer to answer your questions themselves, or to forward your resume to the hiring manager, keeping it out of the general pile. Jeff says the templates worked "for both me and my community" (anecdotal). For follow-up after interviews, see Chapter 7.

## 3.6 Recommendations

Recommendations are LinkedIn's five-star reviews, and they are easiest to get when feedback is already being written. If a colleague has just written your peer review, ask: "Would you mind terribly if I asked for a recommendation on LinkedIn as well?" Otherwise, follow Belsak. Send a template asking for comments on the specific skills you want highlighted, and use reciprocity right after a project ends: "I learned a lot about project management from you. More than happy to write your recommendation on LinkedIn. Is it cool if you do the same for me?" Request one from their profile via More → Request a recommendation (2022 menus).

## 3.7 Posting and project posts

Posting makes you visible because few people do it. Jeff cites Kinsta: 1% of users create more than 99% of LinkedIn content (unsourced). For employed professionals, posting follows the commenting habit; start with one key learning from work. When you finish a meaningful project, post your contribution, tag the teammates who made it possible, and add it to Featured (see Chapter 2). This shows more than the About section can and thanks your coworkers, a trick "not many people" know. Thank-you posts that tag you can go in Featured too.

**Self-promotion anxiety.** Jeff still feels it. Paraphrasing Ali Abdaal and Roberto Blake, he says that if you don't respect your work enough to market it, you can't expect others to. He also cites Garry Tan's advice to start before you're ready and iterate.

## 3.8 The restriction risk

Low-quality requests can get your account restricted. This account is second-hand from a recruiter friend and dates from 2020. The sign is that LinkedIn asks for an email address each time you add someone. The reported cause is five people clicking "I don't know this person." The fixes are a Help Center ticket (search "account restricted") or LinkedIn's help account on Twitter. Requests don't need to be formal, but they need to be real.

## 3.9 Where the videos differ

| Question | Positions | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| How to write a request | Pick-one message types (2020) vs. ordered steps ending in a question (Belsak, 2021) | Complementary: A is content, B is structure (3.3) |
| How much to comment | Five a day for two weeks (2021) vs. one every Friday for a month (2022) | Depends on audience: job seekers vs. employed (3.4) |
| How to find people | Alumni filters and groups (2020), then Boolean search, post search and refined People-tab filters (2020–2024) | Additive: later videos add methods |

**Chapter standard:** You have a short, filtered list of people in or near your target role, found through alumni, company, group, Boolean or post searches. Every request you send is personalized with a clear choice of framework. You comment at a cadence that fits your situation, you have asked at least one recruiter for an introduction rather than a favor, and nothing you send would lead a stranger to click "I don't know this person."
