# Chapter 4 — Opening and Motivation Answers

This chapter shows you how to build the four answers that set up most interviews: "tell me about yourself," "why are you a good fit," "why do you want to work here," and "where do you see yourself in five years."

![Three linked panels show past achievements, a current role, and a future team contribution.](/assets/career/jeff-su-playbook/chapter-4/01-present-past-future.webp)

*The opening answer connects your current role, relevant past evidence, and future contribution.*

## 4.1 What these four questions have in common

Each question hides a narrower one, and a strong answer responds to that hidden question (see Chapter 1).

| Question as asked | What the interviewer is really asking | Framework |
|---|---|---|
| Tell me about yourself | Who you are now, how you got there, and what value you bring | Present, Past, Future |
| Why are you a good fit? / Why should we hire you? | What you bring, what you want from the role, and what makes you unique | Three questions, 50/30/20 |
| Why do you want to work here? | Your growth mindset, your research, and your enthusiasm | Three questions, mainly selfless |
| Where do you see yourself in five years? | Whether you will be dependable and stay | Specific to Broad |

The answers also connect. The future section of "tell me about yourself" makes the same case as "why are you a good fit," and the "why here" answer is reused in the transition answers of Chapter 6.

## 4.2 Tell me about yourself: Present, Past, Future

"Tell me about yourself" (TMAY) is a short case for your fit in three time frames, following the interviewer's order of interest. Jeff calls it "the most important interview question to get right" because it sets the tone and gives you an early confidence boost. It is not a one-sided self-introduction: its job is to connect what you do now with what the new role needs.

**Present.** Say what you do, the metrics you are measured against, and a recent achievement you are proud of. This marks you first as a professional (not "the town you grew up in"), shows you are results-oriented, and plants follow-up questions.

> "I'm currently a management consultant with Ernst & Young focused on finance shared service center projects. My primary contributions are, one, organizing training workshops for clients adopting the new SAP system, and two, putting together pitch decks through market research for future business development. A recent one I'd like to share is when I used data from a free trial of eMarketer to put together a deck on B2B marketing trends in China, which ended up helping my senior manager close a $500,000 deal."

(The figures are illustrative.)

**Past.** Identify two or three attributes the role needs (communication for sales, attention to detail for accounting), pick the experiences that best show them, and do "the hardest step": choose one key moment in each as its highlight (4.3). Reframe the same experience for each role. In Jeff's hypothetical example, an events coordinator applying for marketing stresses teaching herself Canva and MailChimp and training 30 coordinators (98% satisfaction); for accounting, she stresses managing the year's budget. Students start with their field of study, then projects, internships and leadership roles, and should care about the qualities a story conveys, not how impressive it is.

**Future.** Reinforce your fit so the interviewer can "imagine ever so slightly" you on their team.

**Editing a draft.** Treat an AI or first draft as "80% there." Replace fluff ("delivered exceptional results") with facts ("15 times return on investment"), keep only the most relevant past experience, and add a short- and long-term bridge: "In the short term, I believe my existing skill set will help with a faster ramp up, while in the longer term I'm excited to round out my B2B capabilities in the sales operations team." Make the future section company-specific, since one that "can apply to any company" is too vague. Jeff's demonstration uses a Netflix role where he has "zero experience," showing that the future section can address that gap. Drafting prompts are in Chapter 8.

**Where the videos disagree: the time budget.**

| Version | Present | Past | Future | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The 2020 version makes the past the showcase; the late-2023 version makes it the shortest section, treating the answer as a bridge to the new role. Editorial guidance: the later split suits career changers, whose past is least related; the earlier suits candidates whose past is the evidence. Either way, stay near two minutes. The 2023 video also claims that 33% of interviewers decide within the first 90 seconds (unsourced).

## 4.3 The highlight method

Put only a small, impressive fragment of a longer prepared story into your TMAY answer. It keeps the answer concise and, done well, triggers a follow-up ("Wow, which eMarketer report was this?") that lets you tell the whole story, so a great TMAY shapes what the interviewer asks next. Have the full story ready. Jeff's example is one sentence: as a Google product marketer, he pivoted a training event from three days offline to three weeks online for "a five times increase in eligible signups."

**On STAR.** The 2020 videos tell you to prepare highlight and value-proposition stories in STAR. Later videos favor CARL, which adds a learning. Chapter 5 compares the two.

## 4.4 Why are you a good fit? Bring, get, unique

"Why are you a good fit?" (also "Why should we hire you?") asks three underlying questions. Answer all three in set proportions over two to two and a half minutes. The 50/30/20 split comes from Jeff's own experience.

| Underlying question | Share | How to prepare |
|---|---|---|
| What can you bring? | 50% | Identify the role's main themes (Chapter 1). If you have relevant experience, "double down on it" and frame the role as a new challenge; if you are new to the field, highlight transferable attributes. Prepare a full STAR story. |
| What do you want to get out of it? | 30% | Picture doing the job well: satisfaction, growing expertise, new connections. Ask someone in the role whether they are happy, and why. |
| What makes you unique? | 20% | Name something genuinely uncommon and relate it to the role, or a common strength with a memorable example. |

**The selfish element.** Jeff argues that an "honest, selfish element" makes you genuine and memorable. In his Google account manager interview, he said he got "a rush" from seeing clients adopt his Google Ads recommendations immediately, where consulting results took months. This showed he was results-driven, had researched the job's core platform, and was honest enough to admit the "dopamine hit." It works only if it is specific and tied to the job's real mechanics.

**The unique element.** The reason is "very simple and slightly superficial": to be memorable. Jeff's example is an upbringing split between the US and Greater China, tied to a multinational employer, though it hardly makes him a "special snowflake." The alternative is a common strength told memorably: his proactivity, shown by making how-to YouTube videos for sales clients. For a role outside your experience, lead with transferable strengths. For Tesla operations, where his supply-chain experience is "limited to tracking my package on Amazon," he would lead with team leadership and process optimization.

## 4.5 Why do you want to work here? Growth, research, enthusiasm

This question asks three implicit questions, and unlike the fit question, the answer should "mainly focus on selfless reasons."

**1. Do you have a growth mindset?** Frame the move as growth, not pay. Within your industry, present a new challenge: moving from small to large accounts is "a natural extension," and joining a smaller firm lets you bring corporate best practices to a startup. If you are new to the industry or a fresh graduate, describe a hard problem you solved and how you would repeat those steps, to show you can ramp up with little guidance. Jeff says a growth mindset has been "scientifically" proven to outperform a fixed one (unsourced).

**2. How much research have you done?** Research the role, not just the company. Mission statements are "too high level," and quoting Google's mission for an account manager role seems "too idealistic." Ask someone in the role what they are measured on at review time (see Chapter 1). Jeff learned that slide-making mattered for junior consultants, so he mentioned teaching himself Apple Keynote, which implied his work would need less review.

**3. How enthusiastic are you?** Show it in your delivery: rehearse in a mirror or on video, and "smile, eyebrows up and nod" while the interviewer talks. Jeff says "research has shown" that, all else equal, companies value enthusiasm over a particular skill set, because skills can be trained (unsourced). Salary, location and commute "suck the energy out." A Google colleague interviewing a hardware-team intern saw his "eyes lit up" and heard him cite MKBHD, and they talked for an hour past the interview.

**Reuse.** Keep this answer ready as a module. The forward-looking part of the answers on leaving a job, a layoff and an employment gap reuses it (see Chapter 6).

**Where the videos disagree: selfish or selfless.** The fit video (2020) wants a selfish element worth 30%; the "why here" video a month later says "mainly selfless." The later video names the contrast itself, so this is scoping by question, not a change of mind. **When asked why they should hire you, include what you want; when asked why you want to work there, lead with what you give.** Either way, the selfish element must be specific and grounded in the role, never salary, location or commute.

## 4.6 Where do you see yourself in five years? Specific to Broad

The real question is whether you will be dependable and stay, so describe the journey, not a title. Any title is wrong: an aggressive one sounds arrogant, a conservative one suggests low confidence. Jeff himself joined consulting expecting to stay and left for Google two years later.

**The formula.** For the first one to two years, *be specific*, using research from Glassdoor, LinkedIn and alumni about the day-to-day. For the remaining two to three years, *be broad*, because uncertainty is greater. This lets you name skills you want to develop and signals willingness to stay without a promise. Mentoring new hires and helping pitch work imply promotion without naming a title.

> "I had the opportunity to chat with a few alumni who currently work at EY, and I understand that first-year consultants are generalists... So for the first one to two years, I look forward to keeping an open mind and absorbing as much industry-specific knowledge as I can... For the two to three years after that, I look forward to specializing in one business vertical and really expanding my network and capabilities in that area... So to sum up, in five years I see myself working on projects across different industries, specializing in one business vertical, and hopefully adding value along each step of the way."

Jeff's EY answer also names a skill he brings (project management from a KPMG internship).

**An inconsistency in the video.** It first says "one to three year intervals," then "two time intervals" in the recap, and the sample uses two. Editorial guidance: follow the recap and the sample.

**Chapter standard:** Each of your four answers should respond to the hidden question, fit within about two minutes, contain at least one objective metric or specific research finding, and end on a clear link to this role. Your "why here" answer should be written as a reusable module, and every highlight should have a full story behind it that you can tell on request.
