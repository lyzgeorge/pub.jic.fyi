# Chapter 5 — Evidence Under Pressure: Behavioral and Situational Answers

This chapter gives you two frameworks, CARL for past experiences and RCS for hypothetical ones, and shows how to apply them to strengths, weaknesses, failure, conflict, and common hybrid-work questions.

![A four-step story moves from a challenge to action, a measured result, and a lesson applied later.](/assets/career/jeff-su-playbook/chapter-5/01-story-to-learning.webp)

*Behavioral answers become more useful when the result and learning are tied back to the role.*

## 5.1 Two kinds of evidence question

**Behavioral** questions ask how you handled a real past situation ("Tell me about a time when..."); answer them with CARL. **Situational** questions ask what you would do in a scenario you have not faced ("Imagine if..."); open them with RCS.

Jeff states that "over 90% of non-technical interview questions" fall into these two categories (unsourced). Two frameworks therefore cover most of what you will be asked. Generate the likeliest questions for a role from its job description (Chapter 8).

## 5.2 From STAR to CARL

CARL (Context, Action, Results, Learning) is a story structure that ends on what you learned rather than on the result. Jeff calls it "STAR on steroids."

**Purpose.** A behavioral question lets the interviewer "guesstimate how well you would perform in the new role." STAR stops at the result, showing you succeeded once. CARL keeps Context, Action, and quantified Results and adds a Learning, ideally one you applied later, showing you can repeat the result. Keep it short: Jeff caps a drafted answer at 260 words, and general answers at two to three minutes.

**Example: one sentence changes the ending.** Jeff once answered "What are your strengths?" in pure STAR: claiming communication, he described persuading the CFO of a Shanghai advertising-agency client to adopt a less extreme proposal, which reduced compliance issues. His fix adds a learning: it was *because of* strong communication that he could persuade the CFO, tying the story back to the strength. "A small adjustment... can make quite a big difference."

**Where the videos disagree: when to use STAR.**

| Date | Position |
|---|---|
| August 2020 | Prepare the story behind a TMAY highlight or your "what you bring" claim in STAR. |
| November 2020 | Don't rely completely on STAR; for reflective questions such as strengths, add the Learning. |
| 2021–2022 | CARL is "a superior answer structure to the STAR method." |
| January 2024 | CARL is the framework for behavioral questions in general: "I honestly don't understand why the CARL method doesn't get talked about more." |

Editorial guidance: since CARL keeps every STAR element and adds one, prepare your story bank in CARL, and drop the Learning when a highlight or follow-up leaves no time.

## 5.3 RCS: answering the question you did not prepare for

RCS (Rephrase, Clarify, Structure) is a three-step opening for situational or unexpected questions. It buys thinking time and makes your reasoning visible.

**Purpose: the answer isn't the point.** Interviewers know smart candidates need time to think, and usually allow it except on basics such as TMAY. RCS buys that time "in a natural, non-awkward way" and shows you won't blurt out the first thing that comes to mind. "The answer isn't the point here."

**Mechanism.** **Rephrase** the question to confirm you understood it, **clarify** the ambiguous parts, then ask for a minute to **structure** your thoughts, on paper if needed. The video's script (lightly cleaned):

> **Interviewer:** Imagine an irritated customer demands an impossible request. What would you do?
> **Rephrase:** To make sure I got it right, you're asking how I would handle an upset customer who's asking for something we can't realistically provide, correct?
> **Clarify:** Is it impossible because of company policy, resource constraints, or some other reason? Have we faced similar requests before? If so, how were they handled?
> **Structure:** Got it. Can I please get one minute to structure my thoughts?

**The fuller 2021 version: segment and answer.** RCS (2024) condenses a three-stage process from a 2021 video, which continues past the stall:

1. **Stall for time** by clarifying, repeating the question back, and asking for a minute.
2. **Break the question into segments.** With little information, make your own assumptions and divide the problem so the segments are MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive), a principle Jeff says was "coined by McKinsey" (unsourced).
3. **Answer each segment**, stating your assumptions aloud.

**Example: a wrong answer with clear logic still passed.** Jeff was asked, "If I spoke with 10 random people in your life right now, how many of them would dislike you?" Clarifying and repeating the question bought "at least two minutes." He split the ten into five personal and five professional contacts, estimated dislike in each, and arrived at three. The reasoning "isn't scientific," but it was clear.

He passed, but the interviewer later said he should have segmented by the teams the role worked with and their objectives, since some might dislike him when his decisions clashed with their goals, which is acceptable "as long as I'm doing what's right for the business." **Distinction:** any MECE split is "a way," not "the way," but the best one follows the role's context.

## 5.4 Strengths: transferable, relevant, and closed with a learning

The strengths question balances humility with confidence. Jeff frames it as three mistakes.

| Mistake | Why it fails | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| A hard or technical skill ("Excel guru," Salesforce CRM) | The role or company may use other tools, and insiders may know it better | A transferable skill that "grows with you," e.g. honesty and respect for an account manager |
| A strength irrelevant to the role ("creative thinking" for accounting) | It signals "this person did not do the research" | A strength that matches the team's high performers, so the interviewer "unconsciously" pictures you in the job |
| Relying completely on STAR | The story ends on the result, not the strength | CARL, with the Learning re-emphasizing the strength (5.2) |

In Jeff's "unpopular opinion," job descriptions are "often just too vague," so ask an insider what made a recent hire stand out (Chapter 1).

## 5.5 Weakness: honest, acceptable, and still in progress

A good weakness answer names a real shortcoming that doesn't break the job and shows what you are doing about it. It is "not okay to say a weakness that's really a strength in disguise."

1. **Stay away from deal breakers.** Poor multitasking, weak attention to detail, or not being a people person tells a manager they would have to micromanage and double-check you.
2. **Highlight a skill expected of someone more senior.** Nobody expects it at your level, and it shows a growth mindset. A rep plans the next account visit; a manager decides which verticals to bet on, so for a junior role "strategic thinking" is relatively safe.
3. **Give a concrete example in CARL.** Specific detail shows self-awareness and lets you "sneak in" the steps you are taking.

**The sample answer** (condensed): "My biggest weakness is that sometimes I lack the confidence to present my work, especially in a larger group setting." Given two days to present a quarterly business review section, the speaker practiced and sent the slides to other teams as a pre-read so questions could be handled offline. "While I can't say I'm suddenly a confident speaker, I think aligning with team members ahead of a meeting like that is a great way to avoid surprises and therefore make me less nervous."

**Distinction: the tip and the sample don't match.** Tip 2 recommends a senior-level skill; the sample uses presentation confidence, a personal development area. The video offers both without comment. Editorial guidance: either passes if it avoids deal breakers, is specific, and shows ongoing improvement rather than a finished cure.

## 5.6 Failure: define it, keep it single, end on a learning

"Tell me about a time you failed" tests self-awareness, determination, and willingness to improve. The basics: use a specific example, take responsibility without blaming others, and avoid a failure so large it makes you look like a liability. Three advanced tips:

1. **Define failure in your own words**, e.g. "My definition of a failure is over-promising and under-delivering." This is "not a trick": it removes ambiguity, the interviewer will likely follow your lead, and it puts you "in a position of strength."
2. **Focus on one thing that went wrong**, a one-time mistake rather than a pattern. A social media manager should pick overcommitting and learning to say no, not repeated typos (a deal breaker, 5.5).
3. **End with a learning rather than a result**, using CARL and naming a later situation where you applied it.

**Sample answer:** Jeff's own example from joining a product marketing team was shown on screen only, so it is not reproduced here.

## 5.7 Conflict and difficult situations: the outcome is a by-product

"Tell me about a time you handled a difficult situation" tests two behaviors, not a happy ending. A "perfect fairy tale ending" sounds too good to be true and falls apart under follow-up; a less-than-ideal outcome can still be powerful. Hiring managers know conflict is inevitable and often healthy (Jeff cites an unnamed Harvard Business Review study).

| Behavior | How to show it | CARL section |
|---|---|---|
| You listen to and accept others' views | "Hold your tongue." Ask the other party why they believe they are right and paraphrase it back; Jeff quotes Nelson Mandela, "Be the last one to speak." Feeling heard "is half the battle," even if their idea is rejected. | Action |
| You put business needs above your own | At the moment everyone risks losing face, de-escalate by "providing a platform for everyone to step down on" (a cool-off and a revisit next day, or admitting the other idea may be better), then show how the business benefited. | Results |

For Context, pick a conflict you could face in the new role, such as influencing without authority. For Learning, recap what you did, why, and what you would do next time.

**Sample answer:** shown in the video only. Jeff notes the candidate stayed open "even if it meant extra work," would "suffer a short-term loss for the benefit of the client and business," and got a "less than perfect" result.

## 5.8 Hybrid work and other common questions

A February 2022 video lists seven questions for judging whether a candidate will succeed in a hybrid workplace. It opens with the claims that "52 percent of us want a hybrid model," that hybrid-work searches rose "up to 400 percent" in two years, and that "research has shown" benefits (all unsourced). Editorial guidance: the list reflects the 2022 context, but several items are useful behavioral questions anywhere.

| Question | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1. Experience working from home or hybrid? | Explain how you structure your time (meetings in office, focus work at home) and stress adaptability; if none, stress communication and time management. |
| 2. How do you manage your time and stay organized? | Describe your workflow and apps: "At the start of every workday I go through the task list on my Notion dashboard, then I use the inbox zero method to clear out my inbox, and finally go through my calendar." |
| 3. What distributed-team tools have you used? | Don't list tools; give an example of improving a process. Ask the recruiter which tools they use. |
| 4. How will you coordinate with coworkers? | "The big one." CARL, with upfront questions, check-ins, and timeline expectations in Action. |
| 5. How do you deal with uncertainty or change? | CARL, showing resilience and a solution found with minimal guidance. |
| 6. A time you added creativity to your work | CARL, quantified, e.g. Kahoot quizzes giving "an 89% participation rate" (illustrative). |
| 7. What self-care strategies do you use? | Be honest: going offline after hours, a separate workspace, a routine. |

The pattern: replace a list with an example, use CARL for the past, and show steps someone could repeat.

## 5.9 Preparing the answers efficiently

1. Generate the ten likeliest behavioral and ten likeliest situational questions from the job description (Chapter 8).
2. Draft each behavioral answer in CARL, with one specific example, under about 260 words.
3. Practice the RCS opening aloud for each situational question, then segmenting and answering.
4. Check answers against the traps: tool-based strengths, disguised strengths, pattern failures, fairy-tale endings.
5. Keep all questions and answers in one document.

**Chapter standard:** Every behavioral story should state a real context, your own actions, a measurable or honestly imperfect result, and a learning you have applied since. For every situational question, you should be able to rephrase it, ask one useful clarifying question, and lay out segments that point back to the role before you answer.
