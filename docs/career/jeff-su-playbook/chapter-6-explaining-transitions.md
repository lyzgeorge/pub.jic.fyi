# Chapter 6 — Explaining Transitions

This chapter lets you answer the three questions about breaks in your story (why you are leaving, why you were laid off, and why there is a gap) briefly, honestly, and in a way that moves the conversation to the job in front of you.

## 6.1 What is the interviewer worried about?

A transition question is a risk check: will something in your past limit your performance in the new role? For gaps, Jeff says employers may worry about your reliability, your capabilities, and your commitment, "even if everything else about you looks great on paper." For leaving a job, the interviewer should end convinced that your reason for looking is "not going to be a limiting factor to your success in the new role."

- **Leaving a current job:** will the reason follow you into this role?
- **Layoff:** will emotion cloud your judgment, and how did you actually perform?
- **Gap:** are you reliable, current in your skills, and committed?

Jeff attributes to former FBI negotiator Chris Voss the point that "why" questions "immediately place you on the defensive" (unsourced).

## 6.2 The shared pattern: brief, honest, positive, then forward

All three videos make the same move: a short, truthful, positive account, then most of the answer on why you fit. The explanation removes doubt; the forward part sells you. Dwelling on the old job means "you're losing the opportunity to sell yourself on this new one."

1. **Answer directly.** Interviewers are "generally wise to this sort of non-answer" and will ask again.
2. **Keep it short and prepared**, so you don't rant in the moment: one to two sentences for leaving, about 20% of the answer for a layoff.
3. **Word it positively**, and "allow the interviewer to read between the lines."
4. **Pivot** to the skills you built and the work you enjoy.
5. **Finish with the future:** researched interest in this role and company.

**The reusable future module.** The forward part of every transition answer is your "why do you want to work here" answer (Chapter 4). The layoff video says the future portion "is very simple because you can just use your answer to the why you want to work here interview question", and the gap video and ETP's Potential step ask for the same. Editorial guidance: prepare the "why here" answer once and plug it into all three.

## 6.3 Leaving your current job: ETP

ETP (Explain, Transition, Potential) is a three-part structure for "Why are you leaving your current job?" that works "regardless of the specific situation you're in," keeping you truthful without sounding negative.

| Pillar | How to do it |
|---|---|
| **Explain** | One to two prepared sentences that answer directly. Show you are "dignified, professional, and will not talk negatively about another company or boss"; word negatives carefully. |
| **Transition** | A concrete example of why you are considering leaving, tied to the new job, so the interviewer sees "a slight mismatch between your strengths and what your current role asked of you." The pivot should be hard to notice. |
| **Potential** | "Educated assumptions" from your research, showing the mismatch "should not exist" in the new role. |

**The leverage line.** In his Explain step Jeff also said he was especially interested in this particular role, "giving the impression that if it weren't for the specific role, I might not be looking," which he calls "a little bit of leverage." His answer closed on why he wanted "this role. Not just any role."

**Example.** Jeff built the answer when moving from consulting to sales at Google; the answer was shown on screen only, pivoting from "the negative aspects about consulting" to what he looked forward to. ETP rests on that one move and assumes you are still employed and leaving by choice.

## 6.4 Explaining a layoff: Facts to Future

Facts to Future, which Jeff calls "particularly powerful when explaining a layoff," spends about 20% of the answer on the facts and the remaining 80% on the role you want (his own guideline; unsourced). A brief factual start avoids the three failures in his bad opening ("the reason I was let go didn't really make much sense. I was one of the high performers..."): emotion clouding judgment, bad-mouthing the employer ("a huge red flag"), and selling the old job instead of yourself.

1. **Facts (about 20%).** Address the question head on, briefly.
2. **Pivot** to the skills you have and the tasks you enjoy.
3. **Future (about 80%).** Reuse your "why here" answer, adding three things: skills that are immediately transferable, because "all hiring managers want a candidate who can start doing the job well as soon as possible"; evidence that this is "a calculated decision and not just a desperate attempt to get any job"; and an offer to connect them with your previous manager.

**Example script from the video** (trimmed; a hypothetical layoff and Apple role):

> Sure. As you may have heard, the entire tech sector was affected by layoffs. This time around, leadership decided to de-prioritize the product area I was part of, and high performers across a range of functions from finance to business development were all impacted. Not gonna lie, I was pretty hurt when I first found out. But in the two weeks that followed, I took some time, reflected on the skills I learned, and listed out all the tasks I enjoyed doing in my previous role, and I realized product marketing is an area I'm extremely passionate about... Based on some desktop research and speaking with a few of your team members, I understand that Apple is expanding aggressively in the search ads space. Not only is my B2B marketing experience immediately applicable here, hopefully resulting in a quicker ramp-up, my experience in user acquisition campaigns is also very relevant... And to give you an idea of how I performed in my last role, I'm more than happy to connect you with my previous manager.

The pivot begins at "reflected on the skills I learned." The facts take three sentences and blame a business decision, not the candidate. Jeff presents it as a sample, not his own story.

## 6.5 Explaining an employment gap: reframe to skills

For a gap, reframe the question from "Why weren't you working?" to "What relevant skills did I pick up during this break that apply to the role I'm applying for?" Jeff says gaps "of six months or fewer" and gaps "that occurred a number of years ago usually require no explanation" (unsourced); long or recent ones need "a strong, well-thought-out answer."

Reframing moves you "from being on the defense... to playing offense." Prepare it like a behavioral question, with specific examples (Chapter 5).

**Personal reasons.** Caring for a loved one or yourself was "more relevant than ever in 2020." It builds skills in juggling priorities, making high-impact decisions, and working under stress. In the video's hypothetical, navigating a healthcare system led you to build a deadline-tracking spreadsheet (project management) and research hospitals and doctors (detail-oriented, proactive).

**Professional reasons.** If you were furloughed, downsized, or had an offer rescinded, say you want a long-term prospect, "not just a paycheck," and want to be sure the job is right; then explain why this one suits you, leading into your "why here" answer. An acquaintance of Jeff's, out of work eight months, told a marketing agency he had been downsized and his savings let him choose carefully, praised the agency for cutting pay instead of staff, and described digital marketing courses (Skillshare, Coursera, Udacity) he could apply immediately. He got the offer; the answer "conveyed a sense of strong work ethic."

**Three pointers.** **Be honest:** "a quick reference check will quickly determine the dates of employment." **Don't sound defensive:** acknowledge the concern and refocus; being "composed and unapologetic" reassures. **Optionally, mention it in the cover letter** to look extra prepared. An opener from the video: "I fully understand you need to know what I was up to during that time, and I'd definitely like to discuss that with you."

## 6.6 The three answers compared

| | Leaving a current job (2021) | Layoff (2023) | Employment gap (2020) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Framework** | ETP | Facts to Future | Reframe to skills gained |
| **The past** | One to two sentences | About 20% | Brief; focus on learnings |
| **Pivot** | Concrete example of the mismatch | Skills and tasks you enjoyed | Skills from the break |
| **Forward part** | Researched "educated assumptions" | "Why here," transferable skills, calculated decision, manager reference | Long-term intent, "why here" |
| **Main risk** | Ranting, oversharing, dodging | Emotion, bad-mouthing, dwelling | Defensiveness, misstated dates |
| **Sample answer** | On screen only | Full script | Paraphrased examples |

## 6.7 What not to do

- **Don't bad-mouth** a former company or boss; after a layoff it is "a huge red flag."
- **Don't argue the decision was unfair.** Saying the layoff "didn't really make much sense" shows emotion clouding judgment.
- **Don't dodge.** Skipping the past invites "Wait, so why are you leaving your current job again?"
- **Don't rant, be defensive, misrepresent dates, or sound desperate.** Prepare the line, acknowledge, redirect.

## 6.8 Context and where the videos differ

**Era.** The gap video (October 2020) was recorded during the pandemic and refers to furloughs, rescinded offers, and caregiving "in 2020." The layoff video (February 2023) assumes "the entire tech sector was affected by layoffs." Editorial guidance: the structures don't depend on these events, but phrases like "as you may have heard..." only work when the interviewer shares that context; update them to your market.

**Two frameworks for nearly the same question.** "Why are you leaving your current job?" (ETP, 2021) and "Why did you leave your last job?" (Facts to Future, 2023) agree on a short past and a long future but differ in two ways:

| Point | ETP (2021) | Facts to Future (2023) |
|---|---|---|
| Tone of the past | Keep it positive; let the interviewer read between the lines | A brief admission of feeling is allowed ("I was pretty hurt") |
| Signal about your search | Imply you might not be looking but for this role ("leverage") | Show a calculated decision, not desperation |

Editorial guidance: the difference follows the situation. Someone still employed and choosing to leave can credibly imply selectivity; someone who has already left should show deliberate choice instead of leverage.

**Chapter standard:** A finished transition answer states the facts truthfully in one to three sentences, contains nothing negative about a former employer, and spends most of its length on skills that transfer and on researched reasons for this role. You should be able to say the past portion from memory and plug your "why here" answer in after the pivot.
