# Chapter 7 — Closing the Interview and Following Up

This chapter lets you use the last minutes of an interview and the days after it: asking questions that only the interviewer can answer, collecting the detail and the address you need for a thank-you email, and following up without becoming a nuisance.

## 7.1 Why do your questions matter?

Your answer to "Do you have any questions for me?" is part of the evaluation. Jeff quotes Mark Cuban: "the questions you ask tell... whoever more about you than anything else you do. In particular, [it] tells me about your preparation. The biggest mistake you make is to ask questions you can find the answer to online."

Two rules follow. **Ask only what the interviewer can answer**, never what is on the website or in the news. **Tailor with a detail or two**, so a memorized question stops sounding rehearsed. Good questions also invite "natural follow-ups, making it more of a two-way conversation than a robotic question, answer, question, answer," the same argument Jeff makes for the KPI question you ask insiders (see Chapter 1).

## 7.2 Five questions to ask

| # | Question | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A tailored question about the interviewer | Gets them talking; shows you looked them up. |
| 2 | "If you think back to a recent hire that impressed you, what characteristics did they have? What did they do?" | Forces a specific person, so you get concrete examples. |
| 3 | "Who will I be working with most closely? Which teams will I be working with?" | Tests fit with your long-term goals; invites follow-ups. |
| 4 | "How will my success be measured for the first one or two quarters?" Then: "How do these metrics tie into those of the teams I would work closely with?" | Shows you are data driven and think about implications. |
| 5 | "Do you have any reservations or hesitations about hiring me?" | Surfaces objections while you can respond (7.3). |

**About the interviewer.** "How do you like working here?" sounds memorized; add their title and tenure from LinkedIn, which "takes less than five minutes":

> I see you've been an account manager covering the retail sector for the past three years. Is your experience so far what you expected?

**The recent hire.** Chapter 1 covers this as a research tool. Here it beats "What advice do you have for someone like me?" because it forces specific behavior. Write the answer down and prepare matching experiences for the next round, since the interviewer has "basically just told you the qualities they look for." It also feeds the thank-you email (7.5).

**Who you will work with.** The first reason is openly "selfish," checking fit with your career goals; the second is the follow-up it invites:

> You mentioned the marketing team gives heavy input on the content we share with clients. How is the dynamic between these two teams?

**How success is measured.** "Nothing gets a hiring manager more excited than new hires demonstrating they are data driven and results oriented." The follow-up about neighboring teams also tells you how the teams work together. This is the interview-day version of the measurement mindset in Chapter 1.

## 7.3 Asking about reservations

Jeff calls this the scariest question, but only three kinds of answer come back:

| Answer | How to respond |
|---|---|
| **The "politician answer"** ("Nope, it was great chatting with you... we'll reach out.") | No harm done; asking showed confidence. |
| **A real gap** (a missing hard skill or years of experience) | Don't panic; say you are working on it and highlight transferable skills. "The way you handle their objection matters a lot more than the objection itself." |
| **A misunderstanding** | Correct it with humility. This can "turn a B-plus interview into an A." |

**Example script from the video** (the recording trails off and the company name is unclear):

> Sorry about that, I didn't communicate this very clearly just now, but interestingly, I do have experience with retail export clients. During my time with [company], I was actually responsible for...

## 7.4 The thank-you email: purpose

Top candidates, "sneaky overachievers," plan the email during the interview around one thing the interviewer cares about. Jeff says "studies show that 80% of interviewers find thank-you notes helpful in making hiring decisions, of which 87% prefer email over a phone call, yet only 24% of job seekers remember to send follow-up emails" (no source given; a reason to send the email, not a fact). Specificity is the point: "Really enjoyed your presentation, let's connect" is generic, while naming the idea you liked is specific.

## 7.5 Capture a specific detail during the interview

**Small talk at the start**: on a Monday or Tuesday, ask how their weekend went; later in the week, ask what they have planned. People tend to share something personal. If they skip small talk, **the recent-hire question at the end** ("If you think about a recent hire you enjoyed working with, what characteristics did they have?") "kills two birds with one stone": it shows you are a self-starter and gives you a concrete example to reference. Editorial guidance: note the detail right after the interview, before it fades.

## 7.6 Get the interviewer's email address

**Ask for it** before you leave. Failing that, **ask the recruiter to forward your note**; recruiters "are usually happy to help." Or **use a free email-finder** such as MailScoop or Hunter.io (as of 2022) with the full name: "You lose nothing by trying." Editorial guidance: that last is Jeff's "more aggressive approach"; where you have a recruiter, forwarding is the safer default.

## 7.7 Write the email

Jeff's own email, for the job he held at the time, makes three moves (his notes in brackets):

> Hey Arthur, thanks so much for taking the time to interview me yesterday, especially during a business trip to Singapore. I hope you get some time this weekend to visit those whiskey bars you mentioned. [specific and personal]
> I really enjoyed learning about the SMB business, and in particular how your sales team identifies sustainable businesses to acquire. [something Arthur was proud of]
> I'd like to re-emphasize my interest in this position and believe my get-things-done attitude would be a great fit for the team. [Arthur's team acts quickly on ideas]

For the subject line, "[Your name] – thank you" is safe; Jeff's was "Jeff Su, the candidate with a frozen screen," after his webcam broke. Editorial guidance: go personal only when it refers to something you both experienced.

## 7.8 Pleasant persistence and its limits

"Pleasant persistence" means following up without apology, being "a little shameless." **Send the thank-you within 24 hours**, his rule of thumb amid "a lot of different opinions." **Follow the recruiter's timeline**, following up if a promised week passes in silence. **Stop at three**: "three follow-ups one week apart after that initial thank-you note seems to be the limit before it starts to get a little annoying," a limit from experience, not data. **Stay in the same thread**, citing a different interview detail each time.

## 7.9 Address reservations in writing

Free of nerves and "tunnel vision," you can fix a weak answer or a perceived gap in one or two sentences (figures hypothetical):

> You mentioned the ideal candidate should have content marketing experience. While I mainly worked in product marketing, I'd like to add that I'm also responsible for drafting our quarterly newsletters, which have a 48% open rate, 9% higher than our team average.

Only address a topic you both discussed; don't pitch something new.

**How the two videos connect.** Asking about reservations (7.3) surfaces the concern; the email answers it. The videos do not conflict: both use the recent-hire question, one to learn what the team values, the other as a fallback email detail.

**Chapter standard:** You leave every interview with at least two questions asked that no website could answer, one specific detail about the interviewer written down, and their email address or a recruiter willing to forward your note. Your thank-you email goes out within 24 hours, names that detail, and addresses at most one concern that came up in the conversation.
