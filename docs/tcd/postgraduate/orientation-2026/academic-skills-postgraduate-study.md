# Academic Skills for Successful Postgraduate Study

> **Original title:** Academic Skills PG
>
> **Session date:** 10 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Embed.aspx?id=215e2ba7-4cbb-4f9f-b672-b4b700ec1ee4)

Successful postgraduate study depends on **self-regulated learning**: actively planning, monitoring, and evaluating the cognitive, emotional, and behavioural processes used to learn. The student controls not only study methods, but also motivation, time, environment, and performance monitoring.

## Plan work deliberately

Put fixed commitments into a schedule first, then reserve time for study and genuine downtime. Convert broad intentions into SMART goals: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-based. A practical task list applies three decisions: do very short tasks immediately, schedule worthwhile longer tasks, and discard tasks that are not important.

Priority depends on both importance and urgency. Important and urgent work needs immediate action; important but less urgent work should be planned before it becomes a crisis. Low-importance interruptions should not displace core work, while low-value distractions can be removed.

![A four-quadrant priority matrix comparing importance and urgency](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/academic-skills-postgraduate-study/priority-matrix.jpg)

The matrix makes the central distinction visible: planning protects important work that is not yet urgent. Time blocking can then turn priorities into action. The session suggests the Pomodoro pattern—25 minutes of focused work, a 5-minute break, and a longer break after four cycles—as one optional structure.

## Use active learning rather than passive exposure

There is no single correct way to study. Effective methods require active processing: practice tests or questions, distributed practice over time, elaboration, links to prior knowledge, recognition of patterns, and concrete examples. Discussion, visualisation, teaching another person, asking questions, diagrams, concept maps, and recording an explanation all force information to be retrieved and reorganised.

Start early enough to distribute the work. Match the study place to the task and prepare it in advance; where a permanent desk is unavailable, a portable study kit can reduce setup friction. When beginning feels difficult, five minutes of free writing can expose what is already known, what remains unclear, and what needs investigation.

## Build academic writing around five qualities

Postgraduate academic writing should be formal, clear and cohesive, detached and objective, evidence-based, and critical and analytical.

1. **Formal:** avoid contractions, clichés, and colloquialisms where they weaken precision.
2. **Clear:** make the overall structure and each paragraph concise, coherent, cohesive, and logical.
3. **Detached and objective:** replace emotional intensifiers with precise claims supported by evidence.
4. **Evidence-based:** draw on a range of authors and appropriate disciplinary evidence, then connect sources with explicit signposting and transitions.
5. **Critical and analytical:** question assumptions, evaluate information, identify bias, and show the reasoning that links evidence to a conclusion.

Research begins with knowing where suitable evidence is found—such as disciplinary databases and subject-librarian guidance—and keeping a reliable record of sources from the start. Writers represent other authors through paraphrase, summary, quotation, and synthesis. Reporting verbs should communicate the writer’s relationship to a source rather than treating every claim as equally certain.

Referencing credits ideas, avoids plagiarism, supports arguments, demonstrates breadth of reading, and lets readers locate the original material. It is therefore part of the reasoning, not merely a formatting step.

## Move from description to evaluation

Critical thinking becomes visible when questions progress beyond “what happened?” The Plymouth model moves through description, analysis, and evaluation: establish what, where, who, and when; ask why, how, and what alternatives are possible; then identify significance, implications, conclusions, and next actions.

![The Plymouth critical-thinking model, progressing from descriptive questions to analysis and evaluation](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/academic-skills-postgraduate-study/plymouth-critical-thinking-model.jpg)

The model’s key lesson is to make reasoning explicit. Every substantial claim should answer “so what?” by explaining why it matters, how the parts relate, and what follows from the evidence.

## A compact working routine

- Plan fixed commitments, study blocks, and rest.
- Prioritise important work before urgency takes over.
- Use retrieval, spacing, explanation, and examples to learn actively.
- Begin writing early, even with a short exploratory draft.
- Build claims from appropriate evidence and record sources as you research.
- Revise for formal tone, clarity, objectivity, cohesion, and explicit reasoning.
- End each important claim by checking its implication: **so what?**
