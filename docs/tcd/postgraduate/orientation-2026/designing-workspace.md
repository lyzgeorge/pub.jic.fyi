# Designing a Workspace That Supports Study

> **Original title:** Setting up your Workspace
>
> **Session date:** 8 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=e5e521da-35c6-4767-a914-b4ba00b1dc44)

A workspace is not simply a desk. It combines physical setup, sensory conditions, digital organisation, social context, accessibility, and the task being performed. A space that permits short-term work may still cause fatigue, pain, distraction, or avoidance when used for sustained study.

## Match the space to the task

Different tasks can need different levels of noise, movement, light, privacy, collaboration, and stimulation. Idea generation may work in an active café or shared space, while close reading, analysis, or writing may require a quiet room or acoustic pod. Group discussion, online meetings, laboratory work, teaching, fieldwork, and recovery breaks each create distinct requirements.

Do not assume the assigned office, home desk, or nearest library seat must serve every purpose. A productive day may intentionally move between environments.

## Identify sensory preferences

Everyone has sensory preferences, whether or not they identify as disabled or neurodivergent. Relevant dimensions include:

- brightness, glare, daylight, and seasonal change;
- steady noise versus unpredictable voices or equipment;
- visual movement and foot traffic;
- temperature and ventilation;
- proximity, privacy, and enclosure;
- sitting, standing, movement, and posture;
- textures, smells, and laboratory inputs.

Observe actual effects rather than following generic “ideal workspace” advice. Note where concentration starts easily, what produces fatigue, and which conditions suit each type of work. Experiment and review.

## Physical, virtual, and social environments

**Physical setup** includes chair, desk, screen, input devices, posture, reach, lighting, acoustics, and accessibility. Ergonomic assessment is most useful when it considers duration and the person, not only whether the furniture looks correct.

**Virtual setup** includes file structure, backups, notifications, calendars, communication channels, software accessibility, and the separation of work from personal accounts or time.

**Social setup** concerns who is present, whether interaction is expected, and how connection or interruption affects the work. Some students benefit from quiet co-working; others need solitude for focus and planned social spaces for breaks.

## Use the range of Trinity spaces

The TCD Sense Map describes spaces by characteristics and facilities, not merely location. It can help identify social areas, quiet and respite rooms, accessible routes, power, food or microwave access, and spaces at several Trinity sites. Most listed general spaces are available across disciplines, although some have restrictions.

Libraries provide varied environments, including open study, individual focus areas, acoustic pods, adjustable conditions, sit–stand desks, and bookable rooms. Booking a preferred space around a recurring weekly gap can turn workspace choice into a reliable routine.

## Laboratories and fixed environments

Laboratories can combine equipment noise, alarms, light, smells, movement, protective clothing, and limited control. If informal adjustments are insufficient, identify the precise barrier and discuss scheduling, equipment position, protective options, breaks, or reasonable accommodation with the relevant staff. “Pushing through” a persistent access problem is not the only option.

## Seek tailored support

Disability Service occupational therapists can discuss environment and routine even with students who do not identify as disabled. College Health physiotherapy and ergonomic guidance may help with pain or physical setup. Formal reasonable accommodations can address environmental barriers where needed.

## Workspace experiment

1. Name the task and required duration.
2. Rate current light, noise, movement, comfort, privacy, and access.
3. Change one condition or choose another space.
4. Work for a defined interval and record concentration, output, pain, and fatigue.
5. Keep arrangements that help and reject generic advice that does not.
6. Plan recurring bookings and transitions across the week.
7. Seek occupational, ergonomic, or accessibility support for persistent barriers.
