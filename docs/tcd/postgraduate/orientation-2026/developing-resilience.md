# Developing Resilience: Stress, Coping, and Self-Compassion

> **Original title:** Developing Resilience
>
> **Session date:** 9 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=c7d8061d-bd84-4a23-b28a-b4b700eb9aa2)

Resilience is not the absence of distress. It is the capacity to notice stress responses, remain connected with needs and values, recover flexibility, and use support when self-management is not enough. Psychological wellness includes meaningful work, close relationships, play, and the ability to tolerate emotional pain—not constant happiness.

## Why distress can narrow capacity

Difficulties intensify when important needs such as safety, acceptance, or connection are unmet, or when demands exceed available resources. Under threat, the nervous system can move above or below its workable range. Hyperarousal may involve agitation, emotional flooding, and unclear thinking; hypoarousal may involve numbness, lethargy, withdrawal, or shutting down.

![Window-of-tolerance diagram showing hyperarousal above the workable range and hypoarousal below it](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/developing-resilience/window-of-tolerance.jpg)

The **window of tolerance** is the range in which a person can stay mentally engaged and draw on both thought and emotion. Its width changes with circumstances. Sleep, nutrition, breaks, exercise, mindful practice, and connection with others can widen the range over time. In the moment, grounding, breathing, movement, or contact with a trusted person may help restore regulation.

## Make room for emotion before solving the problem

Emotional pain can carry information about needs, boundaries, loss, or threat. A useful first response is to pause and name the feeling accurately. Making space for an unpleasant feeling is different from approving of a situation or giving up on change; it reduces the additional struggle created by denial or urgent attempts to eliminate the feeling. Mindfulness supports this by training present-moment, non-judgmental awareness.

## Treat thoughts as mental events

Stress can amplify catastrophizing and emotional reasoning. When fused with a thought, a person treats it as reality and has difficulty taking in contrary information. **Thought diffusion** creates enough distance to choose a response rather than acting automatically.

![STOP thought-diffusion sequence: Step back, Take a breath, Observe, and Pull back for perspective](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/developing-resilience/thought-diffusion.jpg)

Use the STOP sequence:

1. **Step back:** pause rather than acting immediately.
2. **Take a breath:** interrupt automatic momentum.
3. **Observe:** identify thoughts and feelings; ask whether the thought is accurate and whether it is helpful.
4. **Pull back:** place the event in a wider perspective.

The aim is not forced positive thinking. It is to distinguish a thought from a fact and recover access to other information.

## Build recovery into ordinary life

Academic work should not consume every source of restoration. A sustainable pattern includes sleep and rest, social connection, movement and food, hobbies, and deliberate relaxation as well as work.

![Example life-balance pie distributing time across academic work, sleep, relationships, movement, recreation, and relaxation](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/developing-resilience/life-balance-pie.jpg)

The percentages in the example are illustrative, not a universal prescription. The practical question is whether the current pattern repeatedly removes the activities that refuel attention and coping capacity. Small routines that survive busy weeks are more protective than an ideal plan used only when life is calm.

## Communicate needs without escalating conflict

Assertiveness combines clear facts, an honest account of impact, and a constructive request. It differs from passivity, which hides the need, and aggression, which attacks the other person.

![Three-part assertive communication framework: say what you see, say what you feel, and say what you need](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/developing-resilience/assertive-communication.jpg)

A concise structure is:

1. **Say what you see:** describe observable facts without accusation.
2. **Say what you feel:** explain the personal impact directly.
3. **Say what you need:** make a specific, workable request.

For example, when offered extra duties, identify the request, explain the concern about core deliverables, and ask to review workload.

## Soften the inner critic

Severe self-criticism can deepen isolation and threat. Arguing point by point is not always effective because the underlying problem may be the hostile way the mind is treating the self. Personifying the inner critic can make its tone and cost easier to observe.

![Three steps for softening the inner critic: notice exact self-talk, respond with compassion, and reframe observations constructively](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/developing-resilience/softening-inner-critic.jpg)

A three-step practice is to notice the exact words of self-criticism, deliberately replace judgment with compassion, and restate any useful observation in a friendly and constructive way. Another test is to compare the response with what one would say to a close friend in the same situation. Self-compassion does not remove accountability; it makes learning from setbacks possible without adding abuse.

## A practical resilience check

- What state am I in: within my workable range, activated above it, or shut down below it?
- What feeling or need requires acknowledgement?
- Am I treating a thought as a fact?
- Which small action—breathing, movement, food, rest, connection, perspective, or a boundary—could restore flexibility?
- Is my inner voice helping me learn, or only punishing me?
- Do I need professional, academic, medical, or emergency support rather than another self-help technique?

Resilience practices complement rather than replace care. Trinity Student Counselling, College Health, Disability Service, chaplaincy, academic tutors, and out-of-hours or emergency services serve different needs; seek help early and use urgent support where safety is at risk.
