# Faith, Spirituality, and Community at Trinity

> **Original title:** Let's Talk About... Faith and Spirituality
>
> **Session date:** 8 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=a4033087-5a71-4b0e-9389-b4ba00b17e60)

Faith and spirituality at Trinity are supported through chaplaincy, dedicated spaces, and student-led societies. Students may use these routes for worship, reflection, community, questions about belief, or conversation during a difficult period, whether they hold a settled faith, are exploring, or have no religious affiliation.

## Chaplaincy as a point of connection

Trinity’s chaplaincy includes representatives of several Christian traditions, including Roman Catholic, Church of Ireland/Anglican, Methodist, and Presbyterian ministry. Its role is not limited to members of those churches: chaplains can help students locate other faith communities and can provide a confidential setting for spiritual or personal conversation.

House 27 is presented as the main point of contact and includes a common room during term. Regular activities mentioned in the recording included a Tuesday soup lunch, but schedules should be checked each year.

## Worship and reflection spaces

Campus provision described in the session includes the Chapel for Christian services, a Christian prayer or quiet space, a Muslim prayer room in Goldsmith Hall, and a multifaith room accessible with chaplaincy guidance. Availability and access arrangements may change, so students should contact the relevant society or chaplaincy before relying on a space.

A multifaith approach does not require all traditions to practise in the same way. The practical aim is to provide suitable spaces and routes to communities while respecting different forms of worship and reflection.

## Finding a student society

The Trinity societies directory groups spiritual societies and provides descriptions and contact details. The session specifically mentions an active Muslim Students Association and Jewish Society, alongside Christian Union and the Laurentian Catholic Society. Freshers’ Fair and societies listings are useful when meeting times have not yet been published.

Student societies differ from formal chaplaincy. They are peer-led communities that may organise worship, discussion, study, social events, cultural meals, sport, retreats, charity, and public conversations. Joining usually happens through the societies system, while current event details may also appear on society pages or social channels.

## Examples of Christian student communities

The Christian Union describes itself as open to Christians from different backgrounds and to people curious about Christianity. Activities mentioned include weekly talks, small-group Bible study, prayer, questions about faith and student life, international events, and public discussion.

The Laurentian Society supports Catholic community through prayer, Mass, reading groups, retreats, social time, and links with Catholic chaplaincy. Its speakers likewise welcomed people who are exploring or simply wish to attend an event.

These examples describe two societies rather than the whole faith landscape. Students seeking another tradition should use the societies directory or ask chaplaincy for an introduction.

## Participation principles

- Attend as a learner without pretending commitment you do not hold.
- Ask organisers what an event involves if expectations are unclear.
- Respect prayer, dietary, dress, gender, calendar, and sacred-space practices.
- Do not assume one society or representative speaks for every person in a tradition.
- Seek private conversation when a question concerns personal belief, conflict, or wellbeing.
- Check current locations and times before attending.

Faith communities can offer belonging across age, nationality, programme, and study level. Participation is voluntary, and students should be free both to practise religion and not to practise it.
