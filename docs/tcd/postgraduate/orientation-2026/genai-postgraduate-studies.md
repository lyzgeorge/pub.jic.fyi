# Generative AI in Postgraduate Study

> **Original title:** GenAI
>
> **Session date:** 8 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=42634894-a8dd-4b5a-93e2-b4ba00ae52fe)

Generative AI can support selected learning tasks, but it cannot assume a student’s academic responsibility. Appropriate use depends on the specific course, assessment, research context, data involved, and acknowledgement requirements.

## Permission is contextual

The session states that Trinity permits generative-AI use unless a course handbook or assessment says otherwise, with acknowledgement required. That general position does not override local instructions. Before using a tool for a thesis, paper, assignment, coding task, data analysis, or research process, check the current School handbook and the instructions for that work.

Acknowledgement should make the extent and purpose of use visible. It does not convert prohibited assistance into permitted assistance, and it does not remove responsibility for the submitted result.

## Never disclose protected material

Prompts are disclosures to an external system. The session prohibits entering private student or staff information and confidential Trinity, teaching, administrative, study, or research content that is not public or for which the user lacks rights. This includes unpublished participant data, identifiable records, confidential drafts, restricted documents, and third-party copyrighted material.

Privacy, GDPR, research ethics, confidentiality agreements, and intellectual-property obligations still apply. Removing a name may not sufficiently anonymise a distinctive case or dataset.

## Understand the risks

Academic risks include fabricated facts and citations, no reliable access to paywalled literature, generic prose, loss of originality, and academic-misconduct consequences. Overreliance can prevent development of critical thinking, synthesis, expression, source evaluation, and the ability to form an independent position.

Wider concerns include disinformation, energy use, appropriation of human-created work, exploitative data acquisition, bias and discrimination, unequal access, and weak cybersecurity safeguards. These are reasons to make a deliberate decision about whether AI belongs in a task—not merely reasons to write a better prompt.

## Decide what to delegate

A task may involve defining the question, finding evidence, understanding concepts, reasoning, generating possibilities, judging quality, writing, and taking responsibility. Delegating the parts that constitute the intended learning can defeat the exercise even when AI use is technically allowed.

Potential uses mentioned in the session include explaining terminology, creating a starting mind map, identifying candidate themes, testing understanding, prioritising tasks, revising syntax, or reviewing a draft. Results vary, especially for summaries, key-point extraction, theory explanations, and factual validation.

![Slide listing possible GenAI strengths—summarising, key-point extraction, explanation, task initiation, language revision, and mind mapping—with a warning that results vary](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/genai-postgraduate-studies/strengths-opportunities.jpg)

Every output should be checked against primary or authoritative sources. A generated citation is only a search lead until the cited work is found, read, and verified.

## Two complementary frameworks

![ROCKS prompting framework alongside the complete 4D AI-fluency framework: Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/genai-postgraduate-studies/rocks-4d-framework.jpg)

**ROCKS** structures a prompt:

- **Role:** identify the role or perspective requested;
- **Objective:** state the purpose;
- **Community:** specify the audience;
- **Key:** define tone, style, and constraints;
- **Shape:** state the desired output format.

The **4D framework** governs the larger decision:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Delegation | What should remain the student’s work, and what—if anything—can be delegated? |
| Description | Have the goal and constraints been expressed clearly enough to elicit useful behaviour? |
| Discernment | Can the output’s accuracy, relevance, bias, and quality be judged in context? |
| Diligence | Has the user verified, acknowledged, protected data, and accepted responsibility? |

A precise prompt improves usability, but only discernment and diligence address whether the output should be trusted or used.

## Preserve the skills the degree is meant to build

Reading, note-taking, critical thinking, academic writing, structure, and revision are not obstacles to be automated away: they are learning outcomes. AI is most defensible when it helps practise or support these capabilities rather than replacing them. Student Learning Development resources can support the underlying skills directly.

## Responsible-use checklist

1. Read the current assessment and School rules.
2. Decide whether AI use would bypass the intended learning.
3. Exclude personal, confidential, restricted, and unlicensed material.
4. State a clear purpose, audience, constraints, and output form.
5. Verify every material claim and citation independently.
6. Rewrite from understanding rather than laundering generated prose.
7. Acknowledge use accurately and retain a record when required.
8. Accept responsibility for the final work.
