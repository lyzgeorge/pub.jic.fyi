# Health and Wellbeing During Postgraduate Study

> **Original title:** The Healthy Student PG
>
> **Session date:** 8 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=38f1da4a-8a70-4a18-890e-b4b700ea89ed)

Health is physical, mental, and social wellbeing, not merely the absence of illness. Individual choices matter, but they occur within housing, income, work, education, community, healthcare, culture, and the physical environment. A useful wellbeing plan therefore combines manageable habits with use of services and changes to conditions.

![Dahlgren–Whitehead model showing health as shaped by individual factors, lifestyle, social networks, living and working conditions, and wider society](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/healthy-student/determinants-of-health.jpg)

## Avoid turning health into another performance target

Postgraduate workloads can make food, movement, sleep, friendship, and medical care feel secondary. Perfectionist health plans often fail under deadline pressure. Prefer minimum viable routines that survive difficult weeks: a few reliable meals, regular sleep and waking cues, movement built into travel, planned contact with others, and early help when symptoms persist.

The language of what a student “should” do can obscure cost, disability, culture, caring duties, housing, or available time. Adapt recommendations to circumstances and seek professional advice for individual medical needs.

## Food and routine

Eating regularly supports concentration and mood. Planning several affordable meals, keeping basic ingredients available, cooking extra portions, and learning a small set of flexible recipes reduces reliance on expensive impulse purchases. Cooking with others can combine food preparation and social connection.

A balanced pattern is more useful than labelling individual foods as morally good or bad. Students with allergies, medical diets, eating difficulties, sensory needs, or financial constraints should use appropriate professional and practical support.

## Movement that fits ordinary life

Physical activity does not require identifying as “sporty.” Walking or cycling for transport, taking movement breaks, joining a beginner activity, using stairs where appropriate, or scheduling short sessions can be more sustainable than an ambitious programme abandoned after one week.

Recommendations vary by age, health, disability, pregnancy, and intensity. Use current public-health guidance and adapt safely. Trinity Sport, clubs, active travel, and bike schemes offer possible routes, but the best activity is one that is accessible and repeatable.

## Mental health is both daily practice and clinical care

![Healthy Trinity mental-health resource categories including activity, study management, mindfulness, food and mood, sleep, connection, and online support](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/healthy-student/mental-health-supports.jpg)

Daily supports can include realistic study planning, sleep, movement, nourishment, time outside, mindfulness, connection, and recovery from work. These practices do not replace care when someone is distressed, unsafe, unable to function, or experiencing persistent symptoms.

College Health, Student Counselling, Disability Service, academic support, and emergency or external services have different roles. Seek help early and use urgent services where immediate safety is at risk. A student does not need to diagnose themselves before asking what support is appropriate.

## Social support protects wellbeing

Friendship, peer contact, clubs, societies, classmates, research groups, family, faith groups, and professional support can buffer stress. Building connection is easier before a crisis: attend recurring activities, arrange regular check-ins, and let at least one person know when the workload or mood is deteriorating.

Connection should fit the individual. A quiet shared study session may be more restorative than a large social event. International and commuting students may need deliberate routines because spontaneous campus contact is less available.

## Tobacco, vaping, alcohol, and other substances

The session identifies Trinity as a tobacco-free campus and includes vaping within campus restrictions. Students who want to stop smoking can use current cessation support. Alcohol or other substance use should be considered in relation to sleep, medication, safety, consent, spending, and mental health rather than treated as separate from wellbeing.

## Sexual and reproductive health

College Health provides healthcare and can advise on sexual health, contraception, testing, and referrals. The recording points to Ireland’s free contraception scheme for eligible ages; eligibility changes and should be checked. Consent, confidentiality, infection prevention, reproductive choice, and inclusive care all form part of sexual wellbeing.

## Personal wellbeing plan

1. Identify the conditions—not only behaviours—that affect your health.
2. Choose food, sleep, movement, and study routines that remain possible during busy weeks.
3. Schedule recurring social contact and recovery time.
4. Register with or locate health services before urgently needing them.
5. Adjust for disability, medication, culture, finances, and caring responsibilities.
6. Seek help when self-management is insufficient.
7. Use emergency support immediately when safety is at risk.
