# Managing Conflict in Shared Housing

> **Original title:** Let's Talk About... Managing Conflict in Shared Housing
>
> **Session date:** 7 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=71c020a7-9aae-490d-9cce-b4ba00ad2ded)

Successful shared housing depends less on flatmates having identical habits than on making expectations explicit. The session’s two recurring principles are **respect and communication**: discuss needs early, divide responsibilities fairly, and treat disagreements as shared problems rather than contests.

## Establish the basics early

A workable household starts with predictable conduct:

- clean up after yourself and do not take another person’s food or belongings without permission;
- keep noise down, particularly during agreed quiet hours;
- contribute to shared essentials and divide unpleasant jobs such as bins and fridge cleaning;
- tell flatmates before inviting guests into shared space;
- keep personal belongings from taking over communal areas;
- respect bedrooms as private space and carry your own key;
- check in when someone appears unwell or has not been seen for an unusual period.

Flatmates do not need to become close friends. House traditions or occasional shared meals can build trust, but attempts to force closeness can violate another person’s need for privacy.

## Boundaries and the house contract

A **house contract** is a written record of the arrangements everyone has discussed and accepted. It is not presented as a legal document; its purpose is to make expectations visible and provide a neutral reference when memories or assumptions differ.

![House-contract slide listing decisions about cleaning, dishes, shared purchases, quiet hours, food, and belongings](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/managing-conflict-shared-housing/house-contract.jpg)

The key insight is to define operational details rather than rely on vague agreement.

| Area | Questions to settle |
| --- | --- |
| Cleaning | Who cleans each area, how often, and what counts as “clean”? |
| Dishes | How long may dishes remain before washing and putting away? |
| Shared supplies | Which items are communal, who buys them, and is there a shared fund or rota? |
| Food and belongings | What may be shared, and what always requires permission? |
| Quiet hours | At what times should residents use headphones and reduce noise? |
| Guests | How much notice is required, how many guests are reasonable, and may anyone stay overnight? |
| Shared space | How will everyone receive fair access to the kitchen, living room, and storage? |
| Money | Who pays rent and each bill, on what dates, by which method, and how are repayments tracked? |

Expectations should be realistic and revisable. A rule that seemed acceptable before living together may prove impractical, so the contract should be treated as a living agreement.

## Prevent recurring sources of conflict

Common problems include different standards of cleanliness, food being taken, incompatible sleeping or study patterns, noise, smoking or substance use, unequal use of communal space, money, and a partner effectively becoming an additional resident.

Many disputes arise from unspoken assumptions. Communicating a need—such as quiet after 22:00—gives others something concrete to act on. Asking before a party is also different from apologising afterwards: advance notice allows a flatmate with an exam or other need to object or negotiate.

Financial arrangements deserve particular precision in private accommodation. Residents may pay a landlord separately, appoint a reliable lead tenant, or assign different bills to different people. Whatever the arrangement, record due dates and ensure the person whose account is charged receives everyone’s contribution in time.

## Approach a problem constructively

Not every irritation requires a formal meeting, but persistent or important issues should not be left to accumulate. Prepare for the conversation rather than confronting someone at the moment of greatest anger.

![Slide explaining how to approach a problem with flatmates calmly and without accusations](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/managing-conflict-shared-housing/approaching-problems.jpg)

A useful sequence is:

1. Decide whether the issue materially affects you or the household.
2. Wait until you are calm enough to discuss it without attacking.
3. Describe your own experience with “I feel” or “I think” statements.
4. Listen for information that may change your understanding.
5. Frame the discussion as **everyone versus the problem**.
6. Refer to the house contract and renegotiate it if the existing rule does not work.

“I feel that the bins are not being taken out as often as we agreed, and I think we should change the rota” identifies an observable problem and invites a solution. “You never take the bins out” assigns blame and makes defensiveness more likely.

Avoid passive-aggressive notes or messages, long-term silence, alliances of “me versus them,” and attempts to win at another resident’s expense. At the same time, cooperation does not mean ignoring needs that genuinely matter to you.

## Five conflict-resolution styles

The session presents five styles in an order from generally preferable to least useful for an ongoing flatmate relationship.

![Slide listing collaborating, compromising, avoidance, accommodating, and competing as conflict-resolution styles](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/managing-conflict-shared-housing/conflict-resolution-styles.jpg)

| Style | Appropriate use and limitation |
| --- | --- |
| **Collaborating** | Everyone develops a solution that meets the important needs of all parties; this is the preferred “us versus the problem” model. |
| **Compromising** | Each person gives something up, but the result is workable and perceived as fair when no complete win–win exists. |
| **Avoidance** | A short delay can provide time to calm down or manage a stressful week; indefinite avoidance allows resentment and the problem to grow. |
| **Accommodating** | Sensible for a minor preference you do not care about, but harmful when it repeatedly suppresses important needs. |
| **Competing** | Produces a winner and loser. Even if it settles one dispute, it damages a relationship between people who must continue living together. |

The choice of style can vary by issue. The practical test is whether the response solves the present problem while preserving a tolerable household relationship.

## Everyday sustainability and support

Switching off lights, unplugging unused appliances, and putting devices to sleep reduces electricity costs as well as energy use. These practices can be included in the same shared agreement as other household responsibilities.

If a situation goes beyond ordinary differences—particularly when behaviour is unsafe, severely unreasonable, or cannot be managed through a house discussion—the session recommends contacting the Students’ Union welfare officer for advice and support.

## Practical checklist

1. Hold an initial house meeting before habits harden.
2. Write specific rules for cleaning, guests, food, shared purchases, quiet hours, and bills.
3. Say no clearly when a request genuinely does not work for you.
4. Give notice and ask before changing how communal space will be used.
5. Raise important problems calmly, using observable facts and “I” statements.
6. Listen, collaborate where possible, and compromise fairly where necessary.
7. Revisit the agreement as circumstances change.
8. Seek welfare support when a dispute exceeds normal flatmate conflict.
