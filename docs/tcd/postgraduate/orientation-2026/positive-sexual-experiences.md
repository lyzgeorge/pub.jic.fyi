# Positive Sexual Experiences, Consent, and Bystander Action

> **Original title:** Let's Talk About... Positive Sexual Experiences (Monday)
>
> **Session date:** 7 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=249ee796-589f-4669-b057-b4ba00aa87c9)

A positive sexual experience is one in which everyone can make a free choice, communicate boundaries, remain comfortable, and pursue connection or pleasure without pressure. Consent is therefore not only a safeguard against harm: ongoing communication can also improve trust, intimacy, and enjoyment.

## What makes an experience positive

The session identifies several connected conditions:

- **Consent:** everyone chooses to take part.
- **Comfort and boundaries:** each person can state limits and have them received respectfully.
- **Ongoing communication:** participants keep checking that they remain on the same page.
- **Vulnerability and trust:** intimacy may involve emotional and bodily vulnerability, so safety and respect matter.
- **Individuality:** bodies, preferences, and responses differ; what worked for one person or on one occasion cannot be assumed for another.

Checking in can be direct and natural: “Do you like this?”, “How does this feel?”, “Would you prefer something different?”, or “Do you want to keep going?” A previous yes, a relationship, a date, or an expectation about what might happen later does not remove anyone’s right to change their mind.

## Consent: ongoing, mutual, and freely given

The Active Consent framework uses **OMFG**: consent is **Ongoing, Mutual, and Freely Given**.

![Slide defining consent as ongoing, mutual, and freely given](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/positive-sexual-experiences/consent-omfg.jpg)

The framework’s central insight is that consent is a continuing process rather than a one-time question.

| Principle | Meaning in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Ongoing | Check in from beginning to end; consent can change at any time and applies to each activity. |
| Mutual | Everyone understands what is happening, wants to participate, and communicates verbally or nonverbally. |
| Freely given | The choice is made without pressure, force, manipulation, control, or an inability to decide. |

Consent applies across genders, orientations, relationships, in-person encounters, and online interactions. A “no” should be accepted without treating it as a personal insult or trying to negotiate around it.

### Freedom and capacity

**Freedom to consent** means that saying yes or no is genuinely possible. Repeated pressure, threats, manipulation, exploiting a position of power, or making someone feel that refusal is not an option removes that freedom.

**Capacity to consent** means being mentally and physically able to understand the situation and make and communicate a decision. A person who is asleep or unconscious cannot consent. Alcohol, drugs, and medication can affect capacity, but the session rejects a universal drink-count rule: people have different tolerances, and equal consumption does not imply equal capacity. Check whether each person understands what is happening, can communicate clearly, and is actively choosing to take part. If there is doubt, stop and wait.

In Ireland, the session states that the age of consent for sexual activity is **17**.

## Consent and intimate images

Online conduct follows the same principles. Consent to create or privately share an intimate image is not consent for anyone to redistribute it. The session describes sharing, or threatening to share, intimate images beyond the intended recipient as image-based sexual abuse and a crime in Ireland.

Treat private material as private unless the person concerned explicitly agrees otherwise. Receiving an image never creates permission to forward, publish, or show it to others.

## Bystander intervention: the five Ds

Bystander intervention is action intended to reduce harm or support someone when behaviour appears unsafe or inappropriate. The five-D model offers alternatives so intervention does not depend on confrontation.

![Slide listing the five Ds of bystander intervention: Direct, Distract, Delegate, Delay, and Document](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/positive-sexual-experiences/bystander-5ds.jpg)

| Approach | Example or purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Direct** | Address the behaviour when doing so is appropriate and safe. |
| **Distract** | Interrupt the situation with an unrelated question or activity. |
| **Delegate** | Ask a friend, staff member, security worker, or another suitable person to help. |
| **Delay** | Check on the affected person afterwards when immediate intervention was unsafe or impossible. |
| **Document** | Record relevant details that may later help the person seek support or report. |

Safety comes first. Freezing or deciding not to intervene immediately does not prevent later support. Documentation also requires care: filming a person experiencing harm in public can compound that harm, especially when recording replaces active assistance. Note useful information where possible and centre the affected person’s wishes.

## Recognising stalking

The session uses **FOUR** to describe stalking: **Fixated, Obsessive, Unwanted, and Repeated** behaviour.

![Slide defining stalking as fixated, obsessed, unwanted, and repeated behaviour](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/positive-sexual-experiences/stalking-four.jpg)

The key distinction is a pattern rather than a single welcome interaction: one person maintains repeated, unwanted attention toward another, online or in person. The recording states that stalking is a standalone crime in Ireland. Anyone concerned about their own behaviour or another person’s behaviour can seek advice rather than waiting for the pattern to escalate.

## Trinity programmes and support

### Dignity, Respect & Consent Service

Trinity’s **Dignity, Respect & Consent (DR&C) Service** provides information and support concerning bullying, harassment, and sexual misconduct. The session uses sexual misconduct as an umbrella covering sexual harassment, sexual violence, image-based sexual abuse, and stalking. A student who has experienced behaviour, suspects that it may be happening, or simply has questions can book an appointment with a support adviser to discuss support and reporting options.

The recording gives the following contact points:

- Email: `drc@tcd.ie`
- Office: Ground Floor, House 48/51
- Website: [Trinity Dignity and Respect](https://www.tcd.ie/hr/dignity-and-respect/)

The service can also direct students to other internal and external supports. The session mentions national resources including 24-hour helplines, sexual-assault services, internet-safety support, and LGBT support.

### Uphold and further training

**Uphold** is a paid, student-led ambassador programme working with DR&C. It is designed to keep student voices central when the university develops workshops, events, campaigns, training, and charity initiatives about healthy relationships and positive sexual experiences.

The session also points students to DR&C online training, including a multi-module bystander-intervention course on Blackboard, and to `@uphold.tcd` for student events and campaigns.

## Practical principles

1. Ask rather than assume.
2. Keep checking in; consent can change.
3. Make room for a genuine no without punishment or pressure.
4. Treat each person’s body, preferences, and alcohol or drug response as individual.
5. Stop when capacity or willingness is unclear.
6. Respect the original scope of permission for intimate images.
7. Choose the safest useful bystander action, including seeking help or checking in later.
8. Use support services for questions as well as incidents.
