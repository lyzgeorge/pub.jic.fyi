# Building an Effective Student–Supervisor Relationship

> **Original title:** The Student-Supervisor Relationship
>
> **Session date:** 8 September 2026
>
> **Source:** [Panopto recording](https://tcd.cloud.panopto.eu/Panopto/Pages/Viewer.aspx?id=4b85bb5c-762f-4311-ba78-b4b700ea0a08)

Research supervision is a professional relationship with an unavoidable power difference, but students are not passive recipients of direction. The supervisor guides research quality and process; the student owns the project, prepares the work, and actively manages communication.

## Guidance rather than companionship

A supervisor is best understood as a guide or expert consultant. They may not know the student’s exact topic as deeply as the student eventually will, but they contribute experience in research design, disciplinary standards, argument, structure, and academic development. The relationship is academic and professional rather than pastoral or personal, although a supervisor can signpost support.

![Slide listing Trinity’s expectations of supervisors: guidance, quality advice, communication, meetings, continuity, feedback, structure, writing guidance, and final reading](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/student-supervisor-relationship/supervisor-responsibilities.jpg)

Expected supervisory contributions include regular communication and meetings, constructive feedback, guidance on quality, structure, and writing, a final reading within the defined process, and continuity arrangements during sabbatical or absence.

The student remains responsible for submitted work, agreed training, draft production, ethical and regulatory compliance, regular contact, preparation, time management, and seeking help early. A supervisor is not the project manager or routine proof-reader.

## Make expectations explicit

Supervision quality and style vary. Early in the relationship, discuss:

- research interests and relevant supervisory experience;
- how often meetings will occur and how long feedback normally takes;
- what kind of material the supervisor expects before a meeting;
- whether feedback will focus on ideas, methods, structure, writing, or all four;
- which decisions the student can make independently;
- how urgent issues and absences will be handled;
- what role a co-supervisor, thesis committee, or School contact will play.

The goal is not to find one universally “ideal” style but to understand the actual working arrangement and adapt it where possible.

## Direct the feedback you need

A vague request—“please give feedback on my chapter”—forces the supervisor to guess the draft’s maturity and the student’s priorities.

![Slide explaining how a student can direct feedback by identifying draft stage, strengths, weaknesses, uncertainties, and next steps](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/student-supervisor-relationship/directing-feedback.jpg)

When sending work, state:

1. whether it is exploratory, rough, revised, or near-final;
2. what you think already works;
3. what you know needs development;
4. the specific questions you cannot resolve;
5. the decision or next step for which you need advice.

Do not automatically expect line-by-line spelling, grammar, or stylistic correction. If language-level review is needed, ask whether that is within the agreed supervisory role and use other writing support where appropriate.

Receiving criticism can feel personal because research is closely tied to effort and identity. Treat feedback as information about the work, clarify ambiguous comments, and allow time before responding defensively. Self-compassion means recognising that revision is normal academic work, not evidence of personal failure.

## Run purposeful meetings

![Complete empowered-meetings checklist: agree an agenda, schedule the next meeting, send a written recap with actions and timelines, and invite corrections](/assets/tcd/postgraduate/orientation-2026/student-supervisor-relationship/meeting-checklist.jpg)

Before a meeting, send a short agenda and invite additions. Bring written material, decisions, evidence, and focused questions. During the meeting, clarify unfamiliar advice, respectfully explain disagreement, and ask whether any important concern remains unstated.

Before leaving, schedule the next meeting. Afterwards, send a concise recap recording issues discussed, actions assigned, deadlines, and open questions; invite corrections. This creates a shared record without turning the relationship into an adversarial audit.

## Communicate assertively and professionally

Because it is the student’s degree, respectful questioning is legitimate. Useful phrases include:

- “I want to make sure I understand—could you repeat the point about…?”
- “Could you explain what you mean by…?”
- “I am not sure I agree; may I explain my reasoning?”
- “Is there anything else I need to know about my progress at this stage?”
- “Can we schedule the next meeting now?”

Assertiveness states a need or disagreement clearly while respecting the other person. It differs from either passive acceptance or hostility.

## Respond when supervision is not working

First identify the specific failure: unavailable meetings, unclear feedback, conflicting expectations, delayed review, interpersonal difficulty, subject mismatch, or a more serious boundary or conduct concern. Where safe, raise the concrete issue with the supervisor and propose an operational change.

Keep relevant written records and seek support early. Depending on the issue, routes may include a co-supervisor, thesis committee, postgraduate coordinator, Director of Postgraduate Teaching and Learning, Head of School, or the Postgraduate Advisory Support Service. Serious conduct or safety concerns should use the appropriate specialist or formal route rather than relying only on direct negotiation.

## Working checklist

- Clarify roles, meeting frequency, feedback scope, and turnaround expectations.
- Prepare agendas, documents, and targeted questions.
- Label the maturity of every draft and specify the feedback required.
- Record actions, owners, deadlines, and the next meeting.
- Take responsibility for training, regulations, ethics, and progress.
- Question and disagree professionally when needed.
- Seek independent support before a persistent problem becomes a crisis.
